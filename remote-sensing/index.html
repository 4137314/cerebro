Physics PDF

Systems PDF

  • Remote sensing system model
    Remote sensing acquires information about an object without contact, by measuring the electromagnetic field it emits or reflects; a remote sensing system chains a source, the atmosphere, the surface, a sensor on a platform, a transmission link and a ground segment where images are processed and recognised.

Radiation PDF

  • Electromagnetic spectrum and spectral regions definition
    Radiation is ordered by wavelength ; remote sensing of the Earth uses the visible (0.4–0.7 µm), near and short-wave infrared (reflected sunlight), mid and thermal infrared (emitted heat, 3–14 µm) and microwaves (1 mm–1 m, radar and radiometers), the regions where the atmosphere is transparent.
  • Radiometric quantities definition
    Radiant energy [J], flux [W], exitance and irradiance (flux leaving or reaching a surface per unit area, W m), intensity (flux per solid angle, W sr) and radiance (flux per unit projected area and solid angle, W m sr): radiance is what a sensor measures, and it does not change with distance in a lossless medium.
  • Black body and Planck's law theorem
    A black body absorbs all incident radiation and emits, at temperature , the spectral exitance ; every body above 0 K emits, and real bodies are described relative to this reference.
  • Real bodies: emissivity and Kirchhoff's law theorem
    Incident energy splits into reflected, absorbed and transmitted parts, ; a real body emits with emissivity , and Kirchhoff's law gives : good absorbers are good emitters.
  • Brightness temperature and surface temperature definition
    The brightness temperature is the temperature a black body would need to emit the measured radiance, obtained by inverting Planck's law ; it underestimates the kinetic temperature of a real surface because , so land-surface temperature retrieval corrects for emissivity and atmosphere.
  • Stefan–Boltzmann and Wien laws theorem
    Integrating Planck's law over all wavelengths gives the total exitance ( W m K); setting its derivative to zero gives the peak wavelength with µm K.
  • The Sun and the Earth as sources example
    Modelled as black bodies, the Sun (5778 K) peaks at 0.5 µm and dominates the visible to short-wave infrared, the Earth (300 K) peaks at 9.66 µm in the thermal infrared; the two curves cross near 3–5 µm, so reflective sensing and thermal sensing use different bands.

Atmosphere PDF

  • Atmospheric transmittance and windows definition
    The atmosphere transmits a fraction of the radiation crossing it; water vapour and CO absorb strongly in some bands (e.g. 1.4 and 1.9 µm, 2.7 µm, 6 µm), so sensors are designed in the windows where is high: visible–NIR, SWIR, 3–5 µm, 8–14 µm and microwaves.
  • Atmospheric scattering: Rayleigh and Mie definition
    Besides absorbing, the atmosphere scatters radiation: particles much smaller than give Rayleigh scattering, proportional to (blue light is scattered most); particles comparable to or larger than (aerosol, smoke, cloud droplets) give Mie and non-selective scattering, weakly dependent on .
  • From DN to top-of-atmosphere and surface reflectance method
    Calibration turns DN into at-sensor radiance ; dividing by the solar irradiance on a horizontal surface gives the top-of-atmosphere reflectance ; removing path radiance and transmittance with an atmospheric model gives surface reflectance, the quantity comparable across dates and sensors.

Signatures PDF

  • Lambertian surfaces and the BRDF model
    A Lambertian surface reflects with the same radiance in every direction, so whatever the viewing angle; real surfaces are described by the bidirectional reflectance distribution function , between the Lambertian (diffuse) and specular (mirror) extremes.
  • Spectral signature definition
    The spectral signature of a surface is its reflectance as a function of wavelength, ; different materials have different shapes — vegetation low in the red and high in the NIR, water dark beyond the visible, soil rising slowly — but each class is a random family of curves, so discrimination is statistical.

Acquisition PDF

Orbits PDF

  • Acquisition platforms definition
    Sensors fly on platforms at very different altitudes — UAVs below 1 km, aircraft at 5–15 km, stratospheric platforms at 15–36 km, polar satellites at 500–800 km, the ISS at 400 km, geostationary satellites at 36 000 km — trading resolution and flexibility against coverage, stability and cost.
  • Satellite orbits definition
    An orbit is an ellipse (or circle) with the Earth at a focus, described by its inclination, its ascending and descending nodes, apogee and perigee; Kepler's third law ties the period to the semi-major axis, so altitude fixes the period.
  • Geostationary orbit construction
    A circular equatorial orbit with period equal to one sidereal day (23 h 56 min) keeps a satellite over the same point of the equator; Kepler's law fixes its altitude at 35 786 km, from where it sees about 43% of the Earth continuously — ideal for meteorology and telecommunications, at kilometre resolution.
  • Sun-synchronous polar orbits and revisit time definition
    Most Earth-observation satellites fly near-polar orbits at 600–800 km whose plane precesses 360° a year, so they pass over every latitude at the same local solar time and similar illumination; the revisit time over a site depends on orbit, swath and pointing, and is shortened by constellations.

Satellites PDF

  • Orbits of telecommunication satellites definition
    Telecommunication satellites use geostationary orbits (one satellite covers 43% of the Earth), highly elliptical 64° orbits with 12 h period (three satellites cover high latitudes), MEO at about 10 000 km and LEO constellations at about 1000 km (dozens of satellites, short delays); the choice depends on area, latitude, elevation angle, delay and launcher.
  • Satellite system architecture model
    A satellite system has a space segment (the satellite plus tracking, telemetry and command stations) and a ground segment (receiving, processing, archiving and distribution); the satellite is a payload module (antennas or sensors) carried by a service module (power, attitude and orbit control, thrusters, TT&C), powered by solar arrays and by batteries during eclipses.
  • Satellite data transmission definition
    Satellite links use regulated frequency bands (uplink above downlink, e.g. 6/4 GHz, 14/12 GHz, 30/20 GHz) and digital phase modulations (BPSK, QPSK) protected by forward error correction with convolutional codes; Earth-observation data are downlinked directly, stored on board until a station is visible, or relayed through a telecommunication satellite.

Optical Sensors PDF

  • Passive and active sensors definition
    Passive sensors measure radiation emitted or reflected by the target (cameras, multispectral and hyperspectral scanners, thermal cameras, microwave radiometers); active sensors illuminate the target and measure what comes back (radar, SAR, lidar, ground-penetrating radar); both can produce measurements (1-D signals) or images (2-D).
  • Scanner geometry: IFOV, GIFOV, swath definition
    A detector of size behind optics of focal length sees the angle ; from altitude this is a ground cell , the geometric resolution; the image is built line by line by a rotating mirror (whiskbroom) or by a linear array of detectors (pushbroom) while the platform moves.
  • Detector, quantization and digital numbers definition
    The detector converts the radiance focused by the optics into a current or voltage, which is sampled and quantized with bits into a digital number ; calibration relates DN linearly to radiance, .
  • Geometric, radiometric, spectral and temporal resolution definition
    A sensor integrates radiance over space (geometric resolution, the GIFOV), wavelength (spectral resolution, band width and number), and is limited by noise (radiometric resolution, the smallest measurable radiance difference) and by revisit (temporal resolution); for a given detector they trade against each other because each pixel must collect enough energy.
  • Multispectral and hyperspectral images model
    A multispectral scanner measures a few broad bands, a hyperspectral one hundreds of narrow contiguous bands; either way the image is a cube , and each pixel is a vector of values that samples the spectral signature of its ground cell.
  • Image storage formats: BSQ, BIL, BIP definition
    A cube of lines, columns and bands can be written band after band (BSQ), line after line with all bands of each line (BIL), or pixel after pixel with all bands of each pixel (BIP, also BIS); the choice decides which access is fast.
  • Point spread function and spectral response definition
    Real sensors do not integrate over ideal boxes: spatially the DN is the radiance weighted by the point spread function, which overlaps neighbouring cells and blurs edges; spectrally it is weighted by the relative spectral response of each band, a smooth curve instead of a rectangle.
  • Signal-to-noise ratio and noise-equivalent quantities definition
    Radiometric resolution is limited by noise: the noise-equivalent radiance difference (NEΔL) or temperature difference (NEΔT) is the change that produces a signal equal to the noise; with photon (shot) noise , so longer dwell time, larger optics or wider bands improve it.

Radar PDF

  • Radar principle and radar equation theorem
    A radar transmits a pulse and measures the echo: its delay gives the range , its power depends on the radar cross section of the target, i.e. on how the surface backscatters at that wavelength.
  • Side-looking radar geometry definition
    An imaging radar looks sideways, perpendicular to its track: points are separated in range by the delay of their echoes and in azimuth by the motion of the platform; looking straight down would be ambiguous, because points symmetric about the track return at the same time.
  • Azimuth resolution of a real-aperture radar theorem
    Along the track, two targets are separated only if they are not in the beam at the same time; an antenna of length has beamwidth , so at slant range the azimuth resolution is : it worsens with distance, and from orbit would require antennas kilometres long.
  • Backscattering coefficient definition
    is the radar cross section per unit area, usually in dB; it depends on the radar (wavelength, polarization, incidence angle) and on the surface (roughness relative to , dielectric constant i.e. moisture, geometry and orientation): smooth surfaces mirror energy away and look dark, rough ones and corners look bright.
  • Range resolution theorem
    Two targets are separated in range if their echoes do not overlap: with a pulse of duration , slant-range resolution is and ground-range resolution , finer at far range and independent of the distance from the sensor.
  • Chirp and pulse compression construction
    Instead of a very short, very powerful pulse, radars transmit a long linear-frequency-modulated pulse (chirp) of bandwidth and correlate the echo with a replica (matched filter); the output peak has width , so the range resolution becomes , independent of the pulse duration.
  • Foreshortening, layover and shadow definition
    Because a radar orders points by distance, relief distorts its images: slopes facing the radar appear compressed and bright (foreshortening), very steep ones have their top imaged before their foot (layover), and slopes facing away steeper than the beam receive no energy (shadow).
  • Radar frequency bands definition
    Imaging radars use letter-named microwave bands: Ka (26.5–40 GHz), K, Ku (12.5–18), X (8–12.5, COSMO-SkyMed, TerraSAR-X), C (4–8, Sentinel-1), S (2–4, NISAR), L (1–2, SMAP, NISAR), P (0.3–1 GHz, Biomass); longer wavelengths penetrate further into vegetation, soil and ice.
  • Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) construction
    A small antenna moving along the track illuminates each target during a whole stretch of path ; recording amplitude and phase of all echoes and combining them coherently synthesizes an antenna of length , whose resolution is : finer for smaller antennas and independent of range and wavelength.
  • SAR complex, amplitude and intensity images definition
    The SAR receiver measures in-phase and quadrature components of the echo, so each pixel is a complex number ; from it one derives the amplitude image , the intensity image (proportional to ) and the phase image, which alone looks random but carries range information exploited by interferometry.
  • SAR interferometry (InSAR and DInSAR) method
    Multiplying one SLC image by the conjugate of another, , gives an interferogram whose phase measures path differences to a fraction of the wavelength: across-track baselines turn it into topography, repeat passes from the same orbit into ground displacement with millimetre sensitivity; coherence says how reliable it is.
  • SAR polarimetry definition
    A fully polarimetric radar measures the complex scattering matrix of each cell; decompositions such as Pauli separate surface (single-bounce), double-bounce and volume scattering, which map naturally onto bare soil and water, buildings and tree trunks, and vegetation canopies.

Missions PDF

  • Main Earth-observation missions example
    Geostationary meteorological satellites (Meteosat) trade resolution for continuous coverage; the Landsat series gives the longest optical record at 30 m; SPOT, IKONOS, QuickBird and WorldView push to sub-metre commercial imaging; PRISMA brings hyperspectral data; ERS, ENVISAT, COSMO-SkyMed, TerraSAR-X and Biomass cover SAR from C to X to P band.
  • Copernicus and the Sentinel missions example
    Copernicus is the European Earth-observation programme: Sentinel missions, mostly pairs of satellites for frequent revisit, with free and open data — Sentinel-1 C-band SAR, Sentinel-2 13-band multispectral at 10–60 m, Sentinel-3 ocean and land colour, temperature and altimetry, Sentinel-4/5/5P atmospheric chemistry, Sentinel-6 sea level.

Processing PDF

Images PDF

  • Digital images definition
    A digital image is an matrix of numbers (pixels); in remote sensing each value is the digital number of a ground cell in one band, and a multiband image is a stack of such matrices.
  • Image histogram definition
    The histogram counts, normalised by the number of pixels, how often each DN occurs; it approximates the probability density of the image values and ignores where pixels are; its running sum, the cumulative histogram, approximates the distribution function.
  • Image statistics: moments, mode, median definition
    Mean and variance summarise the level and spread of the DNs; the mode is the histogram peak, the median the value splitting the pixels in half; skewness (third standardised moment) measures asymmetry and kurtosis (fourth) the weight of the tails.
  • Look-up tables and visualization definition
    Screens show 256 gray levels per channel, while sensors deliver up to DNs; a look-up table is an associative memory applied pixel by pixel, which converts (and enhances) the data for display without changing the stored values.

Enhancement PDF

  • Contrast stretching method
    Radiometric enhancement maps the occupied DN range onto the full display range: linear stretching , linear with saturation at percentiles to ignore outliers, piecewise linear, or nonlinear (logarithmic, exponential) to expand dark or bright tones.
  • Histogram equalization method
    Using the cumulative histogram as the transfer function, , spreads the pixels as uniformly as possible over the gray levels: densely populated DN ranges get stretched, sparse ones compressed.
  • Pansharpening method
    Sensors pair a fine panchromatic band with coarser multispectral bands; pansharpening injects the spatial detail of the pan band into the co-registered, upsampled multispectral image — by component substitution (IHS, PCA, Gram–Schmidt) or by adding the pan's high-pass detail — trading some spectral fidelity for resolution.
  • Principal component analysis of multispectral images method
    Adjacent bands are strongly correlated; rotating pixel vectors onto the eigenvectors of the band covariance matrix, , gives uncorrelated components ordered by variance — the first few hold almost all the information, the last ones mostly noise — used for compression, visualisation, feature reduction and change detection.
  • True and false colour composites definition
    Three bands are assigned to the red, green and blue channels of the display; with red, green and blue bands one gets a true-colour image, while any other assignment is a false-colour composite — the classic NIR-red-green to RGB makes vegetation bright red because of its high NIR reflectance.

Corrections PDF

  • Geometric distortions definition
    An image is not a map: Earth rotation during acquisition skews it, Earth curvature and wide fields of view stretch pixels off nadir (panoramic distortion), platform attitude and altitude variations (roll, pitch, yaw) warp lines, and relief displaces elevated points away from nadir.
  • Geometric correction, geocoding and registration method
    Ground control points identified both in the image and on a map (or in a reference image) fit a mapping, typically a low-order polynomial by least squares, from map to image coordinates; the corrected grid is filled by resampling — nearest neighbour, bilinear or cubic convolution — and the residual error at independent points measures the accuracy.
  • Radiometric errors and corrections method
    Measured DNs are distorted by the sensor (detector differences causing stripes, dropped lines, calibration drift) and by the atmosphere and illumination (path radiance, attenuation, sun angle, topography); corrections convert DN to radiance and reflectance, equalise detectors, fill lines and remove the atmospheric offset.

Filtering PDF

  • Moving-window (convolution) filters method
    A linear spatial filter moves a small kernel over the image and replaces each pixel by the weighted sum of its neighbourhood, ; averaging kernels smooth (low-pass), kernels with zero sum enhance differences (high-pass).
  • Edge detection and directional filters method
    Edges are places of large local change, detected by derivative filters: Sobel or Prewitt kernels estimate the gradient components , , whose magnitude marks edges and whose angle gives their orientation; directional filters respond to one orientation only, the Laplacian to all, at zero crossings.
  • Two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform definition
    The 2-D DFT writes an image as a sum of sinusoidal patterns, ; it is periodic, Hermitian-symmetric for real images, its value at the origin is times the mean, low frequencies hold smooth variations and high frequencies edges and details.
  • Filtering in the frequency domain method
    Multiplying the DFT by a transfer function and transforming back filters the image: a low-pass filter keeps smooth variations (blur), a high-pass filter keeps details and edges, a notch filter removes periodic noise; multiplication in frequency is convolution in space.
  • Sampling theorem and aliasing theorem
    A signal sampled every represents frequencies up to the Nyquist limit ; finer detail is not lost but folded back as false low-frequency patterns (aliasing, moiré); sensors limit it with the blur of their PSF, and image resampling must low-pass before decimating.
  • Statistical (rank) filters method
    Nonlinear filters replace each pixel with a statistic of its window — median, mode, minimum, maximum, variance; the median removes impulse noise while preserving edges, the mode cleans classification maps, variance and range measure local texture.
  • Texture features definition
    Texture is the spatial arrangement of tones; it is measured by first-order statistics in a window (variance, range, entropy) or by second-order statistics from the grey-level co-occurrence matrix — how often level sits next to level at a given offset — from which contrast, homogeneity, entropy and energy are derived.
  • The convolution theorem theorem
    Convolving an image with a kernel equals multiplying their Fourier transforms: ; so every moving-window filter has a transfer function telling which spatial frequencies it passes, and large kernels are cheaper through the FFT ( instead of ).

Sar Processing PDF

  • Statistics of homogeneous areas in optical images model
    In images from passive sensors, the pixels of a physically homogeneous area still vary, because of detector thermal noise and natural variability of the spectral signature; many small independent effects add up, so the DNs of a class are well modelled by a Gaussian, multivariate across bands.
  • Speckle and its statistics theorem
    A SAR resolution cell contains many elementary scatterers whose echoes add with random phases; for many independent scatterers with uniform phases, real and imaginary parts are zero-mean Gaussian with variance , so the amplitude is Rayleigh distributed and the intensity negative exponential with mean and standard deviation equal to its mean: multiplicative noise.
  • Speckle reduction method
    Speckle is reduced by multilooking (averaging independent looks, at the cost of resolution) or by adaptive filters such as Lee, Kuan and Frost, which in homogeneous areas average strongly and near edges and point targets keep the original value, by comparing the local coefficient of variation with the speckle's.

Segmentation PDF

  • Image segmentation definition
    Segmentation partitions the image into connected regions that are homogeneous by some criterion and labels each region; the criterion depends on the data (for optical images closeness of means, for SAR closeness of mean intensities given multiplicative speckle), and the main families are histogram thresholding, contour-based, split-and-merge and region growing.
  • Histogram thresholding method
    When the histogram is bimodal, a threshold between the modes splits the image into two classes; Otsu's method chooses maximising the between-class variance , and with Gaussian models the Bayes threshold is where the weighted class densities cross.
  • Mathematical morphology method
    With a small structuring element , erosion shrinks objects (a pixel stays only if fits inside), dilation grows them, opening (erosion then dilation) removes objects and protrusions smaller than , closing (dilation then erosion) fills gaps and holes smaller than : the standard clean-up of binary masks and a source of shape features.
  • Region growing method
    Starting from seed pixels or small atomic regions, each region repeatedly absorbs adjacent pixels that satisfy the homogeneity criterion with respect to it (e.g. difference from the region mean below a tolerance), until no region can grow; seeds come from user clicks, local minima of variance or a regular grid.
  • Split and merge segmentation method
    Split: starting from the whole image, recursively divide every square block that fails the homogeneity test into four, building a quadtree whose leaves are homogeneous "atomic" regions; merge: join adjacent regions whose union is still homogeneous, removing the artificial square boundaries.

Recognition PDF

Features PDF

  • Recognition systems model
    Recognition extracts information from the data — thematic maps, change maps, bio/geo-physical parameters, risk maps, 3-D models — either by photointerpretation or automatically; an automatic classifier chains pre-processing, feature extraction, a classifier trained on labelled samples (supervised) or not (unsupervised), and post-processing, and is designed in a cycle of feature selection, model selection, training and validation.
  • Feature extraction method
    Feature extraction computes, for each pixel (pixel-based) or each segmented region (region-based), a vector of parameters useful to discriminate the classes — original bands, spectral indices such as NDVI, texture measures, region shape — normalised to a common range before classification.
  • Feature space and spectral classes definition
    Each pixel is a point of the feature (multispectral) space; pixels of a land-cover type form clusters whose shape and spread depend on features, cover type, noise and topography; information classes (what the user wants) may consist of several spectral classes (what the data separate).
  • Linear spectral unmixing method
    A pixel larger than the objects it contains is a mixture: the linear mixing model writes its spectrum as , a combination of the endmember spectra weighted by abundances , ; solving the constrained least-squares problem gives sub-pixel fractions instead of a single label.
  • NDVI and normalised difference indices definition
    exploits the contrast between red absorption by chlorophyll and high NIR reflectance of leaves: dense green vegetation gives 0.6–0.9, soil 0.1–0.2, water negative; the normalisation cancels much of the illumination variation.
  • Photointerpretation method
    A trained analyst classifies the scene visually on one or more false-colour composites, using tone and colour, size, shape, texture, pattern, shadow and context; it exploits knowledge that is hard to formalise, but is slow, qualitative and subjective, and today mainly produces training and validation data for automatic classifiers.
  • Scattergram definition
    A scattergram plots, for two bands, the number of pixels at each pair of values (a two-dimensional histogram); it shows clusters, their correlation between bands and the classes' separability, and is the basic exploratory view of a multispectral image.

Classification PDF

  • Accuracy assessment and the confusion matrix method
    A classified map is evaluated on independent test samples: the confusion matrix counts test pixels of reference class labelled as class ; overall accuracy is the diagonal fraction, producer's and user's accuracies measure omission and commission per class, and Cohen's kappa discounts agreement expected by chance.
  • Discriminant functions and decision regions definition
    A classifier for classes is a set of discriminant functions : the pixel goes to the class with the largest value; the decision boundary between and is , a hyperplane when the are linear.
  • Bayes decision rule theorem
    Assigning each pixel to the class with the largest posterior probability minimises the probability of error; it needs the class-conditional densities and the prior probabilities, and gives the discriminants .
  • Gaussian maximum-likelihood classifier construction
    Modelling each class as a multivariate Gaussian with mean and covariance estimated from training pixels, the Bayes discriminant becomes : quadratic boundaries that account for spread and correlation of each class.
  • Hughes phenomenon (curse of dimensionality) theorem
    With a fixed number of training samples, classification accuracy first grows as features are added and then decreases, because the number of parameters to estimate (a Gaussian classifier needs per class) grows faster than the information; hyperspectral data hit this peak quickly, hence feature selection, extraction or classifiers with few parameters.
  • Minimum-distance classifier method
    Each class is represented by the mean of its training pixels and every pixel goes to the nearest mean; with Euclidean distance the discriminants are linear and the boundaries are perpendicular bisectors, with Mahalanobis distance the class covariances are taken into account.
  • k-means clustering method
    Unsupervised classification groups pixels without labels: k-means alternates assigning each pixel to the nearest of centroids and moving each centroid to the mean of its pixels, monotonically decreasing the within-cluster sum of squares; the analyst then labels the spectral clusters as information classes.
  • Perceptron algorithm method
    The perceptron learns a linear discriminant (with augmented by a 1) from labelled samples: whenever a sample is misclassified, ; if the classes are linearly separable the algorithm converges in a finite number of steps.
  • Support vector machines method
    Among all separating hyperplanes an SVM picks the one with maximum margin , solving subject to ; the solution depends only on the support vectors, and kernels give non-linear boundaries — a classifier robust with few training samples and many features, widely used for multispectral and hyperspectral data.
  • Training, validation and test samples method
    Labelled samples are split into a training set (fit parameters), a validation set (choose hyperparameters and features, stop training) and a test set (estimate accuracy once, at the end); k-fold cross-validation reuses small sets, and in remote sensing the split must be spatially independent — whole fields or blocks, not random pixels — or accuracy is overestimated.

Neural Networks PDF

  • Artificial neuron and machine learning model
    Inspired by biological neurons, an artificial neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias and passes it through an activation function, : a hard limiter gives the perceptron, sigmoids and ReLU give differentiable units that can be trained by gradient descent in networks.
  • Multilayer perceptron construction
    An MLP stacks layers of neurons with one-way connections: an input layer with one neuron per feature, hidden layers with non-linear activations, and an output layer with one neuron per class; with enough hidden units it can approximate any decision boundary, and it is trained by backpropagation.
  • Backpropagation method
    An MLP is trained by gradient descent on an error (squared error or cross-entropy) between outputs and target labels; the chain rule lets the gradient be computed layer by layer backwards, each neuron's error being its activation derivative times the weighted errors of the neurons it feeds, and every weight moves by .
  • Convolutional neural networks and deep learning construction
    Deep learning stacks many layers that learn features directly from pixels; a CNN uses convolution layers (small learned kernels shared over the image), non-linearities and pooling to build increasingly abstract spatial–spectral features, and is used for scene classification, semantic segmentation (U-Net) and object detection in remote sensing.

Applications PDF

  • Applications of remote sensing example
    Optical and radar remote sensing with automatic image analysis serve environmental monitoring, risk analysis and civil protection, climate change, natural resources, agriculture and food security, forestry, urban areas, the cryosphere, meteorology and planetary exploration; each pairs specific sensors with specific processing.
  • Change detection method
    Comparing co-registered, radiometrically consistent images of the same area at two dates reveals changes: differencing (optical) or log-ratio (SAR) images thresholded into change/no-change, change vector analysis on multispectral vectors (magnitude and direction), or post-classification comparison of two thematic maps.
  • Unsupervised change detection with EM and Bayes method
    The difference (or log-ratio) image is modelled as a mixture of two classes, unchanged and changed, ; the expectation–maximization algorithm estimates the priors and the class densities without any label, and the Bayes rule then gives the minimum-error threshold — optionally refined with spatial context.
