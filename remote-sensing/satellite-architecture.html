Satellite system architecture

A satellite system has a space segment (the satellite plus tracking, telemetry and command stations) and a ground segment (receiving, processing, archiving and distribution); the satellite is a payload module (antennas or sensors) carried by a service module (power, attitude and orbit control, thrusters, TT&C), powered by solar arrays and by batteries during eclipses.

Model

  • Space segment: satellite(s) and TT&C stations that control and monitor them. Links: uplink (ground to satellite, usually the higher frequency), downlink, interlinks between satellites (radio or optical).
  • Ground segment: for Earth observation, a flight operations segment (configuration, on-board software, orbit control) and a data management segment (processing, storage, dissemination to users).
  • Payload module: transponders and antennas (telecommunications) or remote sensing instruments and their management (Earth observation).
  • Service module (bus): power generation, attitude and orbit control, thrusters, TT&C, and for Earth observation the data transmission system.
  • Power: primary subsystem = solar arrays; secondary = electrochemical batteries, charged in sunlight, used in the shadow cone. In eclipse an Earth-observation satellite often cannot both acquire and transmit: sensors are switched off, or data are recorded on board and downlinked later.
  • Launch: a launch vehicle (Vega, Ariane, Falcon 9) places the satellite in orbit in minutes (about 24 min for Sentinel-2 on Vega) at very high cost; reusable stages reduce it.

Worked example

A satellite at 700 km ( km) is in the Earth's shadow over an arc of about of the orbit in the worst season, i.e. up to 35% of the 99-minute period (≈ 35 min) for orbits whose plane contains the Sun direction; dawn–dusk sun-synchronous orbits avoid eclipses almost entirely, which is why some radar missions use them.

Intuition

The instrument is the reason for the mission; everything else on board exists to keep it pointed, powered, warm and talking to the ground.

Pitfall

Power and downlink capacity, not the sensor, often limit how much a satellite can image per orbit.
