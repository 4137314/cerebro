Orbits of telecommunication satellites

Telecommunication satellites use geostationary orbits (one satellite covers 43% of the Earth), highly elliptical 64° orbits with 12 h period (three satellites cover high latitudes), MEO at about 10 000 km and LEO constellations at about 1000 km (dozens of satellites, short delays); the choice depends on area, latitude, elevation angle, delay and launcher.

Definition

orbit incl. altitude period example
highly elliptical ≈64° 550 – 40 000 km ≈12 h Molniya, 3 satellites
LEO ≈90° ≈1000 km ≈1 h 30 min Iridium, Globalstar
MEO ≈50° ≈10 000 km ≈6 h a dozen satellites
GEO 0° 35 786 km 24 h broadcast

The 64° inclination cancels the rotation of the line of apsides, keeping the apogee over the same hemisphere, where the satellite lingers.

Why satellites for telecommunications: wide coverage from one position, natural broadcasting, large bandwidth, reconfigurability.

Criteria

  • extent and latitude of the area to cover;
  • elevation angle: mobile and GPS users need satellites high in the sky to avoid trees and buildings;
  • propagation delay and visibility duration;
  • interference with other satellites;
  • launcher capacity.

Worked example

One-way propagation delay at the sub-satellite point: GEO ms, so a ground–satellite–ground hop takes 239 ms and a round-trip conversation about 0.5 s; MEO (10 000 km) 33 ms per leg; LEO (1000 km) 3.3 ms. Free-space path loss grows as : GEO links lose about dB more than LEO links at 1000 km.

Intuition

GEO is one big fixed antenna in the sky; LEO is a swarm of small fast ones handing users over to each other.

Pitfall

LEO constellations need many satellites and inter-satellite links or many ground stations, since each satellite is visible from a point for only a few minutes.
