Satellite data transmission

Satellite links use regulated frequency bands (uplink above downlink, e.g. 6/4 GHz, 14/12 GHz, 30/20 GHz) and digital phase modulations (BPSK, QPSK) protected by forward error correction with convolutional codes; Earth-observation data are downlinked directly, stored on board until a station is visible, or relayed through a telecommunication satellite.

Definition

  • Bands: commercial 5925–6425 MHz up / 3700–4200 MHz down (C), 14–14.5 / 11.7–12.2 GHz (Ku), 27.5–30.5 / 17.7–20.2 GHz (Ka); military 7.9–8.4 / 7.25–7.75 GHz (X). Earth-observation downlinks typically in X band (8 GHz), increasingly Ka.
  • Modulation: BPSK carries 1 bit per symbol with phases ; QPSK 2 bits per symbol with , double rate in the same bandwidth at the same bit error rate per bit.
  • Channel coding: forward error correction (convolutional codes, Viterbi decoding; today also LDPC and Reed–Solomon) lets the receiver correct errors without retransmission, essential with long delays and short passes.
  • Paths: direct satellite–ground link during a pass; store and forward with on-board memory; relay to a geostationary data-relay satellite (interlink), which forwards to the ground almost in real time.

Derivation

Data volume per orbit: a pushbroom with pixels across track, bands, bits, ground speed km/s and pixel produces bit/s. For Sentinel-2-like values ( at 10 m over 290 km, 4 bands of 10 m, 12 bits) Gbit/s before compression: why on-board compression and fast downlinks are needed.

Intuition

The satellite sees far more than it can tell: transmission, not sensing, is the bottleneck.

Pitfall

Higher frequencies give more bandwidth but suffer more rain attenuation (Ka band), so link budgets need margins tied to the station's climate.
