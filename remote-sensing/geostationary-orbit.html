Geostationary orbit

A circular equatorial orbit with period equal to one sidereal day (23 h 56 min) keeps a satellite over the same point of the equator; Kepler's law fixes its altitude at 35 786 km, from where it sees about 43% of the Earth continuously — ideal for meteorology and telecommunications, at kilometre resolution.

Derivation

s (sidereal day), ms:

The angular velocity equals the Earth's and the orbital plane is the equatorial one (), so the satellite looks fixed in the sky.

Properties

  • continuous observation of one disc (images every 5–15 minutes: cloud motion, storms, volcanic plumes);
  • coverage up to about latitude, poor near the poles;
  • resolution of 1–3 km at nadir for passive sensors, since the distance is about 50 times that of LEO;
  • propagation delay s for a ground–satellite–ground hop.

Intuition

A camera nailed to the sky over the equator: always the same view, never close.

Pitfall

"Geosynchronous" (period of one day) is not enough: with non-zero inclination the satellite traces a figure eight in the sky; only the equatorial circular case is geostationary.
