Logic begins when a formula stops being notation for an argument and
becomes an object a theorem can quantify over. From that point the
subject has a fixed shape: define what it means for a formula to be
/true/ in a structure, define separately what it means to be
/derivable/, and then compare the two. Everything below is that
comparison, or a consequence of it.

Part of [[id:1ba699a0-80ee-42e3-ae2b-bfbbc0a2c6a8][Foundations]].

* Propositional logic

[[id:b3043ad1-2aac-4361-8f17-605013e7a12a][Propositional logic: formulas, assignments and compactness]] gives the
semantics -- two inductive definitions, one building formulas and one
evaluating them -- and the theorem that makes the subject infinite
rather than finitary: satisfiability is decided by finite subsets.

[[id:36cd1905-a97d-4a57-8df6-c624798c4eb9][A proof system: soundness and the deduction theorem]] gives the syntax,
as a finite combinatorial object, and the easy half of the comparison.
[[id:77484172-ce88-4a74-8fcf-b70251b5034f][Completeness of propositional logic]] gives the hard half, by building a
model out of the syntax itself -- after which compactness reappears as a
corollary, for the reason that a derivation is finite.

* First-order logic

[[id:49031e90-dddd-4b31-b30c-bff152d81526][First-order languages, structures and satisfaction]] makes "\(G\)
satisfies the group axioms" a mathematical statement, and with it
statements about /all/ structures satisfying a theory.
[[id:97912891-a007-42a1-8b9f-410b0c47e802][Ultraproducts and the theorem of Los]] then prove compactness at this
level without a proof system at all, by assembling a model out of a
family of models along an [[id:e3c053e0-2462-4692-8b0d-ee81002ec63c][ultrafilter]] -- the only property used being
that "large" is closed under complement, which is exactly what negation
needs.

[[id:4400af63-8a49-4b93-a45a-6dc1505f616c][Elementary substructures and the Lowenheim-Skolem theorems]] say how
little a first-order theory controls: every infinite structure has a
countable elementary substructure and arbitrarily large elementary
extensions, so no such theory pins down the size of its models.

* The limits

[[id:eb14cc58-7808-4c04-a956-042143f30664][Computability, the halting problem and Rice's theorem]] is the other kind
of limitative result: not "the theory has too many models" but "no
procedure decides this". The two undecidability proofs there use only
two properties of any reasonable model of computation -- a program can
simulate a program, and a parameter can be frozen -- which is why the
conclusions do not depend on the machine chosen.

* What is missing

Godel's completeness theorem for first-order logic, whose consequence
was obtained here by [[id:97912891-a007-42a1-8b9f-410b0c47e802][ultraproducts]] instead; the incompleteness theorems,
and with them arithmetic as an object of study; quantifier elimination
and the model theory of specific theories; intuitionistic and modal
logic; and the connection between [[id:eb14cc58-7808-4c04-a956-042143f30664][computability]] and definability.
