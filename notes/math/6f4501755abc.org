#+TITLE: Local compactness and the one-point compactification
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:topology:general_topology:
#+MSC: 54D45
#+HASH: 6f4501755abc

Compactness is the hypothesis that makes theorems work and \(\R^{n}\)
does not have it. The cheapest repair is to add a single point and
declare the neighbourhoods of it to be the complements of compact sets
-- which is exactly the convention "\(x_{n}\to\infty\) means \(x_{n}\)
eventually leaves every compact set", made into a topology.

#+DEPENDS: def-compact, def-hausdorff, prop-compact-subset-of-a-hausdorff-space-is-closed
#+NAME: def-locally-compact
#+begin_definition
A Hausdorff space \(X\) is /locally compact/ if every point has a
compact neighbourhood.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-locally-compact, prop-compact-subset-of-a-hausdorff-space-is-closed, prop-closed-subspace-of-a-compact-space
#+NAME: prop-locally-compact-neighbourhood-basis
#+begin_proposition
In a locally compact Hausdorff space, every point has a neighbourhood
basis of compact sets: for every open \(U \ni x\) there is an open
\(V\) with \(x \in V \subseteq \overline{V}\subseteq U\) and
\(\overline{V}\) compact.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \(K\) be a compact neighbourhood of \(x\) and \(U\) open with
\(x \in U\). The set \(C = K \setminus U\) is compact -- closed in the
compact \(K\), being the intersection of \(K\) with the closed
\(X\setminus U\) -- and does not contain \(x\). By the separation of a
point from a compact set in a Hausdorff space there are disjoint open
\(V_{0}\ni x\) and \(W \supseteq C\). Put \(V = V_{0}\cap K^{\circ}\),
an open neighbourhood of \(x\). Its closure is contained in the compact
\(K\), hence compact, and it misses \(W \supseteq C\), so
\(\overline{V}\subseteq K \setminus C \subseteq U\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-locally-compact, def-topological-space, def-compact
#+NAME: thm-one-point-compactification
#+begin_theorem
Let \(X\) be locally compact Hausdorff and put
\(X^{+} = X \cup \set{\infty}\), declaring open the open subsets of
\(X\) together with the sets \(\set{\infty}\cup(X\setminus K)\) for
\(K \subseteq X\) compact. Then \(X^{+}\) is a compact Hausdorff space
containing \(X\) as an open dense subspace, unless \(X\) is already
compact.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Write the sets of the second kind as \(X^{+}\setminus K\). The family is
a topology, and it is worth checking because both kinds occur. It
contains \(\emptyset\) and \(X^{+} = X^{+}\setminus\emptyset\).

Unions: a union of sets of the first kind is open in \(X\). A union
involving second-kind sets is
\(\bigl(\bigcup_{i}U_{i}\bigr)\cup\bigl(X^{+}\setminus\bigcap_{j}K_{j}\bigr) = X^{+}\setminus\Bigl(\bigcap_{j}K_{j}\setminus\bigcup_{i}U_{i}\Bigr)\);
here \(\bigcap_{j}K_{j}\) is compact, being closed in any one of the
\(K_{j}\) -- compact subsets of a Hausdorff space are closed -- and
removing the open \(\bigcup_{i}U_{i}\) leaves a closed subset of it,
again compact.

Intersections of two members: two of the first kind give an open subset
of \(X\); \(U \cap (X^{+}\setminus K) = U \cap (X\setminus K)\) is open
in \(X\) because \(K\) is closed; and
\((X^{+}\setminus K_{1})\cap(X^{+}\setminus K_{2}) = X^{+}\setminus(K_{1}\cup K_{2})\),
a union of two compact sets being compact.

Compactness: let \(\mathcal{U}\) cover \(X^{+}\). Some member contains
\(\infty\), so it is \(\set{\infty}\cup(X\setminus K)\) with \(K\)
compact; finitely many members of \(\mathcal{U}\) cover \(K\), and
together with that one they cover \(X^{+}\).

Hausdorff: two points of \(X\) are separated inside \(X\), which is
open. For \(x \in X\) and \(\infty\), local compactness gives a compact
neighbourhood \(K\) of \(x\); then \(K^{\circ}\) and
\(\set{\infty}\cup(X\setminus K)\) are disjoint open sets. \(X\) is open
in \(X^{+}\) by construction, and dense unless \(\set{\infty}\) is
itself open, which happens exactly when \(X\) is compact.
#+end_proof

#+NAME: cor-sphere-is-a-compactification-of-the-plane
#+begin_corollary
\(\R^{n}\) is locally compact, and \((\R^{n})^{+}\) is homeomorphic to
the sphere \(S^{n}\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
A closed ball is a compact neighbourhood of each point, by Heine-Borel,
so \(\R^{n}\) is locally compact Hausdorff. Stereographic projection
from the north pole \(N\) is a homeomorphism \(S^{n}\setminus\set{N}\to\R^{n}\);
extend it to \(S^{n}\to(\R^{n})^{+}\) by sending \(N\) to \(\infty\).
This is a bijection, and it is continuous: at points other than \(N\)
this is the projection, and a neighbourhood
\(\set{\infty}\cup(\R^{n}\setminus K)\) of \(\infty\) pulls back to the
complement of the compact preimage of \(K\), a neighbourhood of \(N\).
A continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a
homeomorphism.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The construction explains the two conventions that look like abuses of
notation. "\(1/x \to \infty\) as \(x \to 0\)" is continuity of a map
into \(\R^{+}\), and the Riemann sphere of complex analysis is
\(\C^{+}\), on which a rational function is genuinely continuous
everywhere -- the poles are not exceptions but ordinary points mapping
to \(\infty\).
#+end_remark
