#+TITLE: The derivative
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26A24
#+HASH: 1ff8e27e7638

The derivative is a limit, so everything proved about limits applies to
it unchanged. The one implication worth isolating at once is that
differentiability is strictly stronger than continuity.

#+DEPENDS: def-function-limit
#+NAME: def-derivative
#+begin_definition
Let \(I \subseteq \R\) be an open interval, \(f \colon I \to \R\) and
\(c \in I\). Then \(f\) is /differentiable/ at \(c\) if
\[ f'(c) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(c + h) - f(c)}{h} \]
exists in \(\R\); the number \(f'(c)\) is the /derivative/ of \(f\) at
\(c\). If this holds at every point of \(I\), \(f\) is differentiable on
\(I\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-derivative, def-continuity-at-point
#+NAME: prop-differentiable-implies-continuous
#+begin_proposition
If \(f\) is differentiable at \(c\), then \(f\) is continuous at \(c\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
For \(h \ne 0\) write
\(f(c + h) - f(c) = h \cdot \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h}\). As \(h \to 0\)
the second factor tends to \(f'(c)\) and the first to \(0\), so the
product tends to \(0\), which is exactly \(f(c + h) \to f(c)\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The converse fails at a single point already: \(\abs{x}\) is continuous
at \(0\) and has one-sided difference quotients \(1\) and \(-1\) there,
so the limit defining the derivative does not exist.
#+end_remark
