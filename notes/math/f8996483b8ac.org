#+TITLE: The Fourier transform, inversion and Plancherel
#+DATE: [2026-08-11 Tue]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:harmonic_analysis:
#+MSC: 42A38
#+HASH: f8996483b8ac

On a bounded interval the frequencies are discrete and the expansion is
a series; on the line they form a continuum and the sum becomes an
integral. What survives is what matters for [[id:b8b253f2-97fc-4461-8ee4-281c7a8202d2][mathematical physics]]:
differentiation becomes multiplication, convolution becomes
multiplication, and the transform preserves the \(L^{2}\) norm -- so a
differential equation becomes an algebraic one.

#+DEPENDS: def-measure, thm-dominated-convergence, def-expectation
#+NAME: def-fourier-transform
#+begin_definition
For \(f\) integrable on \(\R\), the /Fourier transform/ is
\[ \hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{\R} f(x) e^{-i\xi x}\dd x, \qquad \xi \in \R, \]
and the /convolution/ of integrable \(f, g\) is
\((f*g)(x) = \int_{\R}f(x-y)g(y)\dd y\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-fourier-transform, thm-dominated-convergence, thm-fubini
#+NAME: prop-elementary-properties-of-the-transform
#+begin_proposition
For integrable \(f\): \(\hat{f}\) is bounded by
\(\int\abs{f}\) and uniformly continuous; \(\widehat{f*g} = \hat{f}\hat{g}\);
if \(f\) is \(C^{1}\) with \(f, f'\) integrable then
\(\widehat{f'}(\xi) = i\xi\hat{f}(\xi)\); and if \(xf(x)\) is integrable
then \(\hat{f}\) is differentiable with
\(\hat{f}'(\xi) = \widehat{-ixf}(\xi)\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
The bound is the triangle inequality, the modulus of the exponential
being \(1\). Uniform continuity: \(\abs{\hat{f}(\xi+h)-\hat{f}(\xi)} \le \int\abs{f(x)}\abs{e^{-ihx}-1}\dd x\),
independent of \(\xi\), and the integrand is dominated by
\(2\abs{f}\) and tends to \(0\) pointwise, so dominated convergence
applies.

For the convolution, \(\abs{f(x-y)g(y)}\) is integrable on the plane by
Tonelli, so Fubini permits exchanging the order in
\(\int\!\!\int f(x-y)g(y)e^{-i\xi x}\dd y\dd x\); substituting
\(x = u+y\) factorises the exponential and gives the product.

Integrating by parts on \([-R,R]\) and letting \(R\to\infty\) -- the
boundary terms tend to \(0\) because \(f\) is integrable with integrable
derivative, hence has limit \(0\) at \(\pm\infty\) -- gives the rule for
\(f'\). Differentiation under the integral sign, justified by the
dominating function \(\abs{xf(x)}\), gives the last rule.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-fourier-transform, prop-elementary-properties-of-the-transform
#+NAME: prop-the-gaussian-is-its-own-transform
#+begin_proposition
For \(g(x) = e^{-x^{2}/2}\) one has \(\hat{g}(\xi) = \sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\xi^{2}/2}\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Both rules of the previous proposition apply to \(g\), which decays
faster than any polynomial. Differentiating under the integral and then
integrating by parts,
\(\hat{g}'(\xi) = \int (-ix)e^{-x^{2}/2}e^{-i\xi x}\dd x = i\int \frac{\dd}{\dd x}\bigl(e^{-x^{2}/2}\bigr)e^{-i\xi x}\dd x = -\xi\hat{g}(\xi)\).
So \((\hat{g}(\xi)e^{\xi^{2}/2})' = 0\), whence
\(\hat{g}(\xi) = \hat{g}(0)e^{-\xi^{2}/2}\), and
\(\hat{g}(0) = \int e^{-x^{2}/2}\dd x = \sqrt{2\pi}\) is the Gaussian
integral, computed by squaring it and passing to polar coordinates.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-the-gaussian-is-its-own-transform, thm-fubini, thm-dominated-convergence
#+NAME: thm-fourier-inversion
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f\) be integrable and continuous with \(\hat{f}\) integrable.
Then
\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\R}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{i\xi x}\dd\xi \qquad \text{for every } x. \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
For \(\eps>0\) consider
\(I_{\eps}(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int \hat{f}(\xi)e^{-\eps^{2}\xi^{2}/2}e^{i\xi x}\dd\xi\).
Substituting the definition of \(\hat{f}\) and applying Fubini --
legitimate because \(\abs{f(y)}e^{-\eps^{2}\xi^{2}/2}\) is integrable on
the plane -- gives
\(I_{\eps}(x) = \int f(y)\,\Phi_{\eps}(x-y)\dd y\), where
\(\Phi_{\eps}(u) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{-\eps^{2}\xi^{2}/2}e^{i\xi u}\dd\xi\).
By the Gaussian computation and a change of scale,
\(\Phi_{\eps}(u) = \frac{1}{\eps\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^{2}/(2\eps^{2})}\),
which is non-negative with total integral \(1\) and concentrates at
\(0\).

Hence \(I_{\eps}(x) \to f(x)\) as \(\eps\to0\), by the same splitting
argument as for Fejer's kernel, using continuity of \(f\) at \(x\) and
integrability to control the tail. On the other hand \(I_{\eps}(x)\)
tends to the right-hand side of the statement by dominated convergence,
the integrand being dominated by \(\abs{\hat{f}}\). The two limits agree.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-fourier-inversion, def-l2, thm-riesz-fischer, thm-parseval-for-fourier-series
#+NAME: thm-plancherel
#+begin_theorem
For \(f\) integrable and square-integrable with \(\hat{f}\) integrable,
\[ \int_{\R}\abs{f(x)}^{2}\dd x = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\R}\abs{\hat{f}(\xi)}^{2}\dd\xi. \]
Consequently the transform extends to a bijective linear map of
\(L^{2}(\R)\) onto itself preserving the norm up to the factor
\(2\pi\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Put \(g(x) = \overline{f(-x)}\), so that
\(\hat{g} = \overline{\hat{f}}\) and \((f*g)(0) = \int\abs{f}^{2}\). The
convolution \(f*g\) is continuous and its transform
\(\hat{f}\hat{g} = \abs{\hat{f}}^{2}\) is integrable, so the inversion
formula applies at \(0\):
\((f*g)(0) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int\abs{\hat{f}(\xi)}^{2}\dd\xi\), which is
the identity.

The set of such \(f\) is dense in \(L^{2}\), and an isometry defined on
a dense subspace of a complete space extends uniquely to the whole; the
extension is onto because the inverse transform extends likewise and the
two compositions are the identity on the dense subspace.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The three rules -- derivative to multiplication, convolution to product,
norm preserved -- are exactly what makes the transform the standard tool
for linear constant-coefficient equations: applying it turns the heat
and wave equations into ordinary differential equations in \(\xi\),
solvable by inspection, and Plancherel guarantees that the answer
obtained by inverting has the same energy as the data.
#+end_remark
