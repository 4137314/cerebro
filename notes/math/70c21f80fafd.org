#+TITLE: Zeros, the identity theorem and the maximum principle
#+DATE: [2026-08-11 Tue]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:complex_analysis:
#+MSC: 30C80, 30B10
#+HASH: 70c21f80fafd

Analyticity makes a holomorphic function rigid in two ways that have no
real analogue: its values on any set with an accumulation point
determine it everywhere, and its modulus can have no interior maximum.
Both are the local power series expansion, read once for the zeros and
once for the boundary.

#+DEPENDS: thm-holomorphic-implies-analytic, def-accumulation-point
#+NAME: thm-zeros-are-isolated
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f\) be holomorphic on a connected open \(U\), not identically
zero, and \(f(z_{0}) = 0\). Then there are a unique \(m \ge 1\), the
/order/ of the zero, and a holomorphic \(g\) with \(g(z_{0})\ne0\) and
\(f(z) = (z-z_{0})^{m}g(z)\) near \(z_{0}\). In particular the zeros of
\(f\) are isolated.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Expand \(f(z) = \sum_{n}a_{n}(z-z_{0})^{n}\) near \(z_{0}\). Not all
\(a_{n}\) vanish: the set where every derivative of \(f\) vanishes is
open -- there \(f\) is identically zero on a disc -- and closed, being
an intersection of zero sets of continuous functions, so by connectedness
it is empty or all of \(U\), and the latter is excluded.

Let \(m\) be least with \(a_{m}\ne0\), which is at least \(1\) since
\(a_{0} = f(z_{0}) = 0\). Then
\(f(z) = (z-z_{0})^{m}\sum_{k\ge0}a_{m+k}(z-z_{0})^{k}\), and the second
factor is a convergent power series, hence holomorphic, with value
\(a_{m}\ne0\) at \(z_{0}\). By continuity it is non-zero on a
neighbourhood, on which \(z_{0}\) is the only zero of \(f\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-zeros-are-isolated, def-accumulation-point, prop-connected-components
#+NAME: thm-identity
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f, g\) be holomorphic on a connected open \(U\) and suppose they
agree on a set having an accumulation point in \(U\). Then \(f = g\) on
\(U\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Apply the previous theorem to \(h = f-g\). If \(h\) is not identically
zero its zeros are isolated, contradicting the existence of an
accumulation point of zeros inside \(U\). Hence \(h \equiv 0\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-cauchy-integral-formula, def-contour-integral
#+NAME: prop-mean-value-property
#+begin_proposition
If \(f\) is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of \(\overline{B}(z_{0},r)\)
then
\(f(z_{0}) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(z_{0}+re^{i\theta})\dd\theta\):
the value at the centre is the average over the circle.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Put \(z = z_{0}\) in the Cauchy integral formula and parametrise the
circle by \(w = z_{0}+re^{i\theta}\), so \(\dd w = ire^{i\theta}\dd\theta\)
and \(w - z_{0} = re^{i\theta}\); the factors cancel, leaving
\(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(z_{0}+re^{i\theta})\,i\dd\theta\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-mean-value-property, thm-identity, cor-extreme-value-on-a-compact-space
#+NAME: thm-maximum-modulus
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f\) be holomorphic on a connected open \(U\). If \(\abs{f}\)
attains a local maximum at a point of \(U\), then \(f\) is constant.
Consequently, if \(U\) is bounded and \(f\) extends continuously to
\(\overline{U}\), then \(\abs{f}\) attains its maximum on the boundary.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Suppose \(\abs{f}\le\abs{f(z_{0})}\) on \(B(z_{0},r)\). By the mean
value property, for \(\rho<r\),
\(\abs{f(z_{0})} \le \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\abs{f(z_{0}+\rho e^{i\theta})}\dd\theta \le \abs{f(z_{0})}\).
So the continuous non-negative function
\(\theta\mapsto\abs{f(z_{0})}-\abs{f(z_{0}+\rho e^{i\theta})}\) has zero
integral and therefore vanishes identically: \(\abs{f}\) is constant on
every such circle, hence on the disc.

A holomorphic function of constant modulus on a disc is constant: if the
constant is \(0\) this is clear; otherwise \(f\overline{f}\) is
constant, so \(\overline{f} = c/f\) is holomorphic, and a function whose
conjugate is also holomorphic has vanishing derivative by the
Cauchy-Riemann equations, hence is constant. By the identity theorem
\(f\) is constant on all of \(U\).

For the last claim, \(\abs{f}\) is continuous on the compact
\(\overline{U}\), so it attains a maximum; if the maximum were at an
interior point, \(f\) would be constant and the maximum would also be
attained on the boundary.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The identity theorem is what makes analytic continuation well posed:
there is at most one holomorphic extension of a function to a larger
connected set, so "the" continuation of a series beyond its disc of
convergence is a legitimate phrase. It also forbids bump functions --
a holomorphic function vanishing on an open subset of a connected domain
vanishes identically -- which is why the constructions of real analysis
that rely on cutting off have no complex analogue.
#+end_remark
