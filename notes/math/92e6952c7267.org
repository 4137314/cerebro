#+TITLE: The central limit theorem
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:probability:probability_theory:
#+MSC: 60F05
#+HASH: 92e6952c7267

The [[id:dc80cb7b-694a-414b-9847-a1bdf4bef452][law of large numbers]] says the average converges to the mean and
says nothing about how. The central limit theorem answers that: the
error, rescaled by \(\sqrt{n}\), converges to one law, the same law
whatever the summands were. Only the first two moments survive, which is
why the normal distribution is the one that keeps appearing in
statements that mention no normal distribution.

#+DEPENDS: def-characteristic-function, def-random-variable
#+NAME: def-standard-normal
#+begin_definition
The /standard normal/ law is the probability measure on \(\R\) with
density \(t \mapsto (2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-t^{2}/2}\) with respect to Lebesgue
measure; a random variable with that law is written \(Z\), and its
characteristic function is \(\varphi_{Z}(t) = e^{-t^{2}/2}\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-standard-normal, thm-moments-and-the-expansion-at-zero
#+NAME: prop-normalisation-of-a-sum
#+begin_proposition
Let \(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\) be independent, identically distributed and
square-integrable, with mean \(\mu\), variance \(\sigma^{2}>0\), and
\(S_{n} = X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}\). Put
\(Z_{n} = (S_{n}-n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt{n})\). Then \(\E[Z_{n}] = 0\),
\(\Var(Z_{n}) = 1\), and
\(\varphi_{Z_{n}}(t) = \varphi_{Y}(t/(\sigma\sqrt{n}))^{n}\), where
\(Y = X_{1}-\mu\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Centring and scaling give the mean and the variance by linearity and by
\(\Var(aX) = a^{2}\Var(X)\), the variance of \(S_{n}\) being
\(n\sigma^{2}\) by independence. For the characteristic function,
\(Z_{n} = \sum_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt{n})\) is a sum of \(n\)
independent variables each distributed as
\(Y/(\sigma\sqrt{n})\), so its characteristic function is the product
of the \(n\) equal factors \(\varphi_{Y}(t/(\sigma\sqrt{n}))\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-normalisation-of-a-sum, thm-moments-and-the-expansion-at-zero, thm-levy-continuity, def-standard-normal
#+NAME: thm-central-limit-theorem
#+begin_theorem
With the notation above, \(Z_{n} \to Z\) in distribution: for every
\(t \in \R\),
\[ \Prob\Bigl(\frac{S_{n}-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \le t\Bigr) \longrightarrow \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-s^{2}/2}\dd s. \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(Y = X_{1}-\mu\), so \(\E[Y] = 0\) and \(\E[Y^{2}] = \sigma^{2}\).
By the expansion at zero,
\(\varphi_{Y}(u) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}u^{2} + r(u)\) with
\(r(u)/u^{2}\to0\) as \(u \to 0\).

Fix \(t\) and put \(u_{n} = t/(\sigma\sqrt{n})\), so \(u_{n}\to0\). By
the previous proposition,
\[ \varphi_{Z_{n}}(t) = \Bigl(1 - \frac{t^{2}}{2n} + r(u_{n})\Bigr)^{n}. \]
Write \(c_{n} = -t^{2}/2 + n\,r(u_{n})\), so that the bracket is
\(1 + c_{n}/n\); then \(n\,r(u_{n}) = (t^{2}/\sigma^{2})\,r(u_{n})/u_{n}^{2} \to 0\),
so \(c_{n}\to -t^{2}/2\). For complex \(c_{n}\to c\) one has
\((1+c_{n}/n)^{n}\to e^{c}\) -- taking logarithms of the principal
branch, which is legitimate for large \(n\) since \(c_{n}/n \to 0\), and
using \(\log(1+z) = z + O(\abs{z}^{2})\). Hence
\(\varphi_{Z_{n}}(t) \to e^{-t^{2}/2} = \varphi_{Z}(t)\) for every
\(t\).

The limit is continuous at \(0\), so Levy's continuity theorem gives
\(Z_{n}\to Z\) in distribution, which is the displayed convergence at
every \(t\) -- the distribution function of \(Z\) being continuous
everywhere.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-central-limit-theorem, cor-chebyshev-inequality
#+NAME: cor-clt-refines-chebyshev
#+begin_corollary
Under the hypotheses above, for every \(a>0\),
\(\Prob(\abs{S_{n}-n\mu} \ge a\sigma\sqrt{n}) \to \Prob(\abs{Z}\ge a)\),
whereas Chebyshev's inequality gives only the bound \(1/a^{2}\) for
every \(n\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
The event is \(\abs{Z_{n}}\ge a\), and its probability is
\(1 - F_{Z_{n}}(a) + F_{Z_{n}}(-a)\) up to the boundary points, which
have probability \(0\) in the limit because \(F_{Z}\) is continuous;
convergence in distribution at the continuity points \(\pm a\) gives the
limit. The Chebyshev bound is the previous note's inequality applied to
\(S_{n}/n\) with \(\eps = a\sigma/\sqrt{n}\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Two hypotheses are doing all the work and neither is decorative. Finite
variance is necessary -- with heavier tails the correct scaling is not
\(\sqrt{n}\) and the limit is not normal -- and identical distribution
can be weakened but not dropped, the honest condition being Lindeberg's.
What is /not/ needed is any assumption on the shape of the law of
\(X_{1}\), and that is the content of the theorem: the limit forgets
everything except the first two moments.
#+end_remark
