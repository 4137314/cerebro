#+TITLE: Rings, ideals and the quotient ring
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:ring_theory:
#+MSC: 16D25, 13A15
#+HASH: d84e77ee8860

\(\Z\) and the polynomials over a field carry two operations, and the
theory of one operation -- groups -- does not see the interaction between
them. A ring is the axiomatisation of that interaction, and the
substitute for "normal subgroup" is not a subring but an ideal: the
condition needed to divide out is absorption under multiplication, and
subrings do not have it.

#+DEPENDS: def-abelian-group, def-binary-operation
#+NAME: def-ring
#+begin_definition
A /ring/ is a set \(R\) with two operations \(+\) and \(\cdot\) such
that \((R, +)\) is an [[id:c982302e-b3f8-40d7-b38d-73177ae644d4][abelian group]] with neutral element \(0\),
multiplication is associative with a neutral element \(1\), and the
distributive laws \(a(b+c) = ab+ac\) and \((a+b)c = ac+bc\) hold. The
ring is /commutative/ if \(ab = ba\) for all \(a, b\). A /unit/ is an
element with a two-sided multiplicative inverse.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-ring
#+NAME: prop-ring-arithmetic
#+begin_proposition
In any ring, \(0 \cdot a = a \cdot 0 = 0\) and \((-a)b = -(ab)\); and if
\(1 = 0\) then \(R = \set{0}\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
From \(0 \cdot a = (0+0)a = 0\cdot a + 0 \cdot a\), cancelling in the
additive group gives \(0 \cdot a = 0\); symmetrically \(a \cdot 0 = 0\).
Then \((-a)b + ab = (-a+a)b = 0 \cdot b = 0\), so \((-a)b = -(ab)\). If
\(1 = 0\) then \(a = a \cdot 1 = a \cdot 0 = 0\) for every \(a\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-ring
#+NAME: def-ideal
#+begin_definition
A subset \(I \subseteq R\) is a /left ideal/ if it is a subgroup of
\((R,+)\) and \(r x \in I\) for all \(r \in R\), \(x \in I\); a /right
ideal/ if instead \(xr \in I\); and a /two-sided ideal/ (here simply an
/ideal/) if both. For \(a \in R\) commutative, \((a) = aR\) is the
/principal ideal/ generated by \(a\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-ideal, thm-quotient-group, thm-universal-property-of-a-quotient-set
#+NAME: thm-quotient-ring
#+begin_theorem
Let \(I\) be a two-sided ideal of \(R\). Then the additive quotient
group \(R/I\) is a ring under \((a + I)(b + I) = ab + I\), and
\(\pi \colon R \to R/I\) is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel
\(I\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
\((R,+)\) is abelian, so \(I\) is a normal subgroup of it and \(R/I\) is
an abelian group. For the product, suppose \(a + I = a' + I\) and
\(b + I = b' + I\), say \(a' = a + x\) and \(b' = b + y\) with
\(x, y \in I\). Then \(a'b' = ab + xb + ay + xy\), and each of
\(xb, ay, xy\) lies in \(I\) because \(I\) absorbs multiplication on
both sides; hence \(a'b' + I = ab + I\) and the product is well defined.
Associativity, distributivity and the identity \(1 + I\) are inherited
from \(R\) term by term. The projection preserves both operations by
construction, and \(\pi(a) = I\) says \(a \in I\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The proof shows precisely why a subring is not enough: well-definedness
needed \(xb\) and \(ay\) to lie in \(I\) for arbitrary \(a, b \in R\),
which is absorption, not closure under multiplication. In \(\Z\) the
subring is \(\Z\) itself and the ideals are the \(n\Z\) -- and it is the
latter that produce modular arithmetic.
#+end_remark

#+DEPENDS: def-ring, def-ideal
#+NAME: def-ring-homomorphism
#+begin_definition
A /ring homomorphism/ is a map \(\varphi \colon R \to S\) with
\(\varphi(a+b) = \varphi(a)+\varphi(b)\),
\(\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)\) and \(\varphi(1) = 1\). Its
/kernel/ is \(\ker\varphi = \setst{a}{\varphi(a) = 0}\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-ring-homomorphism, def-ideal
#+NAME: prop-kernel-is-an-ideal
#+begin_proposition
The kernel of a ring homomorphism is a two-sided ideal, and every
two-sided ideal is such a kernel.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
The kernel is a subgroup of \((R,+)\) because \(\varphi\) is additive,
and if \(\varphi(x) = 0\) then
\(\varphi(rx) = \varphi(r)\varphi(x) = 0\) and likewise
\(\varphi(xr) = 0\); so it absorbs on both sides. Conversely an ideal
\(I\) is the kernel of \(\pi \colon R \to R/I\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-quotient-ring, prop-kernel-is-an-ideal, thm-first-isomorphism-groups
#+NAME: thm-first-isomorphism-rings
#+begin_theorem
For a ring homomorphism \(\varphi \colon R \to S\), the image
\(\varphi(R)\) is a subring of \(S\) and
\(R/\ker\varphi \iso \varphi(R)\) as rings, via
\(a + \ker\varphi \mapsto \varphi(a)\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The image contains \(1\) and is closed under sums, negatives and
products because \(\varphi\) preserves them, so it is a subring. By the
first isomorphism theorem for the additive groups, the stated map is a
well-defined isomorphism of abelian groups onto \(\varphi(R)\), the
cosets of \(\ker\varphi\) being the fibres of \(\varphi\). It is
multiplicative and unital because
\(\bar{\varphi}((a+I)(b+I)) = \varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)\) and
\(\bar{\varphi}(1 + I) = 1\).
#+end_proof
