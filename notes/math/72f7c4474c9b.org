#+TITLE: Regular and normal spaces, and Urysohn's lemma
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:topology:general_topology:
#+MSC: 54D15
#+HASH: 72f7c4474c9b

A topology may have far too few continuous real functions: on an
indiscrete space the only ones are the constants. Urysohn's lemma is the
theorem that separation by open sets is enough to produce them -- in a
normal space, any two disjoint closed sets are separated by a function.
That is what makes a topological space accessible to analysis at all.

#+DEPENDS: def-hausdorff, def-topological-space, prop-points-are-closed-in-a-hausdorff-space
#+NAME: def-regular-and-normal
#+begin_definition
A topological space in which points are closed is /regular/ if a point
and a closed set not containing it have disjoint open neighbourhoods,
and /normal/ if any two disjoint closed sets do.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-regular-and-normal, prop-interior-and-closure-are-extremal
#+NAME: prop-normality-by-shrinking
#+begin_proposition
A space with closed points is normal if and only if for every closed
\(C\) and open \(U \supseteq C\) there is an open \(V\) with
\(C \subseteq V \subseteq \overline{V}\subseteq U\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Assume normality and let \(C \subseteq U\). Then \(C\) and
\(X \setminus U\) are disjoint closed sets, so they have disjoint open
neighbourhoods \(V \supseteq C\) and \(W \supseteq X\setminus U\). Since
\(V \cap W = \emptyset\), \(V\) is contained in the closed set
\(X\setminus W\), hence so is \(\overline{V}\), and
\(X \setminus W \subseteq U\).

Conversely, given disjoint closed \(C, D\), apply the hypothesis to
\(C \subseteq X \setminus D\) to get \(V\) with
\(\overline{V}\subseteq X\setminus D\); then \(V\) and
\(X\setminus\overline{V}\) are disjoint open sets containing \(C\) and
\(D\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-regular-and-normal, prop-metric-spaces-are-hausdorff, def-metric-space, prop-compact-subset-of-a-hausdorff-space-is-closed
#+NAME: prop-metric-and-compact-spaces-are-normal
#+begin_proposition
Every metric space is normal, and every compact Hausdorff space is
normal.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
In a metric space, let \(C, D\) be disjoint and closed and put
\(f(x) = \dist(x,C) - \dist(x,D)\), where
\(\dist(x,A) = \inf_{a\in A}d(x,a)\) satisfies
\(\abs{\dist(x,A)-\dist(y,A)}\le d(x,y)\) and vanishes exactly on
\(\overline{A}\). Then \(f\) is continuous, negative on \(C\) and
positive on \(D\), so \(\setst{x}{f(x)<0}\) and \(\setst{x}{f(x)>0}\)
separate them.

In a compact Hausdorff space, the argument separating a point from a
compact set gives, for each \(x \in C\), disjoint open \(U_{x}\ni x\)
and \(W_{x}\supseteq D\), since \(D\) is compact as a closed subset.
The \(U_{x}\) cover the compact \(C\); take a finite subcover and
intersect the corresponding \(W_{x}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-normality-by-shrinking, def-continuous-map, thm-characterisations-of-continuity, thm-density-of-rationals
#+NAME: thm-urysohn-lemma
#+begin_theorem
Let \(X\) be normal and \(C, D\) disjoint closed subsets. Then there is
a continuous \(f \colon X \to [0,1]\) with \(f \equiv 0\) on \(C\) and
\(f\equiv1\) on \(D\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Enumerate the rationals of \([0,1]\) as \(q_{0}=0\), \(q_{1}=1\),
\(q_{2},\dots\). We construct open sets \(U_{q}\) with
\(\overline{U_{p}}\subseteq U_{q}\) whenever \(p<q\). Put
\(U_{1} = X\setminus D\) and, by the shrinking criterion applied to
\(C \subseteq U_{1}\), choose \(U_{0}\) open with
\(C \subseteq U_{0}\subseteq\overline{U_{0}}\subseteq U_{1}\).
Recursively, given \(U_{q}\) for \(q\) among \(q_{0},\dots,q_{n}\), let
\(p\) and \(r\) be the largest and smallest of those below and above
\(q_{n+1}\); since \(\overline{U_{p}}\subseteq U_{r}\), the criterion
gives \(U_{q_{n+1}}\) with
\(\overline{U_{p}}\subseteq U_{q_{n+1}}\subseteq\overline{U_{q_{n+1}}}\subseteq U_{r}\).

Define \(f(x) = \inf\setst{q}{x \in U_{q}}\), with \(f(x)=1\) when the
set is empty. Then \(f = 0\) on \(C\) and \(f = 1\) on \(D\).

Continuity: it suffices to check that the preimages of \([0,a)\) and of
\((a,1]\) are open, these forming a subbasis of \([0,1]\). Now
\(f(x)<a\) exactly when \(x \in U_{q}\) for some \(q<a\), so
\(f^{-1}([0,a)) = \bigcup_{q<a}U_{q}\) is open. And \(f(x)>a\) exactly
when there is \(q>a\) with \(x \notin \overline{U_{q}}\): if
\(f(x)>a\), pick rationals \(a<q<r<f(x)\), so \(x\notin U_{r}\) and
hence \(x \notin\overline{U_{q}}\); conversely
\(x\notin\overline{U_{q}}\) gives \(x \notin U_{p}\) for all \(p<q\),
so \(f(x)\ge q>a\). Hence
\(f^{-1}((a,1]) = \bigcup_{q>a}(X\setminus\overline{U_{q}})\) is open.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The lemma is the reason normality, rather than the Hausdorff property,
is the hypothesis of the metrisation and extension theorems: it is
exactly the amount of separation that produces functions. Note also what
it does /not/ give -- the function need not be non-zero only on \(C\),
and strengthening it to that ("perfect normality") is a genuinely
stronger axiom.
#+end_remark
