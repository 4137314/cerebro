#+TITLE: Polynomial rings, division and roots
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:commutative_algebra:
#+MSC: 13F20, 12E05
#+HASH: 6ad59197d502

A polynomial is not a function -- over a finite field two different
polynomials can induce the same function -- but a formal object, a
finitely supported sequence of coefficients. Keeping the distinction is
what makes the degree an invariant, and the degree is what makes
division with remainder possible; everything else in this note is that
division, applied.

#+DEPENDS: def-ring
#+NAME: def-polynomial-ring
#+begin_definition
Let \(R\) be a commutative ring. A /polynomial/ over \(R\) is a sequence
\((a_{0}, a_{1}, \dots)\) in \(R\) with \(a_{i} = 0\) for all but
finitely many \(i\), written \(\sum_{i} a_{i} x^{i}\). With coefficientwise
addition and the product \((ab)_{k} = \sum_{i+j=k} a_{i} b_{j}\) these
form the /polynomial ring/ \(R[x]\). The /degree/ \(\deg f\) of
\(f \ne 0\) is the largest \(i\) with \(a_{i} \ne 0\), and \(f\) is
/monic/ if that coefficient is \(1\); the degree of \(0\) is left
undefined.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-polynomial-ring, def-integral-domain
#+NAME: prop-degree-over-a-domain
#+begin_proposition
If \(R\) is an integral domain and \(f, g \in R[x]\) are non-zero, then
\(fg \ne 0\) and \(\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g\). Consequently \(R[x]\)
is a domain and its units are the units of \(R\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \(a_{m}, b_{n}\) be the leading coefficients. The coefficient of
\(x^{m+n}\) in \(fg\) is \(a_{m}b_{n}\), which is non-zero since \(R\)
is a domain, and every higher coefficient vanishes because each of its
terms has a factor of index above the degree. Hence \(fg \ne 0\) with
the stated degree. If \(fg = 1\) then \(\deg f + \deg g = 0\), so both
are constants, and they are inverse units of \(R\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-polynomial-ring
#+NAME: thm-polynomial-division
#+begin_theorem
Let \(R\) be commutative, \(g \in R[x]\) monic of degree \(n \ge 0\).
For every \(f \in R[x]\) there are unique \(q, r \in R[x]\) with
\(f = qg + r\) and either \(r = 0\) or \(\deg r < n\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Existence, by induction on \(\deg f\) (the case \(f = 0\) or
\(\deg f < n\) being \(q = 0\), \(r = f\)). If \(\deg f = m \ge n\)
with leading coefficient \(a\), then \(f - a x^{m-n} g\) has degree less
than \(m\), because \(g\) is monic and the two leading terms cancel; by
induction it is \(q_{1}g + r\), so \(f = (a x^{m-n} + q_{1})g + r\).

Uniqueness: if \(q g + r = q'g + r'\) then \((q - q')g = r' - r\). Were
\(q \ne q'\), the left side would have degree at least \(n\) -- \(g\) is
monic, so multiplying by \(g\) adds exactly \(n\) to the degree, no
cancellation of the leading term being possible -- while the right side
has degree less than \(n\) or is \(0\). So \(q = q'\) and then
\(r = r'\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Monic, not merely non-zero: over a general commutative ring one cannot
divide by the leading coefficient. Over a field every non-zero \(g\) is
a unit multiple of a monic one, so the theorem holds there for all
\(g \ne 0\) -- which is the form used to prove that \(K[x]\) is a
principal ideal domain.
#+end_remark

#+DEPENDS: thm-polynomial-division
#+NAME: cor-root-factor-theorem
#+begin_corollary
Let \(R\) be commutative and \(f \in R[x]\). Then \(a \in R\) is a root
of \(f\), that is \(f(a) = 0\), if and only if \(x - a\) divides \(f\)
in \(R[x]\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Divide by the monic \(x - a\): \(f = q \cdot (x-a) + r\) with \(r\)
constant. Evaluating at \(a\) -- a ring homomorphism \(R[x] \to R\),
since \(R\) is commutative -- gives \(f(a) = r\). So \(f(a) = 0\)
exactly when \(r = 0\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: cor-root-factor-theorem, prop-degree-over-a-domain
#+NAME: cor-number-of-roots
#+begin_corollary
A non-zero polynomial of degree \(n\) over an integral domain has at
most \(n\) roots in that domain.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Induct on \(n\). A non-zero constant has no roots. If \(\deg f = n \ge 1\)
and \(f\) has a root \(a\), write \(f = (x-a)q\) with
\(\deg q = n - 1\). Any root \(b \ne a\) satisfies
\((b-a)q(b) = 0\) with \(b - a \ne 0\), so \(q(b) = 0\) because the ring
is a domain; by induction there are at most \(n-1\) such \(b\), giving
at most \(n\) roots in all.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The hypothesis is not decorative: over a ring with zero divisors it
fails outright, and the failure is what the corollary is really about.
Read positively, it is the statement that a polynomial identity of
degree \(n\) can be checked on \(n+1\) points -- the principle behind
interpolation and behind every "two polynomials that agree often are
equal" argument.
#+end_remark
