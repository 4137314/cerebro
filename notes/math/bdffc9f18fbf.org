#+TITLE: Boolean algebras and Stone's representation theorem
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:foundations:logic:
#+MSC: 06E15, 03G05
#+HASH: bdffc9f18fbf

The algebra of propositions and the algebra of subsets obey the same
laws, and Stone's theorem says this is not an analogy: every Boolean
algebra /is/ an algebra of sets. The proof is the ultrafilter lemma, and
the sets in question are the ultrafilters -- so the points of the
representing space are exactly the consistent complete assignments of
truth values.

#+DEPENDS: def-partial-order, def-complete-lattice, def-ring
#+NAME: def-boolean-algebra
#+begin_definition
A /Boolean algebra/ is a set \(B\) with operations \(\wedge, \vee\),
a unary \(\neg\), and elements \(0 \ne 1\), such that \(\wedge\) and
\(\vee\) are commutative and associative, each distributes over the
other, \(a \wedge (a \vee b) = a = a \vee (a \wedge b)\),
\(a \wedge \neg a = 0\) and \(a \vee \neg a = 1\). One writes
\(a \le b\) for \(a \wedge b = a\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-boolean-algebra, def-partial-order
#+NAME: prop-boolean-order
#+begin_proposition
In a Boolean algebra, \(\le\) is a partial order with least element
\(0\) and greatest element \(1\), in which \(a \wedge b\) is the
infimum and \(a \vee b\) the supremum of \(\set{a,b}\); moreover
\(\neg\) is an order-reversing involution.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Reflexivity is \(a \wedge a = a\), which follows from the absorption
laws: \(a \wedge a = a \wedge (a \vee (a\wedge a)) = a\).
Antisymmetry: \(a \wedge b = a\) and \(b \wedge a = b\) give
\(a = b\) by commutativity. Transitivity: from \(a\wedge b = a\) and
\(b \wedge c = b\), \(a \wedge c = (a\wedge b)\wedge c = a \wedge b = a\).

\(a\wedge b \le a\) and \(a \wedge b \le b\) by associativity and
idempotence, and any \(c\) below both satisfies
\(c \wedge (a\wedge b) = c\); so \(a \wedge b\) is the infimum, and
dually for \(\vee\). Absorption gives \(0 \le a \le 1\). Finally
\(\neg\) is an involution because \(\neg\neg a\) and \(a\) have the same
meet and join with \(\neg a\), and it reverses the order since
\(a \le b\) implies \(\neg b \wedge \neg a = \neg(a \vee b) = \neg b\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-boolean-algebra, def-filter, thm-ultrafilter-lemma
#+NAME: def-boolean-ultrafilter
#+begin_definition
A /filter/ of a Boolean algebra \(B\) is a non-empty
\(F \subseteq B\) with \(0 \notin F\), closed under \(\wedge\) and
upward closed for \(\le\); an /ultrafilter/ is a maximal one.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-boolean-ultrafilter, prop-ultrafilter-criterion, thm-zorn
#+NAME: thm-boolean-ultrafilter-lemma
#+begin_theorem
A filter \(U\) of \(B\) is an ultrafilter if and only if for every
\(a \in B\) exactly one of \(a\), \(\neg a\) lies in \(U\); and every
filter extends to an ultrafilter. Consequently, for \(a \ne 0\) there is
an ultrafilter containing \(a\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Not both \(a\) and \(\neg a\) can lie in a filter, their meet being
\(0\). If \(U\) is maximal and \(a \notin U\), then some \(b \in U\) has
\(b \wedge a = 0\) -- otherwise the upward closure of
\(\setst{b\wedge a}{b \in U}\) would be a strictly larger filter -- and
then \(b \le \neg a\), so \(\neg a \in U\). Conversely the dichotomy
forbids any proper extension, exactly as for filters of sets.

Zorn's lemma applies to the filters containing a given one, the union of
a chain of filters being a filter. For \(a \ne 0\), the upward closure
of \(\set{a}\) is a filter.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-boolean-ultrafilter-lemma, def-topological-space, thm-basis-criterion, def-hausdorff, def-compact
#+NAME: thm-stone-representation
#+begin_theorem
Let \(S(B)\) be the set of ultrafilters of a Boolean algebra \(B\), and
for \(a \in B\) put \(\hat{a} = \setst{U \in S(B)}{a \in U}\). Then the
family \(\setst{\hat{a}}{a \in B}\) is a basis of a topology on
\(S(B)\), and \(a \mapsto \hat{a}\) is an injective map of \(B\) onto a
family of subsets closed under intersection, union and complement, with
\(\widehat{a\wedge b} = \hat{a}\cap\hat{b}\),
\(\widehat{a\vee b} = \hat{a}\cup\hat{b}\) and
\(\widehat{\neg a} = S(B)\setminus\hat{a}\). Every Boolean algebra is
therefore isomorphic to an algebra of sets.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The three displayed identities are the ultrafilter criterion: an
ultrafilter contains \(a\wedge b\) exactly when it contains both (it is
closed under \(\wedge\) and upward closed), it contains \(\neg a\)
exactly when it does not contain \(a\), and the identity for \(\vee\)
follows from the other two by \(a \vee b = \neg(\neg a\wedge\neg b)\).
In particular the family is closed under finite intersections, so it is
a basis, and \(\hat{1} = S(B)\) covers.

Injectivity: if \(a \ne b\), then \(a \wedge \neg b \ne 0\) or
\(\neg a \wedge b \ne 0\) -- otherwise \(a \le b\) and \(b \le a\). Say
the first; by the previous theorem some ultrafilter contains
\(a\wedge\neg b\), hence lies in \(\hat{a}\) and not in \(\hat{b}\).
Being a bijection onto its image preserving the operations, the map is
an isomorphism of Boolean algebras.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The topology makes \(S(B)\) compact and Hausdorff with a basis of clopen
sets, and the correspondence is a duality: continuous maps of such
spaces correspond to homomorphisms of Boolean algebras, backwards. Read
in logic, \(S(B)\) for the algebra of formulas modulo equivalence is the
space of complete consistent theories, and its compactness is the
compactness theorem.
#+end_remark
