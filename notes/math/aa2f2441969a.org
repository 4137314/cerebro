#+TITLE: Statistics

Probability computes forwards, from a law to the behaviour of a sample;
statistics computes backwards, from a sample to the law. The formal
difference is a single quantifier -- the measure is not given but ranges
over a family -- and it changes what a theorem can say: no procedure is
right, so every result is a bound on how wrong one can be, or a proof
that no procedure does better.

Part of [[id:79a8bba8-94c4-44b9-9020-df8e30d12480][Probability]].

* Estimation

[[id:9881653c-b669-4380-85d7-815b643c02a2][Statistical models, estimators and consistency]] sets up the vocabulary
and proves the two things one can say about an estimator with no further
hypotheses: the mean squared error splits into variance and squared
bias, and the sample mean is consistent by the [[id:dc80cb7b-694a-414b-9847-a1bdf4bef452][law of large numbers]].

Then two theorems that say where the good estimators are and how good
they can be:

- [[id:9980a57d-f360-46ea-bba4-3e1ec57cbbe8][Sufficiency and the factorisation theorem]] -- a statistic that keeps
  everything relevant to the parameter, recognised by inspecting how the
  density depends on it.
- [[id:2cbd79ad-4938-4b5b-a6ca-85d300cc1f79][The Rao-Blackwell theorem]] -- conditioning any unbiased estimator on a
  sufficient statistic never makes it worse. The proof is Pythagoras in
  \(L^{2}\), which is why [[id:3c9568ab-072c-4973-9f6b-7506e431be22][conditional expectation]] had to be built
  geometrically first.
- [[id:8cf8a109-5256-46f2-9553-c27e2de0a34e][Fisher information and the Cramer-Rao bound]] -- a floor on the
  variance of every unbiased estimator, depending on the model alone.

Read together they are one argument: improve towards a sufficient
statistic, and stop when the Cramer-Rao floor is reached.

* Decisions

[[id:be7f8732-a492-4657-ae3b-f17b9bf70259][Hypothesis tests and the Neyman-Pearson lemma]] is the other half of the
subject: two kinds of error that cannot both be made small, a
convention that fixes one and minimises the other, and a complete answer
for two simple hypotheses -- reject where the likelihood ratio is large.

* Linear models

[[id:b0adbfc8-96a2-49be-9d03-4e4b419845e4][Least squares and the Gauss-Markov theorem]] is where statistics is
[[id:264810a5-aa5e-4f9b-934b-8e9d286eae4d][linear algebra]]: the fitted values are an orthogonal projection, the
normal equations are that projection in coordinates, and optimality
among linear unbiased estimators follows from Pythagoras again. Notably
no distributional assumption is used.

* What is missing

Maximum likelihood, its equivariance and its asymptotic normality -- the
single largest gap, and the method most used in practice; the
Lehmann-Scheffe theorem, which turns [[id:2cbd79ad-4938-4b5b-a6ca-85d300cc1f79][Rao-Blackwell]] into a uniqueness
statement; confidence intervals and their duality with tests; the
chi-squared, \(t\) and \(F\) distributions, which need the normal theory
of quadratic forms; Bayesian inference; and nonparametric methods.
