#+TITLE: Covering spaces and the fundamental group of the circle
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:topology:algebraic_topology:
#+MSC: 55Q05, 57M10
#+HASH: 8ec154cb8699

The fundamental group was defined for every space and computed for none.
The first computation is the circle, and it is not an accident that it
needs a new idea: loops are compared by lifting them to the line, where
every loop unwinds into a path and the only invariant left is where that
path ends. The winding number is that endpoint.

#+DEPENDS: def-continuous-map, def-homeomorphism, def-subspace-topology
#+NAME: def-covering-map
#+begin_definition
A continuous surjection \(p \colon E \to B\) is a /covering map/ if
every \(b \in B\) has an open neighbourhood \(U\) such that
\(p^{-1}(U)\) is a disjoint union of open sets, each mapped
homeomorphically onto \(U\) by \(p\). Such a \(U\) is /evenly covered/,
and the pieces are its /sheets/.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-covering-map, prop-pasting-lemma, thm-closed-interval-is-compact, lem-lebesgue-number
#+NAME: thm-path-lifting
#+begin_theorem
Let \(p \colon E \to B\) be a covering map, \(\gamma \colon [0,1]\to B\)
a path and \(e_{0}\in p^{-1}(\gamma(0))\). Then there is exactly one
path \(\tilde{\gamma}\colon[0,1]\to E\) with \(p\tilde{\gamma} = \gamma\)
and \(\tilde{\gamma}(0) = e_{0}\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Cover \(B\) by evenly covered open sets; their preimages under
\(\gamma\) form an open cover of the compact metric space \([0,1]\), so
it has a Lebesgue number, and there is a partition
\(0 = t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n} = 1\) with each
\(\gamma([t_{k},t_{k+1}])\) inside an evenly covered \(U_{k}\).

Construct \(\tilde{\gamma}\) on \([0,t_{k}]\) by induction. Given
\(\tilde{\gamma}(t_{k})\), let \(S\) be the unique sheet over
\(U_{k}\) containing it; define \(\tilde{\gamma}\) on
\([t_{k},t_{k+1}]\) as \((p\vert_{S})^{-1}\circ\gamma\), which is
continuous and agrees with the previous piece at \(t_{k}\). The pasting
lemma over the closed intervals makes the result continuous.

Uniqueness: let \(\tilde{\gamma}\) and \(\tilde{\delta}\) be two lifts
agreeing at \(0\), and let \(A\) be the set where they agree. \(A\) is
non-empty. It is open and closed: near any \(t\), both lifts stay in
sheets over an evenly covered neighbourhood of \(\gamma(t)\); if they
agree at \(t\) they are in the same sheet and hence equal near \(t\)
since \(p\) is injective there, and if they differ at \(t\) they are in
different, disjoint sheets and hence differ near \(t\). As \([0,1]\) is
connected, \(A = [0,1]\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-path-lifting, def-path-homotopy, lem-lebesgue-number, prop-pasting-lemma
#+NAME: thm-homotopy-lifting
#+begin_theorem
With \(p\) as above, let \(H \colon [0,1]^{2}\to B\) be continuous and
\(e_{0}\in p^{-1}(H(0,0))\). Then there is exactly one continuous
\(\tilde{H}\) with \(p\tilde{H} = H\) and \(\tilde{H}(0,0) = e_{0}\). In
particular, path-homotopic paths lift to path-homotopic paths with the
same endpoints.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
As before, a Lebesgue number for the cover of \([0,1]^{2}\) by the
preimages of evenly covered sets gives a grid of closed squares each
mapped by \(H\) into an evenly covered set. Lift the squares one at a
time, in the order of a path through the grid that always meets the
already-lifted region in a connected set: on each square, choose the
sheet containing the already-defined values, which is forced because
that region is connected and its image is in one sheet. Continuity
follows from the pasting lemma, and uniqueness by the connectedness
argument of the previous theorem applied to \([0,1]^{2}\).

If \(H\) is a path homotopy, \(H(0,\cdot)\) and \(H(1,\cdot)\) are
constant, so their lifts are paths lying in the discrete fibres over
those points, hence constant; therefore \(\tilde{H}\) is a path
homotopy between the lifts of the two paths, which in particular have
common endpoints.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-path-lifting, thm-homotopy-lifting, thm-fundamental-group, thm-classification-of-cyclic-groups
#+NAME: thm-fundamental-group-of-the-circle
#+begin_theorem
\(\pi_{1}(S^{1},1) \iso \Z\), the isomorphism sending the class of a
loop to the endpoint of its lift starting at \(0\) under
\(p(t) = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The map \(p \colon \R \to S^{1}\) is a covering: each of the four open
half-circles is evenly covered, its preimage being a disjoint union of
open intervals each mapped homeomorphically.

Define \(\deg[\alpha] = \tilde{\alpha}(1)\), where \(\tilde{\alpha}\) is
the lift of \(\alpha\) starting at \(0\). It lands in
\(p^{-1}(1) = \Z\), it is well defined by the homotopy lifting theorem,
and it is a homomorphism: the lift of \(\alpha\cdot\beta\) starting at
\(0\) is \(\tilde{\alpha}\) followed by \(\tilde{\beta}\) translated by
\(\tilde{\alpha}(1)\), which is a lift of \(\beta\) starting there, so
the endpoint is \(\deg[\alpha]+\deg[\beta]\).

Surjective: the path \(t\mapsto nt\) in \(\R\) projects to a loop of
degree \(n\). Injective: if \(\deg[\alpha] = 0\) then
\(\tilde{\alpha}\) is a loop in \(\R\), which is convex, so
\(\tilde{\alpha}\) is path-homotopic to the constant by the
straight-line homotopy; composing that homotopy with \(p\) shows
\(\alpha\) is null-homotopic.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-fundamental-group-of-the-circle, thm-homotopy-invariance-of-the-fundamental-group, prop-convex-sets-are-contractible
#+NAME: cor-no-retraction-of-the-disc
#+begin_corollary
There is no retraction of the closed disc \(D^{2}\) onto its boundary
circle; consequently every continuous \(f \colon D^{2}\to D^{2}\) has a
fixed point.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Suppose \(r \colon D^{2}\to S^{1}\) were a retraction and let
\(\iota\) be the inclusion. Then \(r\circ\iota = \id_{S^{1}}\), so
\(r_{*}\circ\iota_{*} = \id\) on \(\pi_{1}(S^{1}) \iso \Z\); but
\(\iota_{*}\) factors through \(\pi_{1}(D^{2})\), which is trivial since
\(D^{2}\) is convex and hence contractible. So the identity of \(\Z\)
factors through the trivial group -- impossible.

If \(f\) had no fixed point, the map sending \(x\) to the point where
the ray from \(f(x)\) through \(x\) meets \(S^{1}\) would be
continuous -- the intersection point depends continuously on the two
distinct points -- and would fix \(S^{1}\) pointwise, giving a
retraction.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Brouwer's theorem in dimension two costs exactly one computation of a
fundamental group, and the pattern is the whole method of algebraic
topology: a geometric impossibility becomes an algebraic one under a
functor. In higher dimensions \(\pi_{1}\) is useless -- spheres of
dimension at least two are simply connected -- and homology is what
replaces it.
#+end_remark
