Analysis is one subject because it has one hypothesis: completeness.
Every existence theorem below -- a limit, a supremum, a root, a fixed
point, an integral, a solution of a differential equation -- is
completeness of some space, applied to a sequence built for the purpose.
Everything else is an estimate.

Part of [[id:c2fcb100-fc5a-4670-9b5d-c068c0c325ff][Mathematics]].

* The subjects

- [[id:49738d4c-dd4b-4acf-a61b-440121b35d24][Real analysis]] -- the line, sequences and series, continuity,
  differentiation, the Riemann integral, uniform convergence. The bulk
  of the archive, and what everything later abstracts.
- [[id:a61fbcc2-37e7-4599-9e1a-89524a75c335][Complex analysis]] -- the same definition of the derivative in the
  plane, and a subject that behaves nothing like the real one.
- [[id:aa69bc51-db4c-4ffb-b7a7-875f9261af23][Functional analysis]] -- the same arguments with the points replaced
  by functions, where the unit ball is no longer compact.
- [[id:24714815-1fd5-4557-b9c6-a29939957dbb][Partial differential equations]] -- the three model equations, each
  met through the statement that decides uniqueness for it.

  Lebesgue integral]] up to the convergence theorems, and

* Where completeness enters, in order

1. [[id:a8f6ea88-2ae8-479a-bbbd-729e7e40ae78][As an axiom about \(\R\)]], from which the [[id:3df902f9-c527-40c3-994a-78fa87ef1063][supremum]] exists at all.
2. [[id:902b2920-a465-45dc-91a9-01af8e7b7c2d][As monotone convergence]] and [[id:db92d316-8a78-4116-8efb-89274901fdcd][Bolzano-Weierstrass]], the two ways of
   producing a limit from a bound.
3. [[id:03e16e45-1d12-46e1-af18-9f149478b146][As the Cauchy criterion]], which is the form that survives the loss of
   the order and hence generalises to [[id:ab5041dc-f782-4348-8303-01facc0b23d1][metric spaces]].
4. [[id:2f070853-c1c0-4018-9a23-d2caac15f2b4][As compactness]], once "complete and totally bounded" is recognised
   as the same hypothesis; and [[id:44de8493-8687-4cfd-8c39-65561e38033c][as Baire's theorem]], which is what makes
   completeness pay in infinite dimension.

Read that list backwards and it is the history of the subject: the
theorems came first, the hypothesis was isolated afterwards.

* The two integrals

The [[id:29d7ea7b-5f6b-48e4-949b-553595dfa9a3][Riemann integral]] partitions the domain and integrates what is
nearly continuous; the [[id:8cbdd35a-bbc4-44cf-9b1d-0b9623e3dc25][Lebesgue integral]] partitions the range and
integrates what is measurable. The whole difference is visible in the
convergence theorems: Riemann needs [[id:0e79d289-c68d-4e86-824e-4dfa51bcad21][uniform convergence]] to exchange
limit and integral, Lebesgue needs only a dominating function. Neither
subsumes the other in this archive -- the Riemann theory is where
[[id:40e468eb-db21-4321-8a3d-8ef0ec032875][the fundamental theorem of calculus]] lives, and the Lebesgue theory has
no construction of its measure yet.

* Calculus in several variables

The second-year material, which is where analysis stops being about one
variable: [[id:b6f64ed6-db0e-41c8-a093-0df725e22ecf][the inverse and implicit function theorems]] -- the contraction
principle in disguise, and the licence for every coordinate change --
[[id:07b82d71-2c7c-4ddd-84ce-185047ee07e5][constrained extrema and Lagrange multipliers]],
[[id:b9ad54df-4188-44c8-bc78-494d9a6728c8][product measures, Tonelli and Fubini]], and the two theorems that turn a
derivative over a region into data on its boundary:
[[id:f068fc75-7495-420e-8778-f63995b1d1f2][line integrals and Green's theorem]] and
[[id:ef0a7e9f-5957-4e21-8f97-59e80b04aa33][the divergence and Stokes theorems]].

* Harmonic analysis

[[id:58054969-7179-42fd-9754-f55d009d7cc6][Fourier series]] are an orthonormal expansion in \(L^{2}\), so
Parseval's identity is free once Fejer's theorem supplies completeness;
[[id:f667edb9-9594-4469-a3cf-1dd0c497ae9e][the Fourier transform]] does the same on the line, with inversion and
Plancherel; and [[id:e57a531d-6f95-4eff-9ad8-7ae4b983f912][Sturm-Liouville problems]] are what separation of
variables produces.

* What is missing

\(L^{p}\) spaces for \(p \ne 2\) and the duality that goes with them;
the construction of Lebesgue measure, which is used throughout and built
nowhere; distributions; the spectral theorem for unbounded or compact
operators, which is what the eigenfunction expansions here are quietly
assuming.
