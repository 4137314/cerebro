#+TITLE: The alternating series test
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 40A05
#+HASH: 00f3cfd55c94

Every test so far decides convergence by comparing absolute values, and
so can only ever prove absolute convergence. This is the one test in a
first course that uses cancellation, and it is therefore the source of
every example of a series that converges but not absolutely.

#+DEPENDS: def-series-convergence, thm-monotone-convergence, thm-limit-algebra
#+NAME: thm-leibniz
#+begin_theorem
Let \(b_{1} \ge b_{2} \ge \cdots \ge 0\) with \(b_{n} \to 0\). Then
the alternating series \(\sum_{n \ge 1} (-1)^{n+1} b_{n}\) converges.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(s_{N}\) be the partial sums. Grouping in pairs,
\[ s_{2k+2} - s_{2k} = b_{2k+1} - b_{2k+2} \ge 0, \qquad s_{2k+1} - s_{2k-1} = -b_{2k} + b_{2k+1} \le 0, \]
so the even partial sums increase and the odd ones decrease. Moreover
\(s_{2k+1} - s_{2k} = b_{2k+1} \ge 0\), so every even partial sum is at
most every odd one: \((s_{2k})\) is increasing and bounded above by
\(s_{1}\), and \((s_{2k+1})\) is decreasing and bounded below by
\(s_{2}\). By the monotone convergence theorem both converge, say to
\(E\) and \(O\). Since \(s_{2k+1} - s_{2k} = b_{2k+1} \to 0\), the
algebra of limits gives \(O - E = 0\). The two subsequences exhaust the
partial sums and share a limit, so \(s_{N}\) converges to it.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-leibniz, prop-limits-preserve-order
#+NAME: cor-leibniz-remainder
#+begin_corollary
Under the hypotheses above, the sum \(s\) satisfies
\(\abs{s - s_{N}} \le b_{N+1}\) for every \(N\): the error of a partial
sum is at most the first omitted term.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
The proof above shows \(s\) lies between any two consecutive partial
sums: \(s_{2k} \le s \le s_{2k+1}\) for every \(k\), by passing the
inequalities \(s_{2k} \le s_{2j+1}\) to the limit. Hence \(s - s_{N}\)
lies between \(0\) and \(s_{N+1} - s_{N} = \pm b_{N+1}\), so
\(\abs{s - s_{N}} \le b_{N+1}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-leibniz, prop-harmonic-diverges, def-absolute-convergence
#+NAME: prop-conditional-convergence-exists
#+begin_proposition
There is a convergent series that does not converge absolutely: the
class of conditionally convergent series is non-empty.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Take \(b_{n} = 1/n\), which decreases to \(0\), so
\(\sum_{n \ge 1} (-1)^{n+1}/n\) converges by the alternating series
test. The series of absolute values is \(\sum 1/n\), which diverges.
Hence the series converges and does not converge absolutely.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Conditional convergence is fragile in a way absolute convergence is
not: a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to
any prescribed real number, or to diverge. That is why every later
theorem that manipulates the order of summation assumes absolute
convergence, and why the distinction is worth a definition rather than
a remark.
#+end_remark
