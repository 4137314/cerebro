#+TITLE: Hypothesis tests and the Neyman-Pearson lemma
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:probability:statistics:
#+MSC: 62F03
#+HASH: 0abac08adeb5

A test is a decision rule, and it can be wrong in two ways that cannot
be made small at once. Fixing the tolerable rate of one and minimising
the other turns a vague question into a well-posed optimisation, and for
two simple hypotheses that optimisation has a complete answer: reject
where the likelihood ratio is large, and nothing else can do better.

#+DEPENDS: def-statistical-model, def-estimator, def-probability-space
#+NAME: def-test
#+begin_definition
Let \((\Prob_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}\) be a model and
\(\Theta = \Theta_{0}\cup\Theta_{1}\) a partition into a /null/ and an
/alternative/ hypothesis. A /test/ is a measurable
\(\phi \colon \mathcal{X}\to[0,1]\), interpreted as the probability of
rejecting the null on observing \(x\); it is /non-randomised/ if it
takes only the values \(0\) and \(1\), and its /rejection region/ is
then \(\setst{x}{\phi(x)=1}\). Its /power function/ is
\(\beta_{\phi}(\theta) = \E_{\theta}[\phi(X)]\), its /size/ is
\(\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\beta_{\phi}(\theta)\), and it has /level/
\(\alpha\) if its size is at most \(\alpha\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-test
#+NAME: prop-the-two-errors
#+begin_proposition
For \(\theta\in\Theta_{0}\), \(\beta_{\phi}(\theta)\) is the probability
of rejecting a true null -- the /first kind/ of error; for
\(\theta\in\Theta_{1}\), \(1-\beta_{\phi}(\theta)\) is the probability
of failing to reject a false one, the /second kind/. The test
\(\phi\equiv0\) has size \(0\) and power \(0\), and \(\phi\equiv1\) has
size \(1\) and power \(1\); so neither error can be made small without
reference to the other.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Both statements are the definition of \(\beta_{\phi}\) read under a
\(\theta\) on each side of the partition. For the two constant tests,
\(\E_{\theta}[0] = 0\) and \(\E_{\theta}[1] = 1\) for every \(\theta\),
which gives the stated sizes and powers.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-test, def-statistical-model
#+NAME: thm-neyman-pearson
#+begin_theorem
Let \(\Theta = \set{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}}\) with densities
\(f_{0}, f_{1}\) against a dominating measure \(\mu\), and let
\(\alpha \in [0,1]\). Suppose \(\phi^{*}\) satisfies, for some \(k \ge 0\),
\[ \phi^{*}(x) = 1 \text{ where } f_{1}(x) > k f_{0}(x), \qquad \phi^{*}(x) = 0 \text{ where } f_{1}(x) < k f_{0}(x), \]
and has size \(\E_{\theta_{0}}[\phi^{*}] = \alpha\). Then \(\phi^{*}\)
maximises \(\E_{\theta_{1}}[\phi]\) among all tests of level
\(\alpha\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(\phi\) be any test with \(\E_{\theta_{0}}[\phi]\le\alpha\).
Consider the function
\(D = (\phi^{*}-\phi)(f_{1}-kf_{0})\). At every \(x\) it is
non-negative: where \(f_{1}>kf_{0}\) the first factor is
\(1-\phi(x)\ge0\) and the second is positive; where \(f_{1}<kf_{0}\) the
first is \(-\phi(x)\le0\) and the second is negative; where they are
equal the second factor vanishes.

Integrating against \(\mu\),
\[ 0 \le \int D \dd\mu = \bigl(\E_{\theta_{1}}[\phi^{*}]-\E_{\theta_{1}}[\phi]\bigr) - k\bigl(\E_{\theta_{0}}[\phi^{*}]-\E_{\theta_{0}}[\phi]\bigr). \]
The second bracket is \(\alpha - \E_{\theta_{0}}[\phi] \ge 0\), so
\(\E_{\theta_{1}}[\phi^{*}]-\E_{\theta_{1}}[\phi] \ge k(\alpha-\E_{\theta_{0}}[\phi]) \ge 0\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-neyman-pearson, def-test
#+NAME: cor-most-powerful-tests-are-likelihood-ratio-tests
#+begin_corollary
Any test that is most powerful at level \(\alpha\) and has size exactly
\(\alpha\) agrees with a test of the displayed form, except possibly on
the set where \(f_{1} = kf_{0}\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Let \(\phi\) be most powerful of size \(\alpha\) and \(\phi^{*}\) as in
the theorem. Both have size \(\alpha\) and, by maximality of each, the
same power, so the displayed inequality is an equality and
\(\int D\dd\mu = 0\). Since \(D \ge 0\), it vanishes almost everywhere,
which forces \(\phi = \phi^{*}\) wherever \(f_{1}\ne kf_{0}\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The randomisation allowed by letting \(\phi\) take intermediate values
is not a mathematical nicety: with a discrete statistic there may be no
rejection region of size exactly \(\alpha\), and without randomising,
the exact-size hypothesis of the theorem would be unsatisfiable. Beyond
two simple hypotheses the clean statement fails, and what survives is
the theory of uniformly most powerful tests for families with a
monotone likelihood ratio.
#+end_remark
