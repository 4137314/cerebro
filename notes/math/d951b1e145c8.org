#+TITLE: First-order equations and the method of characteristics
#+DATE: [2026-08-11 Tue]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:partial_differential_equations:
#+MSC: 35F05, 35A30
#+HASH: d951b1e145c8

A first-order partial differential equation constrains the derivative of
the unknown along one direction at each point. Following that direction
turns the equation into an ordinary one, so the whole theory is the
[[id:8cd15fbc-3e1f-41a6-8fde-31963a195e78][existence theorem for ordinary equations]] transported along a family of
curves -- and the failures of the method, where characteristics cross or
run along the data, are the failures of the problem itself.

#+DEPENDS: def-differentiable-multivariable, thm-picard-lindelof, def-regular-curve
#+NAME: def-linear-first-order-equation
#+begin_definition
A /linear first-order equation/ on an open \(U\subseteq\R^{n}\) is
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i}(x)\,\partial_{i}u(x) = c(x)\,u(x) + d(x), \]
with \(a = (a_{i})\), \(c\), \(d\) of class \(C^{1}\). The
/characteristic curves/ are the solutions of the ordinary system
\(\dot{x}(s) = a(x(s))\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-linear-first-order-equation, thm-chain-rule, thm-picard-lindelof
#+NAME: prop-the-equation-along-a-characteristic
#+begin_proposition
Let \(u\) be a \(C^{1}\) solution and \(x(s)\) a characteristic. Then
\(z(s) = u(x(s))\) satisfies the ordinary differential equation
\(\dot{z} = c(x(s))z + d(x(s))\); conversely, if \(u\) is \(C^{1}\) and
this holds along every characteristic, \(u\) solves the equation.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
By the chain rule
\(\dot{z}(s) = \inner{\nabla u(x(s))}{\dot{x}(s)} = \sum_{i}a_{i}(x(s))\partial_{i}u(x(s))\),
which the equation identifies with \(c u + d\) at that point. Since
through every point there passes a characteristic -- the ordinary system
has a solution with that initial condition, its right-hand side being
\(C^{1}\) hence locally Lipschitz -- the converse holds pointwise.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-the-equation-along-a-characteristic, thm-picard-lindelof, thm-implicit-function
#+NAME: thm-cauchy-problem-for-a-first-order-equation
#+begin_theorem
Let \(S \subseteq U\) be a \(C^{1}\) hypersurface and \(u_{0}\) a
\(C^{1}\) function on \(S\). If \(a\) is nowhere tangent to \(S\) --
\(\inner{a(x)}{\nu(x)}\ne0\) for the normal \(\nu\) at each \(x \in S\)
-- then the Cauchy problem "\(u\) solves the equation and \(u = u_{0}\)
on \(S\)" has exactly one \(C^{1}\) solution on a neighbourhood of
\(S\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Parametrise \(S\) locally by \(y \mapsto \gamma(y)\), \(y\) in an open
subset of \(\R^{n-1}\). Solve the characteristic system with initial
value \(\gamma(y)\), obtaining \(X(s,y)\), and solve the ordinary
equation of the previous proposition with initial value \(u_{0}(\gamma(y))\),
obtaining \(Z(s,y)\); both depend smoothly on the data by the existence
and uniqueness theorem.

The map \((s,y)\mapsto X(s,y)\) has differential at \(s = 0\) with
columns \(a(\gamma(y))\) and the tangent vectors to \(S\); by the
transversality hypothesis these are independent, so the map is a local
diffeomorphism by the inverse function theorem. Define
\(u(X(s,y)) = Z(s,y)\), which is therefore a well-defined \(C^{1}\)
function near \(S\); by the proposition it solves the equation and it
restricts to \(u_{0}\) at \(s = 0\).

Uniqueness: any solution must satisfy the ordinary equation along each
characteristic with the prescribed initial value, and the solution of
that ordinary problem is unique.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-cauchy-problem-for-a-first-order-equation, def-linear-first-order-equation
#+NAME: cor-transport-equation
#+begin_corollary
The equation \(\partial_{t}u + b\cdot\nabla_{x}u = 0\) with constant
\(b \in \R^{n}\) and data \(u(0,x) = u_{0}(x)\) has the unique solution
\(u(t,x) = u_{0}(x-tb)\): the data are transported at velocity \(b\)
without change of shape.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
The coefficient vector is \((1,b)\) in the variables \((t,x)\), nowhere
tangent to the hypersurface \(\set{t=0}\), whose normal is
\((1,0)\). The characteristics are \((t, x_{0}+tb)\), along which the
equation reads \(\dot{z} = 0\), so \(u\) is constant along them; its
value is \(u_{0}(x_{0})\) with \(x_{0} = x - tb\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Transversality is not a technical convenience: where the data surface is
characteristic, the equation prescribes the derivative of \(u\) /along/
\(S\), so the problem is either overdetermined or has infinitely many
solutions. That is the first instance of the theme running through the
next three notes -- a partial differential equation is not solvable in
the abstract, only relative to data placed where the equation lets
information flow.
#+end_remark
