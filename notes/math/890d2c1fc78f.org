#+TITLE: Canonical transformations and Liouville's theorem
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:mathematical_physics:symplectic_geometry:
#+MSC: 70H15, 53D05
#+HASH: 890d2c1fc78f

A change of coordinates on phase space is useful only if Hamilton's
equations keep their form in the new ones. The transformations with that
property are exactly those preserving the Poisson bracket, and they
preserve one thing more, without any further hypothesis: the volume.
That is Liouville's theorem, and it is the reason Hamiltonian flows
cannot have attractors.

#+DEPENDS: def-poisson-bracket, def-differentiable-multivariable
#+NAME: def-canonical-transformation
#+begin_definition
A continuously differentiable change of variables
\((q,p) \mapsto (Q,P)\) on phase space is /canonical/ if it preserves
the fundamental brackets:
\[ \{Q_{i}, Q_{j}\} = 0, \qquad \{P_{i}, P_{j}\} = 0, \qquad \{Q_{i}, P_{j}\} = \delta_{ij}. \]
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-canonical-transformation, thm-evolution-is-a-bracket, prop-poisson-bracket-is-a-lie-bracket
#+NAME: thm-canonical-preserves-the-bracket
#+begin_theorem
A transformation is canonical if and only if
\(\{F, G\}_{q,p} = \{F, G\}_{Q,P}\) for all continuously differentiable
\(F, G\); and then Hamilton's equations in \((Q,P)\) hold with the same
Hamiltonian expressed in the new variables.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Expanding \(\{F,G\}_{q,p}\) by the chain rule in terms of derivatives
with respect to \(Q\) and \(P\) produces
\[ \{F,G\}_{q,p} = \sum_{i,j} \Bigl( \frac{\partial F}{\partial Q_{i}} \frac{\partial G}{\partial Q_{j}} \{Q_{i},Q_{j}\} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial Q_{i}} \frac{\partial G}{\partial P_{j}} \{Q_{i},P_{j}\} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial P_{i}} \frac{\partial G}{\partial Q_{j}} \{P_{i},Q_{j}\} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial P_{i}} \frac{\partial G}{\partial P_{j}} \{P_{i},P_{j}\} \Bigr), \]
where every bracket on the right is taken in \((q,p)\). The fundamental
brackets collapse this to \(\{F,G\}_{Q,P}\), and conversely taking
\(F, G\) to be the coordinate functions recovers the fundamental
brackets. For the equations of motion, apply the evolution formula to
\(Q_{i}\) and \(P_{i}\): \(\dot Q_{i} = \{Q_{i}, H\}\), which in the new
bracket is \(\partial H/\partial P_{i}\), and likewise for \(P\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-canonical-transformation, thm-hamilton-equations, def-differentiable-multivariable
#+NAME: thm-liouville
#+begin_theorem
The flow of a Hamiltonian vector field preserves phase-space volume: the
Jacobian determinant of the time-\(t\) flow map is identically \(1\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The flow is generated by the vector field
\(X = (\partial H/\partial p, -\partial H/\partial q)\). Its divergence
is
\[ \operatorname{div} X = \sum_{i} \Bigl( \frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}} \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}} - \frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}} \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}} \Bigr) = 0, \]
since mixed second partial derivatives of \(H\) commute. The Jacobian
\(J(t)\) of the flow satisfies \(\dd J/\dd t = (\operatorname{div} X) J\)
with \(J(0) = 1\), hence \(\dd J/\dd t = 0\); a function with vanishing
derivative on an interval is constant, so \(J \equiv 1\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The proof uses nothing about \(H\) beyond the equality of mixed
partials, so the conclusion is a property of the *structure*, not of any
particular mechanical system. It is also what rules out asymptotic
behaviour: a set of positive volume cannot be contracted to a point, so
a Hamiltonian system has no attracting equilibrium -- which is exactly
the difference between this and dissipative dynamics.
#+end_remark
