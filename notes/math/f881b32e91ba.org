#+TITLE: Poisson brackets
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:mathematical_physics:classical_mechanics:
#+MSC: 70H15, 37J05
#+HASH: f881b32e91ba

Hamilton's equations single out one function, \(H\), and describe how the
coordinates move. The bracket makes the picture symmetric: it says how
*any* observable moves, it turns "is conserved" into an algebraic
identity, and it makes the set of observables a Lie algebra in which the
dynamics is a single bracket with \(H\).

#+DEPENDS: def-hamiltonian, def-differentiable-multivariable
#+NAME: def-poisson-bracket
#+begin_definition
For continuously differentiable \(F, G\) on phase space, the /Poisson
bracket/ is
\[ \{F, G\} = \sum_{i} \Bigl( \frac{\partial F}{\partial q_{i}} \frac{\partial G}{\partial p_{i}} - \frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{i}} \frac{\partial G}{\partial q_{i}} \Bigr). \]
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-poisson-bracket, thm-derivative-algebra
#+NAME: prop-poisson-bracket-is-a-lie-bracket
#+begin_proposition
The bracket is bilinear, antisymmetric, satisfies the Leibniz rule
\(\{F, GK\} = \{F,G\}K + G\{F,K\}\), and satisfies the Jacobi identity
\[ \{F, \{G,K\}\} + \{G, \{K,F\}\} + \{K, \{F,G\}\} = 0. \]
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Bilinearity is immediate from linearity of the partial derivatives, and
antisymmetry from exchanging the two terms of the sum. The Leibniz rule
is the product rule applied to each partial derivative of \(GK\).

For Jacobi, observe that each of the three double brackets is a sum of
terms containing *second* derivatives of exactly one of \(F, G, K\).
Collect the terms carrying second derivatives of \(K\): they come from
\(\{F,\{G,K\}\}\) and \(\{G,\{K,F\}\} = -\{G,\{F,K\}\}\), and their sum
is the expression obtained by applying the antisymmetric second-order
operator \(\{F, \{G, \cdot\}\} - \{G, \{F, \cdot\}\}\) to \(K\). That
operator is a first-order derivation -- its second-order parts cancel
because mixed partials commute -- so no second derivatives of \(K\)
survive. By symmetry the same holds for \(F\) and \(G\), and a sum with
no second-derivative terms and no first-derivative terms is zero.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-poisson-bracket, thm-hamilton-equations
#+NAME: thm-evolution-is-a-bracket
#+begin_theorem
Along any solution of Hamilton's equations, a continuously
differentiable observable \(F(q,p,t)\) satisfies
\[ \frac{\dd F}{\dd t} = \{F, H\} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial t}. \]
In particular a time-independent \(F\) is conserved if and only if
\(\{F, H\} = 0\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
By the chain rule,
\(\dd F/\dd t = \sum_{i} (\partial_{q_{i}} F \, \dot q_{i} + \partial_{p_{i}} F \, \dot p_{i}) + \partial_{t} F\).
Substituting \(\dot q_{i} = \partial H/\partial p_{i}\) and
\(\dot p_{i} = -\partial H/\partial q_{i}\) turns the sum into exactly
\(\{F, H\}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-evolution-is-a-bracket, prop-poisson-bracket-is-a-lie-bracket
#+NAME: cor-poisson-theorem
#+begin_corollary
If \(F\) and \(G\) are conserved and time-independent, so is
\(\{F, G\}\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
By the Jacobi identity,
\(\{\{F,G\}, H\} = -\{\{G,H\},F\} - \{\{H,F\},G\}\), and both brackets
on the right vanish because \(\{F,H\} = \{G,H\} = 0\). Hence
\(\{F,G\}\) has vanishing bracket with \(H\) and is conserved.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The corollary is why the Jacobi identity is worth proving rather than
quoting: it says the conserved quantities form a Lie subalgebra, so
symmetries can be *combined*, and it is the classical shadow of the
commutator identity that plays the same role in quantum mechanics.
#+end_remark
