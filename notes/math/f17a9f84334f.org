#+TITLE: Finite fields
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:field_theory:
#+MSC: 12E20, 11T30
#+HASH: f17a9f84334f

There is exactly one field with \(q\) elements for every prime power
\(q\), and none of any other size. That is an unusually complete
classification, and every part of it comes from a single observation:
in characteristic \(p\) the map \(a \mapsto a^{p}\) is a field
homomorphism, so the equation \(x^{q} = x\) is linear-like and its
solution set is a field.

#+DEPENDS: def-integral-domain, def-ring, def-order-of-an-element
#+NAME: def-characteristic
#+begin_definition
The /characteristic/ of a ring \(R\) is the least \(n \ge 1\) with
\(n \cdot 1 = 0\), if there is one, and \(0\) otherwise. The /prime
field/ of a field is the subfield generated by \(1\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-characteristic, thm-first-isomorphism-rings, thm-prime-iff-quotient-is-a-domain
#+NAME: prop-characteristic-of-a-field
#+begin_proposition
The characteristic of a field is \(0\) or a prime \(p\); the prime field
is \(\Q\) in the first case and \(\F_{p} = \Z/p\Z\) in the second. A
finite field has \(p^{n}\) elements for some prime \(p\) and
\(n \ge 1\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
The map \(\Z \to K\), \(m \mapsto m\cdot1\), is a ring homomorphism; its
kernel is an ideal \(c\Z\), and \(\Z/c\Z\) embeds in the field \(K\),
hence is an integral domain, so \(c\) is \(0\) or prime. If \(c = 0\)
the image is a copy of \(\Z\) and the subfield it generates is \(\Q\);
if \(c = p\) the image is \(\F_{p}\), already a field.

A finite field \(K\) has characteristic \(p > 0\), and it is a vector
space over \(\F_{p}\) of some finite dimension \(n\); choosing a basis
gives a bijection with \(\F_{p}^{n}\), so \(\abs{K} = p^{n}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-characteristic, def-ring-homomorphism
#+NAME: prop-frobenius
#+begin_proposition
In a commutative ring of prime characteristic \(p\), the map
\(F(a) = a^{p}\) is a ring homomorphism; on a finite field it is an
automorphism.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Multiplicativity and \(F(1)=1\) are clear. For additivity, the binomial
theorem gives
\((a+b)^{p} = \sum_{k}\binom{p}{k}a^{k}b^{p-k}\), and for
\(0 < k < p\) the integer \(\binom{p}{k} = p!/(k!(p-k)!)\) is divisible
by \(p\), since \(p\) divides the numerator and not the denominator;
those terms vanish in characteristic \(p\). On a field \(F\) is
injective, its kernel being an ideal not containing \(1\), and an
injective map of a finite set to itself is bijective.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: cor-number-of-roots, def-order-of-an-element, thm-classification-of-cyclic-groups
#+NAME: thm-multiplicative-group-is-cyclic
#+begin_theorem
The multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(K\) be a field with \(\abs{K^{\times}} = m\), and let \(d\) be the
largest order of an element of \(K^{\times}\); say \(\ord(a) = d\).

Every element satisfies \(x^{d} = 1\). Indeed, if some \(b\) had order
\(e \nmid d\), then some prime power \(\ell^{k}\) divides \(e\) and not
\(d\); writing \(d = \ell^{j}d'\) with \(\ell\nmid d'\) and \(j<k\), the
element \(a^{\ell^{j}}b^{e/\ell^{k}}\) has order \(d'\ell^{k} > d\) --
the two factors have coprime orders \(d'\) and \(\ell^{k}\), and
commuting elements of coprime orders \(r, s\) have order \(rs\), since
\((xy)^{k} = 1\) puts \(x^{k} = y^{-k}\) in
\(\langle x\rangle\cap\langle y\rangle = \set{1}\), forcing \(r \mid k\)
and \(s \mid k\). This contradicts maximality of \(d\).

So all \(m\) elements of \(K^{\times}\) are roots of \(x^{d}-1\), which
has at most \(d\) roots in a field; hence \(m \le d\). As \(d\) divides
\(m\) by Lagrange, \(d = m\) and \(\langle a\rangle = K^{\times}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-frobenius, thm-splitting-field-exists, thm-splitting-field-is-unique, prop-characteristic-of-a-field, cor-number-of-roots
#+NAME: thm-existence-and-uniqueness-of-finite-fields
#+begin_theorem
For every prime \(p\) and \(n \ge 1\) there is a field with \(q = p^{n}\)
elements, namely the splitting field of \(x^{q}-x\) over \(\F_{p}\), and
any two fields with \(q\) elements are isomorphic.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(L\) be a splitting field of \(f = x^{q}-x\) over \(\F_{p}\) and
let \(E = \setst{a \in L}{a^{q} = a}\). Since \(a \mapsto a^{q}\) is the
\(n\)-th power of the Frobenius homomorphism, hence a homomorphism,
\(E\) is closed under sums, products and inverses of non-zero elements,
and contains \(0\) and \(1\): it is a subfield. It contains all roots of
\(f\), so \(L = E\) by the generation clause in the definition of a
splitting field.

The roots are distinct: a repeated root would be a common root of \(f\)
and its derivative \(qx^{q-1}-1 = -1\), which has none. So \(f\) has
exactly \(q\) roots and \(\abs{L} = q\).

Conversely let \(K\) be any field with \(q\) elements. Its
multiplicative group has order \(q-1\), so \(a^{q-1} = 1\) for
\(a \ne 0\) by Lagrange, and therefore \(a^{q} = a\) for every
\(a \in K\). Thus all \(q\) elements are roots of \(f\), which has at
most \(q\); so \(f\) splits in \(K\) and \(K\) is generated by its
roots -- \(K\) is a splitting field of \(f\) over its prime field
\(\F_{p}\). Uniqueness of splitting fields gives \(K \iso L\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The argument uses the derivative of a polynomial as a formal operation
on coefficients, with no limits involved: over an arbitrary field
\(\sum k a_{k}x^{k-1}\) is a definition, and "\(f\) has a repeated root
iff \(f\) and \(f'\) share one" is an algebraic identity. That formal
derivative is exactly what separability is defined by, and this proof is
the first place it does real work.
#+end_remark
