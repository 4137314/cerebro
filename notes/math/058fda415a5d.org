#+TITLE: Bilinear forms, quadratic forms and Sylvester's law of inertia
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:linear_algebra:
#+MSC: 15A63, 11E04
#+HASH: 058fda415a5d

An [[id:c35a8f01-cbd2-433b-b9b5-a037bae4e91d][inner product]] is a symmetric bilinear form that is positive definite.
Dropping definiteness leaves an object that no longer measures lengths
but still classifies: over the reals, a symmetric form is determined up
to change of basis by two integers, and no basis-dependent computation
can produce anything else. That is what a normal form theorem is for.

#+DEPENDS: def-vector-space, def-linear-map
#+NAME: def-bilinear-form
#+begin_definition
Let \(V\) be a vector space over a field \(K\). A /bilinear form/ is a
map \(b \colon V \times V \to K\) linear in each argument separately. It
is /symmetric/ if \(b(u,v) = b(v,u)\), and the associated /quadratic
form/ is \(q(v) = b(v,v)\). Vectors \(u, v\) are /orthogonal/ for \(b\)
if \(b(u,v) = 0\), and the /radical/ is
\(\setst{v \in V}{b(v,u) = 0 \text{ for all } u \in V}\); \(b\) is
/non-degenerate/ if the radical is \(\set{0}\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-bilinear-form
#+NAME: prop-polarisation
#+begin_proposition
Let \(K\) have characteristic \(\ne 2\). A symmetric bilinear form is
determined by its quadratic form:
\[ b(u,v) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(q(u+v) - q(u) - q(v)\bigr). \]
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Expanding by bilinearity,
\(q(u+v) = b(u+v,u+v) = q(u) + b(u,v) + b(v,u) + q(v)\), and symmetry
turns the middle terms into \(2b(u,v)\). Division by \(2\) is available
since the characteristic is not \(2\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-bilinear-form, prop-polarisation, cor-extension-to-a-basis, thm-rank-nullity
#+NAME: thm-diagonalisation-of-symmetric-forms
#+begin_theorem
Let \(K\) have characteristic \(\ne 2\) and let \(b\) be a symmetric
bilinear form on a finite-dimensional \(V\). Then \(V\) has a basis
\(e_{1}, \dots, e_{n}\) with \(b(e_{i}, e_{j}) = 0\) for \(i \ne j\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Induct on \(n = \dim V\); for \(n \le 1\) there is nothing to prove. If
\(q\) vanishes identically then \(b = 0\) by polarisation and any basis
works. Otherwise pick \(e_{1}\) with \(q(e_{1}) \ne 0\) and set
\(W = \setst{v}{b(e_{1}, v) = 0}\), the kernel of the non-zero linear
functional \(v \mapsto b(e_{1},v)\), so \(\dim W = n-1\) by
rank-nullity. Moreover \(e_{1} \notin W\), so
\(V = \Span\set{e_{1}} \oplus W\) by dimension count. The restriction of
\(b\) to \(W\) is symmetric bilinear, so by induction \(W\) has a basis
\(e_{2},\dots,e_{n}\) orthogonal for \(b\); together with \(e_{1}\),
which is orthogonal to all of \(W\), this is the required basis.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-diagonalisation-of-symmetric-forms, prop-grassmann-formula
#+NAME: thm-sylvester-law-of-inertia
#+begin_theorem
Let \(b\) be a symmetric bilinear form on a real vector space of
dimension \(n\). Then there is a basis in which
\(q(x) = x_{1}^{2} + \cdots + x_{p}^{2} - x_{p+1}^{2} - \cdots - x_{p+m}^{2}\),
and the pair \((p, m)\) -- the /signature/ -- does not depend on the
basis chosen.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
By the previous theorem there is a basis with
\(q(\sum x_{i}e_{i}) = \sum_{i} c_{i} x_{i}^{2}\), where
\(c_{i} = q(e_{i})\). Rescaling \(e_{i}\) by
\(\abs{c_{i}}^{-1/2}\) when \(c_{i} \ne 0\) and reordering brings
\(q\) to the displayed shape, with \(p\) the number of positive \(c_{i}\)
and \(m\) the number of negative ones.

For invariance, let \(P = \Span\set{e_{1},\dots,e_{p}}\), on which
\(q > 0\) off zero, and \(N = \Span\set{e_{p+1}, \dots, e_{n}}\), on
which \(q \le 0\). If \(P'\) is any subspace with \(q > 0\) off zero,
then \(P' \cap N = \set{0}\), so
\(\dim P' + \dim N \le n\) by the Grassmann formula, giving
\(\dim P' \le n - (n - p) = p\). Hence \(p\) is the largest dimension of
a subspace on which \(q\) is positive definite -- a description that
mentions no basis. The same argument applied to \(-q\) characterises
\(m\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Definiteness, the hypothesis that turns a symmetric form into an inner
product, is the case \((p,m) = (n,0)\); the Lorentzian form of
relativity is \((1,3)\). The theorem says these are genuinely different
objects and that no amount of change of coordinates can convert one into
the other -- which is why "signature" is data attached to a geometry
rather than to a choice of frame.
#+end_remark
