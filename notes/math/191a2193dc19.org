#+TITLE: Compactness and the Heine-Borel theorem
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:topology:general_topology:
#+MSC: 54D30
#+HASH: 191a2193dc19

Every theorem of the first course about [[id:0951689a-630a-41a9-9a31-a61b2f129444][continuous functions on a closed
interval]] -- boundedness, attainment of extrema, uniform continuity --
used the same feature of \([a,b]\) and nothing else: a local condition
holding at every point can be made to hold uniformly, because finitely
many neighbourhoods suffice. Compactness is that feature, named, and it
is what makes those proofs transfer.

#+DEPENDS: def-topological-space
#+NAME: def-compact
#+begin_definition
An /open cover/ of a topological space \(X\) is a family of open sets
whose union is \(X\). The space is /compact/ if every open cover has a
finite subcover. A subset is compact if it is so in the subspace
topology, equivalently if every cover of it by open sets of \(X\) has a
finite subfamily covering it.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-compact, def-continuous-map
#+NAME: thm-continuous-image-of-a-compact-space
#+begin_theorem
The continuous image of a compact space is compact. Compactness is
therefore a topological invariant.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(f \colon X \to Y\) be continuous and surjective with \(X\)
compact, and let \((V_{i})\) cover \(Y\). The sets \(f^{-1}(V_{i})\) are
open and cover \(X\), so finitely many of them cover \(X\); the
corresponding \(V_{i}\) then cover \(f(X) = Y\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-compact, prop-closed-sets-of-a-topology
#+NAME: prop-closed-subspace-of-a-compact-space
#+begin_proposition
A closed subset of a compact space is compact.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \(C \subseteq X\) be closed and let \((U_{i})\) be open sets of
\(X\) covering \(C\). Adding \(X \setminus C\), which is open, gives an
open cover of \(X\); extract a finite subcover and discard
\(X\setminus C\) from it. What remains is a finite subfamily of the
\(U_{i}\) covering \(C\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-compact, ax-completeness, def-supremum
#+NAME: thm-closed-interval-is-compact
#+begin_theorem
Every closed bounded interval \([a,b] \subseteq \R\) is compact.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(\mathcal{U}\) be a cover of \([a,b]\) by sets open in \(\R\), and
let
\(S = \setst{x \in [a,b]}{[a,x] \text{ is covered by finitely many members of } \mathcal{U}}\).
It contains \(a\) and is bounded above by \(b\), so \(s = \sup S\)
exists by completeness, and \(s \in [a,b]\).

Some \(U \in \mathcal{U}\) contains \(s\), and being open it contains
\((s-\delta, s+\delta)\) for some \(\delta > 0\). By definition of the
supremum there is \(x \in S\) with \(x > s - \delta\); a finite
subfamily covers \([a,x]\), and adding \(U\) covers
\([a, \min(s+\delta/2, b)]\). Hence \(s \in S\), and if \(s < b\) then
some point of \(S\) exceeds \(s\) -- impossible. So \(s = b \in S\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-closed-interval-is-compact, prop-closed-subspace-of-a-compact-space, prop-compact-subset-of-a-hausdorff-space-is-closed
#+NAME: thm-heine-borel-on-the-line
#+begin_theorem
A subset of \(\R\) is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(K\) be closed and bounded, so \(K \subseteq [a,b]\) for some
interval. Then \(K\) is a closed subset of the compact space \([a,b]\),
hence compact.

Conversely let \(K\) be compact. It is closed, being a compact subset of
the Hausdorff space \(\R\). It is bounded: the open intervals
\((-n, n)\), \(n \in \N\), cover \(K\), so finitely many do, and their
union is the largest of them.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-continuous-image-of-a-compact-space, thm-heine-borel-on-the-line, def-supremum, thm-extreme-value
#+NAME: cor-extreme-value-on-a-compact-space
#+begin_corollary
A continuous real-valued function on a non-empty compact space is
bounded and attains its supremum and its infimum.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
The image \(f(X) \subseteq \R\) is compact, hence closed and bounded.
Bounded and non-empty, it has a supremum \(M\); every neighbourhood of
\(M\) meets \(f(X)\) by the definition of the supremum, so
\(M \in \overline{f(X)} = f(X)\), that is \(M = f(x)\) for some \(x\).
The infimum is the same argument applied to \(-f\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
This is the extreme value theorem of the first course with the interval
replaced by any compact space, and the proof no longer mentions
sequences or subsequences at all. Which formulation to prove first is a
real choice: on metric spaces the covering and sequential definitions
agree, and the equivalence is itself a theorem worth isolating.
#+end_remark
