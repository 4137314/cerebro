#+TITLE: Random variables, laws and distribution functions
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:probability:probability_theory:
#+MSC: 60A10
#+HASH: c803fed2b7ce

A random variable is a measurable function, and nothing in the
definition is random. What the word marks is a change of viewpoint: the
underlying space is never described, and every statement is made about
the measure the function pushes onto the line. That measure is the
object of the subject, and the distribution function is the one-variable
handle on it.

#+DEPENDS: def-measurable-function, def-probability-space, def-borel-sigma-algebra
#+NAME: def-random-variable
#+begin_definition
A /random variable/ on \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},\Prob)\) is a measurable
map \(X \colon \Omega \to \R\). One writes \(\set{X \in B}\) for
\(X^{-1}(B)\) and \(\Prob(X \in B)\) for its probability.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-random-variable, def-measure, prop-measurability-tested-on-rays
#+NAME: thm-the-law-of-a-random-variable
#+begin_theorem
For a random variable \(X\), the /law/
\(\Prob_{X}(B) = \Prob(X \in B)\), defined for Borel \(B\), is a
probability measure on \(\R\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
\(\Prob_{X}\) is defined because \(X\) is measurable, and
\(\Prob_{X}(\R) = \Prob(\Omega) = 1\), \(\Prob_{X}(\emptyset) = 0\). If
\((B_{n})\) are disjoint Borel sets, their preimages are disjoint
events with union \(X^{-1}(\bigcup_{n}B_{n})\), so countable additivity
of \(\Prob\) gives countable additivity of \(\Prob_{X}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-the-law-of-a-random-variable, thm-basic-properties-of-a-measure
#+NAME: def-distribution-function
#+begin_definition
The /distribution function/ of \(X\) is
\(F_{X}(t) = \Prob(X \le t)\), \(t \in \R\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-distribution-function, thm-basic-properties-of-a-measure
#+NAME: thm-properties-of-the-distribution-function
#+begin_theorem
\(F_{X}\) is non-decreasing, right-continuous, and satisfies
\(\lim_{t\to-\infty}F_{X}(t) = 0\) and \(\lim_{t\to+\infty}F_{X}(t) = 1\).
Moreover \(\Prob(X = t) = F_{X}(t) - \lim_{s\uparrow t}F_{X}(s)\), so
\(F_{X}\) is continuous at \(t\) exactly when \(\Prob(X = t) = 0\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Monotonicity: \(s \le t\) gives \(\set{X\le s}\subseteq\set{X\le t}\).

Right-continuity: for \(t_{n}\downarrow t\) the events
\(\set{X\le t_{n}}\) decrease to \(\set{X\le t}\), so continuity from
above of the measure gives \(F_{X}(t_{n})\to F_{X}(t)\); since
\(F_{X}\) is monotone, convergence along one such sequence gives the
one-sided limit.

The limits at \(\pm\infty\) are continuity along
\(\set{X\le -n}\downarrow\emptyset\) and
\(\set{X\le n}\uparrow\Omega\).

For the jump, \(\set{X\le s}\uparrow\set{X<t}\) as \(s\uparrow t\), so
the left limit is \(\Prob(X<t)\), and
\(\Prob(X\le t)-\Prob(X<t) = \Prob(X=t)\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-random-variable, def-independence-of-events
#+NAME: def-independence-of-random-variables
#+begin_definition
Random variables \(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\) are /independent/ if for all
Borel sets \(B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\),
\(\Prob(X_{1}\in B_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in B_{n}) = \prod_{i}\Prob(X_{i}\in B_{i})\).
An infinite family is independent if every finite subfamily is. A
sequence is /independent and identically distributed/ if it is
independent and all its members have the same law.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-independence-of-random-variables, prop-measurability-tested-on-rays
#+NAME: prop-functions-of-independent-variables
#+begin_proposition
If \(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\) are independent and
\(g_{1},\dots,g_{n} \colon \R \to \R\) are Borel measurable, then
\(g_{1}(X_{1}),\dots,g_{n}(X_{n})\) are independent.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
For Borel sets \(B_{i}\), the events \(\set{g_{i}(X_{i})\in B_{i}}\) are
\(\set{X_{i}\in g_{i}^{-1}(B_{i})}\), and \(g_{i}^{-1}(B_{i})\) is
Borel; apply the definition of independence to those sets.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Two random variables with the same law need not be equal, or even
defined on the same space, and every theorem below is insensitive to the
difference -- which is why the underlying \(\Omega\) can be left
unspecified. The converse question, whether the law is determined by
\(F_{X}\), is true and is not proved here: it requires the uniqueness of
measures agreeing on the intervals, which is Dynkin's lemma.
#+end_remark
