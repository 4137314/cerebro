#+TITLE: Cauchy's theorem and the Sylow theorems
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:group_theory:
#+MSC: 20D20, 20E07
#+HASH: 06bcb6a9fe78

Lagrange's theorem forbids subgroups of the wrong size and promises
none. The Sylow theorems are the partial converse: for each prime power
dividing the order exactly, subgroups of that size exist, they are all
conjugate, and their number is constrained modulo the prime. All three
proofs are the same technique -- let the group act on a set built for the
purpose and count the orbits.

#+DEPENDS: def-group-action, thm-orbit-stabiliser, prop-orbits-partition
#+NAME: lem-fixed-points-modulo-p
#+begin_lemma
Let a group \(P\) of order \(p^{k}\), \(p\) prime, act on a finite set
\(X\), and let \(X^{P}\) be the set of points fixed by every element of
\(P\). Then \(\abs{X} \equiv \abs{X^{P}} \pmod p\).
#+end_lemma

#+begin_proof
The orbits partition \(X\), and each has size \([P : P_{x}]\), a divisor
of \(p^{k}\). An orbit of size \(1\) is exactly a fixed point; every
other orbit has size a positive power of \(p\), hence divisible by
\(p\). Summing the orbit sizes gives the congruence.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: lem-fixed-points-modulo-p, def-order-of-an-element, def-group-action
#+NAME: thm-cauchy
#+begin_theorem
If a prime \(p\) divides the order of a finite group \(G\), then \(G\)
has an element of order \(p\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(X = \setst{(g_{1},\dots,g_{p}) \in G^{p}}{g_{1}\cdots g_{p} = e}\).
A tuple is determined by its first \(p-1\) entries, the last being
forced, so \(\abs{X} = \abs{G}^{p-1}\), divisible by \(p\).

The cyclic group \(C\) of order \(p\) acts on \(X\) by cyclic shift:
if \(g_{1}\cdots g_{p} = e\) then \(g_{2}\cdots g_{p}g_{1} = g_{1}^{-1}(g_{1}\cdots g_{p})g_{1} = e\),
so the shift maps \(X\) to itself, and shifting \(p\) times is the
identity. By the lemma \(\abs{X^{C}} \equiv \abs{X} \equiv 0 \pmod p\).
A fixed point is a constant tuple \((g,\dots,g)\) with \(g^{p} = e\);
one of them is \((e,\dots,e)\), so there are at least \(p\) of them, and
any other gives an element \(g \ne e\) with \(g^{p} = e\), of order
\(p\) since \(p\) is prime.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-cauchy, def-subgroup, thm-lagrange
#+NAME: def-sylow-subgroup
#+begin_definition
Let \(\abs{G} = p^{k}m\) with \(p\) prime and \(p \nmid m\). A
/Sylow \(p\)-subgroup/ of \(G\) is a subgroup of order \(p^{k}\). A
group whose order is a power of \(p\) is a /\(p\)-group/.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-sylow-subgroup, thm-cauchy, cor-class-equation, thm-quotient-group, prop-image-is-a-subgroup
#+NAME: thm-sylow-existence
#+begin_theorem
Every finite group has a Sylow \(p\)-subgroup, for every prime \(p\)
dividing its order.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Induct on \(\abs{G}\), with \(\abs{G} = p^{k}m\) and \(k \ge 1\).

If some proper subgroup \(H < G\) has index prime to \(p\), then
\(p^{k}\) divides \(\abs{H}\), and by induction \(H\) has a subgroup of
order \(p^{k}\), which serves for \(G\).

Otherwise every proper subgroup has index divisible by \(p\). Let \(G\)
act on itself by conjugation; the class equation reads
\(\abs{G} = \abs{Z(G)} + \sum_{i}[G : C_{G}(a_{i})]\), where each
centraliser \(C_{G}(a_{i})\) is a proper subgroup, so every term of the
sum is divisible by \(p\). Hence \(p\) divides \(\abs{Z(G)}\), and by
Cauchy's theorem \(Z(G)\) contains an element \(z\) of order \(p\). The
subgroup \(N = \langle z\rangle\) is normal, being central, so
\(G/N\) has order \(p^{k-1}m\); by induction it has a subgroup \(S\) of
order \(p^{k-1}\). Its preimage \(H = \pi^{-1}(S)\) under the projection
\(\pi \colon G \to G/N\) is a subgroup, and \(\pi\) restricted to \(H\)
is surjective onto \(S\) with kernel \(N\), so \(H\) is partitioned into
\(\abs{S}\) cosets of \(N\) and \(\abs{H} = p^{k-1}\cdot p = p^{k}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-sylow-existence, lem-fixed-points-modulo-p, thm-orbit-stabiliser, def-coset
#+NAME: thm-sylow-conjugacy-and-count
#+begin_theorem
Let \(\abs{G} = p^{k}m\) with \(p \nmid m\). Then every \(p\)-subgroup
of \(G\) is contained in a Sylow \(p\)-subgroup, any two Sylow
\(p\)-subgroups are conjugate, and their number \(n_{p}\) satisfies
\(n_{p} \equiv 1 \pmod p\) and \(n_{p} \mid m\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Fix a Sylow \(p\)-subgroup \(P\) and let \(Q\) be any \(p\)-subgroup.
Let \(Q\) act by left multiplication on the set \(G/P\) of left cosets,
of size \(m\), which is prime to \(p\). By the fixed-point lemma the
number of fixed cosets is \(\equiv m \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\), so some
\(gP\) is fixed: \(QgP = gP\), that is \(g^{-1}Qg \subseteq P\). Hence
\(Q\) lies in the Sylow subgroup \(gPg^{-1}\). Taking \(Q\) itself
Sylow forces \(Q = gPg^{-1}\) by equality of orders, which proves
conjugacy and the first claim.

So the Sylow \(p\)-subgroups form one orbit under conjugation, and by
the orbit-stabiliser theorem \(n_{p} = [G : N]\) where \(N\) is the
stabiliser of \(P\), a subgroup containing \(P\); hence \(n_{p}\)
divides \([G:P] = m\). Now let \(P\) act by conjugation on the set of
all Sylow \(p\)-subgroups. A fixed point is a \(Q\) normalised by
\(P\); then \(P\) and \(Q\) are both Sylow subgroups of the group
\(QP\), in which \(Q\) is normal, so they are conjugate there and
\(P = Q\). Thus there is exactly one fixed point, and the lemma gives
\(n_{p}\equiv 1 \pmod p\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-sylow-conjugacy-and-count, prop-normality-criteria
#+NAME: cor-unique-sylow-is-normal
#+begin_corollary
A Sylow \(p\)-subgroup is normal if and only if it is the only one; and
a group of order \(pq\) with primes \(p < q\) and \(p \nmid q-1\) is
cyclic.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Conjugates of a Sylow subgroup are Sylow subgroups and all of them arise
this way, so there is one orbit; it is a single point exactly when the
subgroup is normal.

For the second claim, \(n_{q}\) divides \(p\) and is \(\equiv 1 \pmod q\);
as \(p < q\) this forces \(n_{q} = 1\). Likewise \(n_{p}\) divides
\(q\) and is \(\equiv 1 \pmod p\), and \(n_{p} = q\) would give
\(p \mid q - 1\), excluded; so \(n_{p} = 1\). Both Sylow subgroups are
then normal, of coprime prime orders, so they intersect trivially and
their elements commute -- the commutator of \(a \in P\) and \(b \in Q\)
lies in \(P \cap Q\). Hence \(ab\) has order \(pq\) and generates the
group.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The counting constraints are what make the theorems a classification
tool rather than an existence statement: for a group of order \(pq\),
or \(p^{2}q\), the possibilities for \(n_{p}\) are so few that normality
is forced, and a normal subgroup splits the group into smaller pieces --
which is the programme the Jordan-Holder theorem organises.
#+end_remark
