#+TITLE: The wave equation: d'Alembert's formula and energy
#+DATE: [2026-08-11 Tue]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:partial_differential_equations:
#+MSC: 35L05
#+HASH: e3eeb9489f0c

The wave equation is the model hyperbolic equation and is the opposite
of the [[id:ac600204-166d-4fc0-a452-3c3799428283][heat equation]] in every respect: it does not smooth, it runs
equally well in both time directions, and it propagates at finite speed.
All three are visible in the explicit solution on the line, and the
statement that survives in higher dimension is the energy identity,
which gives uniqueness without any formula at all.

#+DEPENDS: def-differentiable-multivariable, cor-transport-equation, thm-chain-rule
#+NAME: def-wave-equation
#+begin_definition
The /wave equation/ with speed \(c>0\) is
\(\partial_{t}^{2}u = c^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}u\). The /Cauchy problem/ on
the line prescribes \(u(x,0) = f(x)\) and \(\partial_{t}u(x,0) = g(x)\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-wave-equation, thm-chain-rule, thm-ftc-first
#+NAME: thm-dalembert
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f\) be \(C^{2}\) and \(g\) be \(C^{1}\) on \(\R\). Then the Cauchy
problem has exactly one \(C^{2}\) solution, namely
\[ u(x,t) = \frac{f(x-ct)+f(x+ct)}{2} + \frac{1}{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct} g(s)\dd s. \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Change coordinates to \(\xi = x-ct\), \(\eta = x+ct\). By the chain
rule the operator factorises:
\(\partial_{t}^{2}-c^{2}\partial_{x}^{2} = -4c^{2}\partial_{\xi}\partial_{\eta}\).
So \(u\) solves the equation exactly when
\(\partial_{\xi}\partial_{\eta}u = 0\), and that holds exactly when
\(u = F(\xi)+G(\eta)\) for \(C^{2}\) functions \(F, G\): integrating
\(\partial_{\xi}(\partial_{\eta}u) = 0\) in \(\xi\) shows
\(\partial_{\eta}u\) is a function \(h(\eta)\) alone, and integrating
that in \(\eta\) gives the stated form.

Imposing the data: \(F(x)+G(x) = f(x)\) and
\(-cF'(x)+cG'(x) = g(x)\). Differentiating the first and solving the
resulting linear system for \(F', G'\), then integrating,
\(F(x) = \frac{f(x)}{2}-\frac{1}{2c}\int_{0}^{x}g + A\) and
\(G(x) = \frac{f(x)}{2}+\frac{1}{2c}\int_{0}^{x}g - A\). Substituting
gives the displayed formula, and the determination of \(F, G\) up to the
cancelling constant is the uniqueness.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-dalembert, def-wave-equation
#+NAME: cor-finite-propagation-speed
#+begin_corollary
The value \(u(x_{0},t_{0})\) depends only on \(f\) at the two points
\(x_{0}\pm ct_{0}\) and on \(g\) on the interval between them -- the
/domain of dependence/. Consequently, if the data vanish on an interval
\(I\), the solution vanishes on the /domain of determinacy/
\(\setst{(x,t)}{[x-ct,x+ct]\subseteq I}\), and a disturbance travels at
speed at most \(c\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Both statements are read off d'Alembert's formula, which involves the
data only at those points; and if \(f\) and \(g\) vanish on
\([x-ct,x+ct]\subseteq I\) then every term vanishes.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-wave-equation, thm-ftc-first, thm-integration-by-parts, thm-fubini
#+NAME: thm-energy-and-uniqueness
#+begin_theorem
Let \(u\) be a \(C^{2}\) solution on \([0,L]\times[0,\infty)\) with
\(u(0,t) = u(L,t) = 0\), and put
\[ E(t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\bigl((\partial_{t}u)^{2} + c^{2}(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\bigr)\dd x. \]
Then \(E\) is constant. Consequently the initial-boundary value problem
has at most one solution.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Differentiating under the integral sign, legitimate since the integrand
is \(C^{1}\) on a compact interval,
\[ E'(t) = \int_{0}^{L}\bigl(\partial_{t}u\,\partial_{t}^{2}u + c^{2}\partial_{x}u\,\partial_{x}\partial_{t}u\bigr)\dd x. \]
Integrating the second term by parts and using the boundary conditions,
which give \(\partial_{t}u = 0\) at \(x = 0, L\), it becomes
\(-c^{2}\int \partial_{t}u\,\partial_{x}^{2}u\). Hence
\(E'(t) = \int\partial_{t}u\,(\partial_{t}^{2}u - c^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}u) = 0\).

For uniqueness, the difference of two solutions has zero data, hence
\(E(0) = 0\), hence \(E \equiv 0\); the integrand being a sum of squares,
both derivatives vanish identically, so the difference is constant, and
it is \(0\) at \(t = 0\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The energy argument is the one that generalises: it needs no formula, no
dimension one and no explicit characteristics, only an integration by
parts, and it is the standard route to uniqueness and to a priori bounds
for hyperbolic problems. Conservation of \(E\) is also Noether's theorem
for the invariance of the wave equation under time translation, which is
the same statement seen from [[id:a7b50bb3-2a00-4d1c-a808-a6fcc1c43f78][the variational side]].
#+end_remark
