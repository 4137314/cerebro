#+TITLE: Constrained extrema and Lagrange multipliers
#+DATE: [2026-08-11 Tue]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26B10, 49K05
#+HASH: d1e9871b0f80

An unconstrained extremum has vanishing gradient; a constrained one has
a gradient that need not vanish but must be normal to the constraint,
because only the directions along the constraint are available to move
in. The multiplier is the coefficient of that normality, and it is
produced -- not postulated -- by the [[id:b6f64ed6-db0e-41c8-a093-0df725e22ecf][implicit function theorem]].

#+DEPENDS: def-differentiable-multivariable, thm-implicit-function, def-linear-map
#+NAME: def-constraint-set
#+begin_definition
Let \(g \colon U \to \R^{m}\) be \(C^{1}\) on an open
\(U \subseteq \R^{n}\), \(m<n\). The set
\(M = \setst{x \in U}{g(x) = 0}\) is a /regular constraint/ at
\(a \in M\) if \(Dg(a)\) has rank \(m\). A point \(a\) is a /local
constrained extremum/ of \(f\) if it is a local extremum of the
restriction \(f\vert_{M}\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-constraint-set, thm-implicit-function, thm-chain-rule
#+NAME: prop-tangent-directions-are-the-kernel
#+begin_proposition
Let \(M\) be a regular constraint at \(a\). Then for every
\(v \in \ker Dg(a)\) there is a \(C^{1}\) curve \(\gamma\) in \(M\) with
\(\gamma(0) = a\) and \(\gamma'(0) = v\); conversely every such curve
has \(\gamma'(0)\in\ker Dg(a)\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
The converse is the chain rule: differentiating \(g(\gamma(t)) = 0\) at
\(t = 0\) gives \(Dg(a)\gamma'(0) = 0\).

For the direct statement, reorder the coordinates so that the last
\(m\) columns of \(Dg(a)\) are independent, and write
\(x = (u,w)\in\R^{n-m}\times\R^{m}\). The implicit function theorem
gives a \(C^{1}\) map \(h\) with \(g(u,h(u)) = 0\) near \(a = (u_{0},w_{0})\)
and \(Dh(u_{0}) = -D_{w}g^{-1}D_{u}g\). Write \(v = (v_{1},v_{2})\); the
condition \(Dg(a)v = 0\) says exactly \(v_{2} = Dh(u_{0})v_{1}\). Then
\(\gamma(t) = (u_{0}+tv_{1},\, h(u_{0}+tv_{1}))\) lies in \(M\), starts
at \(a\), and has \(\gamma'(0) = (v_{1}, Dh(u_{0})v_{1}) = v\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-tangent-directions-are-the-kernel, lem-interior-extremum, thm-annihilator-dimension
#+NAME: thm-lagrange-multipliers
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f, g\) be \(C^{1}\), \(M = \set{g=0}\) regular at \(a\), and let
\(a\) be a local constrained extremum of \(f\). Then there is a unique
\(\lambda \in \R^{m}\) with
\[ Df(a) = \lambda^{\mathsf{T}} Dg(a), \qquad \text{that is} \quad \nabla f(a) = \sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\nabla g_{j}(a). \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(v \in \ker Dg(a)\) and take a curve \(\gamma\) in \(M\) with
\(\gamma'(0) = v\). Then \(t \mapsto f(\gamma(t))\) has a local
extremum at the interior point \(t = 0\), so its derivative vanishes
there; by the chain rule \(Df(a)v = 0\). Hence
\(\ker Dg(a) \subseteq \ker Df(a)\).

Read in the [[id:6f3bed75-1321-4c80-b0bd-c8932e0c2837][dual space]]: \(Df(a)\) annihilates \(\ker Dg(a)\), whose
annihilator is spanned by the rows \(Dg_{1}(a),\dots,Dg_{m}(a)\) -- the
annihilator of the kernel of a linear map is the image of its transpose,
and those rows are independent by regularity, so they span a space of
dimension \(m = n - \dim\ker Dg(a)\). Therefore \(Df(a)\) is a
combination of them, with coefficients unique by independence.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-lagrange-multipliers, cor-extreme-value-on-a-compact-space, cor-heine-borel-in-euclidean-space
#+NAME: cor-extrema-on-a-compact-constraint
#+begin_corollary
If \(M\) is compact, non-empty and regular at each of its points, and
\(f\) is \(C^{1}\) on a neighbourhood of \(M\), then \(f\) attains a
maximum and a minimum on \(M\), and each is attained at a point
satisfying the multiplier equations.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
A continuous function on a non-empty compact set attains its bounds, and
a point where a bound is attained is in particular a local constrained
extremum, so the theorem applies there.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Regularity is a hypothesis about the /description/ of the constraint,
not about the set: the same circle described by \(g = x^{2}+y^{2}-1\) is
regular everywhere, and described by \(g^{2}\) is regular nowhere, while
the geometry is unchanged. When it fails the conclusion can fail, and
the multiplier rule silently returns nothing -- which is why checking the
rank is part of the method and not a formality.
#+end_remark
