#+TITLE: The Lebesgue integral and the convergence theorems
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:measure_theory:
#+MSC: 28A25
#+HASH: 44618aaf3f95

The integral is built in three steps -- indicator functions, then finite
non-negative combinations of them, then suprema -- and each step is
forced by the one before. What the construction buys is the exchange of
limit and integral under hypotheses that are about the functions rather
than about the convergence being uniform, and those three theorems are
the reason the Lebesgue integral replaced the Riemann one.

#+DEPENDS: def-measure, def-measurable-function, def-supremum
#+NAME: def-simple-function
#+begin_definition
A /simple function/ on a measure space is a measurable
\(s \colon X \to [0,\infty)\) taking finitely many values; writing
\(s = \sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\indicator_{A_{k}}\) with the \(A_{k}\)
disjoint measurable and \(c_{k}\) its distinct values, its integral is
\(\int s \dd\mu = \sum_{k}c_{k}\mu(A_{k})\), with the convention
\(0\cdot\infty = 0\). For measurable \(f \ge 0\),
\[ \int f \dd\mu = \sup\Bigl\{\int s\dd\mu \;\Big\vert\; s \text{ simple}, \ 0 \le s \le f\Bigr\}. \]
For a measurable \(E\) one writes
\(\int_{E}f\dd\mu = \int f\,\indicator_{E}\dd\mu\), and \(f\) is
/integrable/ when \(\int\abs{f}\dd\mu<\infty\), in which case
\(\int f = \int f^{+} - \int f^{-}\) with
\(f^{\pm} = \max(\pm f, 0)\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-simple-function, def-measurable-function, prop-measurability-tested-on-rays
#+NAME: thm-approximation-by-simple-functions
#+begin_theorem
For every measurable \(f \ge 0\) there are simple functions
\(s_{1}\le s_{2}\le\cdots\) with \(s_{n}\to f\) pointwise; the
convergence is uniform where \(f\) is bounded.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Put \(s_{n} = \sum_{k=0}^{n2^{n}-1}\frac{k}{2^{n}}\indicator_{A_{n,k}} + n\,\indicator_{\setst{x}{f(x)\ge n}}\),
where \(A_{n,k} = \setst{x}{k2^{-n}\le f(x)<(k+1)2^{-n}}\), a measurable
set. Each \(s_{n}\) is simple, and \(s_{n}\le s_{n+1}\) because
refining the dyadic grid can only raise the approximation from below.
If \(f(x)<\infty\) then for \(n>f(x)\) we have
\(0 \le f(x)-s_{n}(x)<2^{-n}\), and if \(f(x)=\infty\) then
\(s_{n}(x) = n\to\infty\). Where \(f\le M\), the bound \(2^{-n}\) is
uniform for \(n>M\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-simple-function, thm-basic-properties-of-a-measure, thm-approximation-by-simple-functions
#+NAME: thm-monotone-convergence-of-integrals
#+begin_theorem
Let \(0 \le f_{1}\le f_{2}\le\cdots\) be measurable with
\(f_{n}\to f\) pointwise. Then \(\int f_{n}\dd\mu \to \int f\dd\mu\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The integrals increase and are bounded by \(\int f\), since
\(f_{n}\le f\); let \(L\) be their limit, so \(L \le \int f\).

For the converse, fix a simple \(0 \le s \le f\) and \(c \in (0,1)\),
and put \(E_{n} = \setst{x}{f_{n}(x)\ge c\,s(x)}\), a measurable set.
The \(E_{n}\) increase, and their union is \(X\): where \(s(x)=0\) this
is clear, and where \(s(x)>0\) we have \(f(x)\ge s(x)>c\,s(x)\), so
\(f_{n}(x)\) eventually exceeds \(c\,s(x)\). Now
\(\int f_{n} \ge \int_{E_{n}} f_{n} \ge c\int_{E_{n}}s = c\sum_{k}c_{k}\mu(A_{k}\cap E_{n})\),
and by continuity from below each \(\mu(A_{k}\cap E_{n})\to\mu(A_{k})\).
Letting \(n\to\infty\) gives \(L \ge c\int s\dd\mu\), and then
\(c \to 1\) and the supremum over \(s\) give \(L \ge \int f\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-monotone-convergence-of-integrals, thm-approximation-by-simple-functions
#+NAME: cor-additivity-of-the-integral
#+begin_corollary
For measurable \(f,g\ge0\) and \(\lambda \ge 0\),
\(\int(f+g) = \int f + \int g\) and \(\int \lambda f = \lambda\int f\);
and \(f \le g\) implies \(\int f \le \int g\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
For simple functions both identities are finite sums over the common
refinement of the two partitions, using additivity of \(\mu\) on
disjoint sets. In general take increasing simple \(s_{n}\to f\) and
\(t_{n}\to g\); then \(s_{n}+t_{n}\) is simple, increases and converges
to \(f+g\), so monotone convergence applied three times gives the
additivity. Monotonicity is immediate from the definition as a
supremum, every simple function below \(f\) being below \(g\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-monotone-convergence-of-integrals, def-limsup-liminf
#+NAME: lem-fatou
#+begin_lemma
For measurable \(f_{n}\ge0\),
\(\int \liminf_{n} f_{n}\dd\mu \le \liminf_{n}\int f_{n}\dd\mu\).
#+end_lemma

#+begin_proof
Put \(g_{N} = \inf_{n\ge N}f_{n}\), measurable and increasing with
\(g_{N}\to\liminf_{n}f_{n}\). By monotone convergence
\(\int g_{N}\to\int\liminf_{n}f_{n}\). And \(g_{N}\le f_{n}\) for every
\(n \ge N\), so \(\int g_{N}\le\inf_{n\ge N}\int f_{n}\); taking
\(N\to\infty\) on both sides gives the claim.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: lem-fatou, cor-additivity-of-the-integral, def-absolute-value
#+NAME: thm-dominated-convergence
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f_{n}\) be measurable with \(f_{n}\to f\) pointwise and
\(\abs{f_{n}}\le g\) for a measurable \(g\) with
\(\int g\dd\mu<\infty\). Then \(f\) is integrable and
\(\int f_{n}\dd\mu\to\int f\dd\mu\); indeed
\(\int\abs{f_{n}-f}\dd\mu\to0\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
\(f\) is measurable as a pointwise limit and \(\abs{f}\le g\), so
\(\int\abs{f}\le\int g<\infty\). The functions
\(h_{n} = 2g - \abs{f_{n}-f}\) are non-negative, since
\(\abs{f_{n}-f}\le\abs{f_{n}}+\abs{f}\le2g\), and converge pointwise to
\(2g\). Fatou's lemma gives
\(\int 2g \le \liminf_{n}\int(2g-\abs{f_{n}-f}) = \int 2g - \limsup_{n}\int\abs{f_{n}-f}\),
using additivity and the finiteness of \(\int 2g\) to split the
integral. Hence \(\limsup_{n}\int\abs{f_{n}-f}\le0\), so that integral
tends to \(0\), and
\(\abs{\int f_{n}-\int f}\le\int\abs{f_{n}-f}\to0\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Domination is what fails in the standard counterexample -- the moving
spikes \(n\,\indicator_{(0,1/n)}\) converge pointwise to \(0\) with
integral \(1\) throughout -- and Fatou's inequality is exactly what
survives without it. Note also which hypothesis has disappeared compared
with the theorem on integrating a uniform limit: nothing here is
uniform, which is the entire gain.
#+end_remark
