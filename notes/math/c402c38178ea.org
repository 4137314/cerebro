#+TITLE: Orthogonality, Gram-Schmidt and orthogonal decomposition
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:geometry:linear_algebra:
#+MSC: 15A63, 51N20
#+HASH: c402c38178ea

The [[id:3bcfce93-cefa-48a2-b2e8-8f64d2b680cc][Cauchy-Schwarz inequality]] says an [[id:c35a8f01-cbd2-433b-b9b5-a037bae4e91d][inner product]] defines angles;
orthogonality is the case where the angle is right, and it is worth
isolating because it turns a subspace into a direction and a complement
at once. Gram-Schmidt is the statement that the orthogonal viewpoint
costs nothing: every finite-dimensional inner product space already has
an orthonormal basis.

#+DEPENDS: def-inner-product-space, def-span-and-independence
#+NAME: def-orthogonal-family
#+begin_definition
In an inner product space \(V\), vectors \(u, v\) are /orthogonal/ if
\(\inner{u}{v} = 0\). A family is /orthogonal/ if its members are
pairwise orthogonal and non-zero, and /orthonormal/ if in addition
\(\norm{v} = 1\) for each member. For \(S \subseteq V\), the
/orthogonal complement/ is
\(S^{\perp} = \setst{v \in V}{\inner{v}{s} = 0 \text{ for all } s \in S}\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-orthogonal-family
#+NAME: prop-pythagoras
#+begin_proposition
If \(v_{1}, \dots, v_{k}\) are pairwise orthogonal then
\(\norm{v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}}^{2} = \norm{v_{1}}^{2}+\cdots+\norm{v_{k}}^{2}\);
and an orthogonal family is linearly independent.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Expanding \(\inner{\sum_{i}v_{i}}{\sum_{j}v_{j}}\) by bilinearity leaves
only the diagonal terms, the others vanishing by orthogonality. For
independence, apply \(\inner{\cdot}{v_{j}}\) to a relation
\(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}v_{i} = 0\): every term but the \(j\)-th vanishes,
leaving \(\lambda_{j}\norm{v_{j}}^{2} = 0\), and \(v_{j} \ne 0\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-inner-product-space
#+NAME: prop-parallelogram-identity
#+begin_proposition
In an inner product space,
\[ \norm{u - v}^{2} = 2\norm{u}^{2} + 2\norm{v}^{2} - 4\Bigl\lVert \frac{u+v}{2} \Bigr\rVert^{2} \]
for all \(u, v\); equivalently
\(\norm{u+v}^{2} + \norm{u-v}^{2} = 2\norm{u}^{2}+2\norm{v}^{2}\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Bilinearity gives
\(\norm{u \pm v}^{2} = \norm{u}^{2} \pm 2\inner{u}{v} + \norm{v}^{2}\);
adding the two identities cancels the cross terms and yields the second
form, and the first is that form with
\(4\norm{(u+v)/2}^{2} = \norm{u+v}^{2}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-orthogonal-family, prop-pythagoras
#+NAME: thm-gram-schmidt
#+begin_theorem
Let \(v_{1}, v_{2}, \dots\) be a finite or countable linearly
independent family in an inner product space. Then there is an
orthonormal family \(e_{1}, e_{2}, \dots\) with
\(\Span\set{e_{1},\dots,e_{k}} = \Span\set{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}}\) for
every \(k\). In particular every finite-dimensional inner product space
has an orthonormal basis.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Define recursively
\[ w_{k} = v_{k} - \sum_{i<k} \inner{v_{k}}{e_{i}} e_{i}, \qquad e_{k} = w_{k}/\norm{w_{k}}. \]
The vector \(w_{k}\) is non-zero: otherwise \(v_{k}\) would lie in
\(\Span\set{e_{1},\dots,e_{k-1}} = \Span\set{v_{1},\dots,v_{k-1}}\),
contradicting independence. For \(j < k\),
\(\inner{w_{k}}{e_{j}} = \inner{v_{k}}{e_{j}} - \inner{v_{k}}{e_{j}} = 0\)
because the \(e_{i}\) are orthonormal by induction; so \(e_{k}\) is a
unit vector orthogonal to its predecessors. The spans agree at each
stage because \(e_{k}\) is \(v_{k}\) plus a combination of earlier
\(e_{i}\), and conversely. Applied to a basis, this yields an
orthonormal basis.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-gram-schmidt, prop-pythagoras, cor-extension-to-a-basis
#+NAME: thm-orthogonal-decomposition
#+begin_theorem
Let \(U\) be a finite-dimensional subspace of an inner product space
\(V\). Then \(V = U \oplus U^{\perp}\): every \(v \in V\) is uniquely
\(v = u + u'\) with \(u \in U\) and \(u' \in U^{\perp}\), and
\(u = \sum_{i}\inner{v}{e_{i}}e_{i}\) for any orthonormal basis
\(e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\) of \(U\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Take an orthonormal basis of \(U\) by Gram-Schmidt and put
\(u = \sum_{i}\inner{v}{e_{i}}e_{i}\), \(u' = v - u\). For each \(j\),
\(\inner{u'}{e_{j}} = \inner{v}{e_{j}} - \inner{v}{e_{j}} = 0\), so
\(u'\) is orthogonal to every element of \(U\), that is
\(u' \in U^{\perp}\). Uniqueness: if \(u_{1}+u_{1}' = u_{2}+u_{2}'\)
then \(u_{1}-u_{2} = u_{2}'-u_{1}'\) lies in \(U \cap U^{\perp}\), and a
vector orthogonal to itself has \(\norm{v}^{2} = 0\), hence is \(0\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-orthogonal-decomposition
#+NAME: cor-orthogonal-projection-is-nearest
#+begin_corollary
With the notation above, \(u\) is the unique point of \(U\) nearest to
\(v\): \(\norm{v - u} < \norm{v - x}\) for every \(x \in U\) with
\(x \ne u\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
For \(x \in U\), \(v - x = (v-u) + (u-x)\) with \(v - u \in U^{\perp}\)
and \(u - x \in U\), so by Pythagoras
\(\norm{v-x}^{2} = \norm{v-u}^{2} + \norm{u-x}^{2}\), which strictly
exceeds \(\norm{v-u}^{2}\) unless \(x = u\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The corollary is the whole of least-squares approximation and of Fourier
series, stated before either: "best approximation in a subspace" always
means "orthogonal projection onto it", and the coefficients
\(\inner{v}{e_{i}}\) are computed one at a time precisely because the
basis is orthonormal.
#+end_remark
