#+TITLE: Group actions, orbits and the orbit-stabiliser theorem
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:group_theory:
#+MSC: 20B05, 20A05
#+HASH: 84a2fe3c49ae

A group was defined by axioms, but groups arise as symmetries: the
axioms are what the invertible transformations of a set satisfy. An
action is the bridge back -- a homomorphism into a symmetric group -- and
it converts counting problems about a set into arithmetic in the group,
because an orbit is a quotient by a subgroup.

#+DEPENDS: def-group, def-group-homomorphism
#+NAME: def-group-action
#+begin_definition
An /action/ of a group \(G\) on a set \(X\) is a map
\(G \times X \to X\), \((g,x) \mapsto g \cdot x\), such that
\(e \cdot x = x\) and \(g \cdot (h \cdot x) = (gh) \cdot x\) for all
\(g, h \in G\), \(x \in X\). Equivalently, it is a homomorphism
\(G \to \Sym(X)\), the group of bijections of \(X\) under composition.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-group-action, prop-bijective-iff-invertible
#+NAME: prop-action-is-a-homomorphism
#+begin_proposition
The two descriptions in the definition agree: for an action, each map
\(\sigma_{g} \colon x \mapsto g \cdot x\) is a bijection of \(X\), and
\(g \mapsto \sigma_{g}\) is a homomorphism \(G \to \Sym(X)\);
conversely every such homomorphism defines an action.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
From the axioms, \(\sigma_{g} \circ \sigma_{h} = \sigma_{gh}\) and
\(\sigma_{e} = \id_{X}\). Hence
\(\sigma_{g} \circ \sigma_{g^{-1}} = \sigma_{e} = \id_{X}\) and
likewise in the other order, so \(\sigma_{g}\) is a bijection with
inverse \(\sigma_{g^{-1}}\), and \(g \mapsto \sigma_{g}\) is a
homomorphism. Conversely, given a homomorphism \(\rho\), setting
\(g \cdot x = \rho(g)(x)\) satisfies both axioms.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-group-action, def-subgroup
#+NAME: def-orbit-and-stabiliser
#+begin_definition
For an action of \(G\) on \(X\) and \(x \in X\), the /orbit/ is
\(G \cdot x = \setst{g \cdot x}{g \in G}\) and the /stabiliser/ is
\(G_{x} = \setst{g \in G}{g \cdot x = x}\). The action is /transitive/
if there is only one orbit.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-orbit-and-stabiliser, thm-equivalence-partition, prop-subgroup-criterion
#+NAME: prop-orbits-partition
#+begin_proposition
The orbits partition \(X\), and each stabiliser \(G_{x}\) is a subgroup
of \(G\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \(x \approx y\) mean \(y = g \cdot x\) for some \(g\). This is
reflexive (\(e\)), symmetric (apply \(g^{-1}\)) and transitive
(compose), so it is an equivalence relation whose classes are the
orbits; classes partition \(X\). For the stabiliser, \(e \in G_{x}\),
and if \(g, h\) fix \(x\) then \(gh^{-1} \cdot x = g \cdot (h^{-1}\cdot x) = g \cdot x = x\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-orbit-and-stabiliser, prop-cosets-partition, def-index
#+NAME: thm-orbit-stabiliser
#+begin_theorem
For every \(x \in X\) the map \(g G_{x} \mapsto g \cdot x\) is a
bijection from \(G/G_{x}\) onto the orbit \(G \cdot x\). In particular,
for finite \(G\), \(\abs{G \cdot x} = [G : G_{x}] = \abs{G}/\abs{G_{x}}\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The map is well defined and injective at once:
\(g \cdot x = h \cdot x\) if and only if \(h^{-1}g \cdot x = x\), that
is \(h^{-1}g \in G_{x}\), that is \(gG_{x} = hG_{x}\). It is surjective
onto the orbit by definition. The count follows from Lagrange's theorem.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-orbit-stabiliser, prop-orbits-partition
#+NAME: cor-class-equation
#+begin_corollary
Let \(G\) be a finite group acting on itself by conjugation,
\(g \cdot a = gag^{-1}\). Then
\[ \abs{G} = \abs{Z(G)} + \sum_{i} [G : C_{G}(a_{i})], \]
where \(Z(G)\) is the set of elements whose orbit is a single point,
\(C_{G}(a) = \setst{g}{ga = ag}\), and the \(a_{i}\) are
representatives of the orbits with more than one element.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Conjugation is an action: \(e a e^{-1} = a\) and
\(g(hah^{-1})g^{-1} = (gh)a(gh)^{-1}\). Its orbits partition \(G\), so
\(\abs{G}\) is the sum of the orbit sizes. An orbit is a single point
exactly when \(gag^{-1} = a\) for all \(g\), that is when
\(a \in Z(G)\); every other orbit has size \([G : G_{a}]\) by the
orbit-stabiliser theorem, and the stabiliser of \(a\) under conjugation
is \(C_{G}(a)\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-group-action, prop-injective-iff-trivial-kernel, prop-action-is-a-homomorphism
#+NAME: thm-cayley
#+begin_theorem
Every group \(G\) is isomorphic to a subgroup of \(\Sym(G)\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(G\) act on itself by left multiplication, \(g \cdot a = ga\); the
axioms hold by associativity and the identity law. The associated
homomorphism \(\rho \colon G \to \Sym(G)\) has kernel
\(\setst{g}{ga = a \text{ for all } a}\), which is \(\set{e}\) on taking
\(a = e\). So \(\rho\) is injective, and \(G \iso \rho(G) \le \Sym(G)\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Cayley's theorem is not a tool for computation -- the symmetric group it
produces is far too large -- but a statement about what the axioms
capture: nothing is lost by defining a group abstractly, since every
abstract group is a group of symmetries of something. The useful actions
are the small ones, on cosets and on conjugacy classes, and the class
equation is the first result to extract arithmetic from them.
#+end_remark
