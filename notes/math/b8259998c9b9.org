#+TITLE: Differentiability in several variables
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26B05, 26B10
#+HASH: b8259998c9b9

In one variable the derivative can be defined as a limit of quotients
because dividing by \(h\) is available. In several it is not, and the
definition that survives is the one that was really meant all along:
\(f\) is differentiable at \(c\) when it is approximated near \(c\) by a
*linear map*, with an error small compared to the displacement. Partial
derivatives are then a consequence, not the definition -- and the gap
between the two is where every counterexample in the subject lives.

#+DEPENDS: def-derivative, def-metric-space
#+NAME: def-differentiable-multivariable
#+begin_definition
Let \(U \subseteq \R^{n}\) be open, \(f \colon U \to \R^{m}\) and
\(c \in U\). Then \(f\) is /differentiable at/ \(c\) if there is a
linear map \(A \colon \R^{n} \to \R^{m}\) with
\[ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\norm{f(c + h) - f(c) - A h}}{\norm{h}} = 0. \]
The map \(A\) is the /differential/ \(Df(c)\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-differentiable-multivariable
#+NAME: prop-differential-is-unique
#+begin_proposition
There is at most one such \(A\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Suppose \(A\) and \(B\) both work and put \(C = A - B\). For fixed
\(v \ne 0\) and \(t > 0\), taking \(h = tv\) in the difference of the
two limits gives \(\norm{C(tv)}/\norm{tv} \to 0\) as \(t \to 0\); but
by linearity that quotient equals \(\norm{Cv}/\norm{v}\), a constant.
Hence \(Cv = 0\) for every \(v\), so \(C = 0\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-differentiable-multivariable, prop-differentiable-implies-continuous
#+NAME: prop-differentiable-implies-partials
#+begin_proposition
If \(f\) is differentiable at \(c\) then it is continuous at \(c\), and
every directional derivative exists, with
\(\partial_{v} f(c) = Df(c) v\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Write \(f(c+h) - f(c) = Df(c)h + r(h)\) with
\(\norm{r(h)}/\norm{h} \to 0\). Both terms tend to \(0\) as
\(h \to 0\), giving continuity. For a direction \(v \ne 0\), take
\(h = tv\): dividing by \(t\) gives
\((f(c + tv) - f(c))/t = Df(c)v + r(tv)/t\), and
\(\norm{r(tv)/t} = \norm{v} \norm{r(tv)}/\norm{tv} \to 0\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The converse fails, and that is the content of the definition rather
than a defect of it. Every directional derivative of a function can
exist at a point while the function is not even continuous there:
existence of directional derivatives is a family of *one-dimensional*
statements, each blind to what happens off its own line, and
differentiability is a statement about the whole neighbourhood at once.
#+end_remark

#+DEPENDS: prop-differentiable-implies-partials, thm-mean-value
#+NAME: thm-continuous-partials-implies-differentiable
#+begin_theorem
If all partial derivatives of \(f \colon U \to \R\) exist on a
neighbourhood of \(c\) and are continuous at \(c\), then \(f\) is
differentiable at \(c\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Take \(n = 2\); the general case is the same argument with more terms.
Write \(h = (h_{1}, h_{2})\) and split the increment through a corner:
\[ f(c + h) - f(c) = \bigl( f(c_{1}+h_{1}, c_{2}+h_{2}) - f(c_{1}, c_{2}+h_{2}) \bigr) + \bigl( f(c_{1}, c_{2}+h_{2}) - f(c_{1}, c_{2}) \bigr). \]
Each bracket varies one coordinate only, so the mean value theorem in
one variable applies: the first equals
\(\partial_{1} f(\xi_{1}, c_{2}+h_{2}) h_{1}\) and the second
\(\partial_{2} f(c_{1}, \xi_{2}) h_{2}\), with \(\xi_{i}\) between the
endpoints. Subtracting
\(\partial_{1}f(c) h_{1} + \partial_{2}f(c) h_{2}\) and using continuity
of the partials at \(c\), each coefficient differs from its value at
\(c\) by a quantity tending to \(0\) as \(h \to 0\); since
\(\abs{h_{i}} \le \norm{h}\), the whole error divided by \(\norm{h}\)
tends to \(0\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
So continuity of the partials is *sufficient* and not necessary, and
differentiability is strictly between "the partials exist" and "the
partials are continuous". Naming the middle condition is the reason the
linear-map definition is the right one: it is the weakest hypothesis
under which the chain rule holds.
#+end_remark
