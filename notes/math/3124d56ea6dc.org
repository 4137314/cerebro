#+TITLE: Product measures and the theorems of Tonelli and Fubini
#+DATE: [2026-08-11 Tue]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:measure_theory:
#+MSC: 28A35
#+HASH: 3124d56ea6dc

Computing a double integral by integrating one variable at a time is
the most-used manoeuvre in analysis and the one most often performed
without justification. It is a theorem, it is false without hypotheses,
and the hypotheses come in two flavours: non-negativity, which is
Tonelli, and integrability, which is Fubini and is checked /by/ Tonelli.
The proof is a monotone class argument, and that argument is worth
isolating because it is how almost every statement about a
\(\sigma\)-algebra defined by generation gets proved.

#+DEPENDS: def-sigma-algebra, prop-sigma-algebras-are-closed-under-intersection
#+NAME: def-pi-system-and-lambda-system
#+begin_definition
A family \(\mathcal{P}\) of subsets of \(X\) is a /\(\pi\)-system/ if it
is closed under finite intersections. A family \(\mathcal{L}\) is a
/\(\lambda\)-system/ if \(X \in \mathcal{L}\), it is closed under proper
differences (\(A \subseteq B\) in \(\mathcal{L}\) implies
\(B\setminus A \in \mathcal{L}\)), and it is closed under increasing
countable unions.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-pi-system-and-lambda-system, prop-sigma-algebras-are-closed-under-intersection
#+NAME: thm-dynkin
#+begin_theorem
If a \(\lambda\)-system contains a \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{P}\), it
contains \(\sigma(\mathcal{P})\). Consequently two measures that agree
on a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{A}\), and are finite with the
same total mass, agree on \(\mathcal{A}\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
An intersection of \(\lambda\)-systems is one, so let \(\mathcal{L}\) be
the smallest \(\lambda\)-system containing \(\mathcal{P}\); it suffices
to show \(\mathcal{L}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra, since a
\(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{P}\) contains
\(\sigma(\mathcal{P})\), and a \(\lambda\)-system closed under finite
intersections is a \(\sigma\)-algebra -- complements are proper
differences from \(X\), and a countable union is the increasing union of
finite ones, each a complement of an intersection of complements.

So it is enough that \(\mathcal{L}\) be closed under intersections. Fix
\(A \in \mathcal{P}\) and let
\(\mathcal{L}_{A} = \setst{B \in \mathcal{L}}{A \cap B \in \mathcal{L}}\).
It is a \(\lambda\)-system -- the three defining operations commute with
intersecting by \(A\) -- and it contains \(\mathcal{P}\), because
\(\mathcal{P}\) is closed under intersections; by minimality
\(\mathcal{L}_{A} = \mathcal{L}\). Now fix \(B \in \mathcal{L}\) and put
\(\mathcal{L}_{B} = \setst{C\in\mathcal{L}}{B\cap C\in\mathcal{L}}\);
the previous step shows \(\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{B}\), and
\(\mathcal{L}_{B}\) is again a \(\lambda\)-system, so it is all of
\(\mathcal{L}\).

For the uniqueness statement, the sets where two such measures agree
form a \(\lambda\)-system: closure under proper differences and
increasing unions are additivity and continuity from below, and \(X\) is
in it by the equal total mass.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-sigma-algebra, def-measure, def-measurable-function
#+NAME: def-product-measurable-space
#+begin_definition
Given measurable spaces \((X,\mathcal{A})\) and \((Y,\mathcal{B})\), the
/product \(\sigma\)-algebra/ \(\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}\) is
generated by the /rectangles/ \(A \times B\), \(A\in\mathcal{A}\),
\(B\in\mathcal{B}\). For \(E \subseteq X\times Y\) the /sections/ are
\(E_{x} = \setst{y}{(x,y)\in E}\) and \(E^{y} = \setst{x}{(x,y)\in E}\).
A measure is /\(\sigma\)-finite/ if the space is a countable union of
sets of finite measure.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-product-measurable-space, thm-dynkin, thm-basic-properties-of-a-measure
#+NAME: lem-sections-are-measurable
#+begin_lemma
For \(E \in \mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}\), every section \(E_{x}\) is
in \(\mathcal{B}\) and every \(E^{y}\) is in \(\mathcal{A}\). If the
measures are \(\sigma\)-finite, the function
\(x \mapsto \nu(E_{x})\) is \(\mathcal{A}\)-measurable, and
\[ (\mu\otimes\nu)(E) = \int_{X}\nu(E_{x})\dd\mu(x) = \int_{Y}\mu(E^{y})\dd\nu(y) \]
defines a measure on \(\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}\), the unique one
assigning \(\mu(A)\nu(B)\) to a rectangle.
#+end_lemma

#+begin_proof
The sets whose sections are all measurable form a \(\sigma\)-algebra --
sectioning commutes with complements and unions -- and it contains the
rectangles, since \((A\times B)_{x}\) is \(B\) or \(\emptyset\). Hence it
is everything.

Assume first \(\mu,\nu\) finite. Let \(\mathcal{L}\) be the family of
\(E\) for which \(x\mapsto\nu(E_{x})\) is measurable and the two
iterated integrals agree. It contains the rectangles, on which both
integrals give \(\mu(A)\nu(B)\), and the rectangles form a
\(\pi\)-system. It is a \(\lambda\)-system: for \(A \subseteq B\) the
sections satisfy \(\nu((B\setminus A)_{x}) = \nu(B_{x})-\nu(A_{x})\),
finite, so measurability and the equality pass to differences; and for
an increasing union, continuity from below gives
\(\nu((\bigcup_{n}E_{n})_{x}) = \lim_{n}\nu((E_{n})_{x})\), a pointwise
limit of measurable functions, with the integrals converging by monotone
convergence. Dynkin's theorem gives \(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}\).

Countable additivity of the resulting set function is monotone
convergence applied to the sections. The \(\sigma\)-finite case follows
by exhausting \(X\times Y\) with an increasing sequence of rectangles of
finite measure and passing to the limit; uniqueness on rectangles is the
second half of Dynkin's theorem, applied on each piece.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: lem-sections-are-measurable, thm-monotone-convergence-of-integrals, thm-approximation-by-simple-functions
#+NAME: thm-tonelli
#+begin_theorem
Let \(\mu,\nu\) be \(\sigma\)-finite and \(f \ge 0\) measurable on
\(X \times Y\). Then \(x \mapsto \int_{Y}f(x,y)\dd\nu(y)\) is
measurable and
\[ \int_{X\times Y} f \dd(\mu\otimes\nu) = \int_{X}\Bigl(\int_{Y}f(x,y)\dd\nu(y)\Bigr)\dd\mu(x) = \int_{Y}\Bigl(\int_{X}f(x,y)\dd\mu(x)\Bigr)\dd\nu(y). \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
For \(f = \indicator_{E}\) the statement is the lemma. By linearity it
holds for non-negative simple functions. For general \(f \ge 0\) take
simple \(s_{n}\uparrow f\); each inner integral increases to the inner
integral of \(f\) by monotone convergence, so the outer integrands
converge monotonically, and a second application of monotone convergence
transfers the equality to the limit.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-tonelli, thm-dominated-convergence, def-almost-everywhere
#+NAME: thm-fubini
#+begin_theorem
Let \(\mu,\nu\) be \(\sigma\)-finite and \(f\) integrable on
\(X\times Y\). Then \(f(x,\cdot)\) is integrable for almost every
\(x\), the almost-everywhere defined function
\(x \mapsto \int_{Y}f(x,y)\dd\nu(y)\) is integrable, and the two
iterated integrals both equal \(\int f \dd(\mu\otimes\nu)\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Apply Tonelli to \(\abs{f}\): the iterated integral of \(\abs{f}\) is
finite, so \(\int_{Y}\abs{f(x,y)}\dd\nu(y)<\infty\) for almost every
\(x\) -- a non-negative measurable function with finite integral is
finite almost everywhere. Splitting \(f = f^{+}-f^{-}\), Tonelli applies
to each part with finite value, and subtracting the two identities gives
the claim; the difference is defined for almost every \(x\), which is
where both parts are finite.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Integrability is not a technicality that Fubini could do without: for a
function that is not absolutely integrable the two iterated integrals can
both exist and differ. The standard use is therefore always in one
order -- Tonelli on \(\abs{f}\) to check the hypothesis, Fubini on \(f\)
to exchange -- and writing it the other way round is the usual error.

This note also pays part of a debt recorded in [[id:79a8bba8-94c4-44b9-9020-df8e30d12480][probability]]: the
inversion formula for characteristic functions is a Fubini computation,
so one of the two assumed theorems there is now within reach.
#+end_remark
