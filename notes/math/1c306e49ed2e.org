#+TITLE: The Cauchy criterion for series, the harmonic series and condensation
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 40A05
#+HASH: 1c306e49ed2e

The term test says a convergent series has terms tending to zero, and
says nothing about the converse. The harmonic series is the standard
counterexample, and the condensation test is the clean way to see both
it and the whole family \(\sum 1/n^{p}\) at once -- the comparison
objects every later test is calibrated against.

#+DEPENDS: def-series-convergence, thm-cauchy-criterion
#+NAME: thm-series-cauchy-criterion
#+begin_theorem
The series \(\sum_{n \ge 1} a_{n}\) converges if and only if for every
\(\eps > 0\) there is \(N\) such that
\[ \Bigl| \sum_{k=n+1}^{m} a_{k} \Bigr| < \eps \qquad \text{for all } m > n \ge N. \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The displayed quantity is \(\abs{s_{m} - s_{n}}\), where \((s_{N})\) is
the sequence of partial sums. So the condition says exactly that
\((s_{N})\) is a Cauchy sequence, and by the Cauchy criterion for
sequences that is equivalent to its convergence, which is the
definition of the series converging.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-series-cauchy-criterion
#+NAME: prop-harmonic-diverges
#+begin_proposition
The harmonic series \(\sum_{n \ge 1} 1/n\) diverges.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Take \(\eps = 1/2\). For any \(N\), put \(n = N\) and \(m = 2N\): each
of the \(N\) terms of \(\sum_{k=N+1}^{2N} 1/k\) is at least
\(1/(2N)\), so the sum is at least \(N \cdot 1/(2N) = 1/2\). The Cauchy
criterion therefore fails, and the series diverges.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-comparison-test, thm-monotone-convergence
#+NAME: thm-condensation
#+begin_theorem
Let \(a_{1} \ge a_{2} \ge \cdots \ge 0\). Then \(\sum_{n \ge 1} a_{n}\)
converges if and only if \(\sum_{k \ge 0} 2^{k} a_{2^{k}}\) converges.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Write \(s_{N} = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_{n}\) and
\(t_{K} = \sum_{k=0}^{K} 2^{k} a_{2^{k}}\); both are increasing, so each
converges exactly when it is bounded above.

Grouping the terms of \(s\) into blocks
\(a_{2^{k}} + \cdots + a_{2^{k+1}-1}\) of length \(2^{k}\) and using
monotonicity of \((a_{n})\), each block lies between
\(2^{k} a_{2^{k+1}}\) and \(2^{k} a_{2^{k}}\). Summing the upper
estimate over \(k \le K\) gives \(s_{2^{K+1}-1} \le t_{K}\), so \(t\)
bounded implies \(s\) bounded. Summing the lower estimate gives
\[ s_{2^{K+1}-1} \ge \sum_{k=0}^{K} 2^{k} a_{2^{k+1}} = \frac{1}{2} \bigl( t_{K+1} - a_{1} \bigr), \]
so \(s\) bounded implies \(t\) bounded.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-condensation, prop-harmonic-diverges
#+NAME: cor-p-series
#+begin_corollary
For \(p > 0\) the series \(\sum_{n \ge 1} 1/n^{p}\) converges if and
only if \(p > 1\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
The terms are non-negative and decreasing, so condensation applies:
\(2^{k} a_{2^{k}} = 2^{k} 2^{-kp} = (2^{1-p})^{k}\). The condensed
series is geometric with ratio \(2^{1-p}\), which converges exactly
when \(2^{1-p} < 1\), that is \(p > 1\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The harmonic series is the case \(p = 1\), and the proposition above is
the one-line version of it; condensation is the reason \(p = 1\) is the
threshold and not an accident. The blocks in that proof are the blocks
of the classical estimate
\[ 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \Bigl( \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4} \Bigr) + \cdots \ge 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} + \cdots, \]
stated once for every decreasing sequence instead of once per example.
#+end_remark
