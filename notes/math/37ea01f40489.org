#+TITLE: Uniform continuity, extension and the completion of a metric space
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:topology:general_topology:
#+MSC: 54E40, 54E50
#+HASH: 37ea01f40489

The construction of \(\R\) from \(\Q\) by Cauchy sequences is usually
presented as a fact about the real numbers. It is not: the same
construction completes any metric space, and what is special about
\(\Q\) is only that its completion happens to be a field one wants a
name for. Uniform continuity is the notion of map that survives the
construction, and that is why it is the right hypothesis for extension.

#+DEPENDS: def-metric-space, def-uniform-continuity
#+NAME: def-uniform-continuity-in-metric-spaces
#+begin_definition
A map \(f \colon X \to Y\) between metric spaces is /uniformly
continuous/ if for every \(\eps > 0\) there is \(\delta > 0\) with
\(d(f(x),f(x')) < \eps\) whenever \(d(x,x') < \delta\) -- the same
\(\delta\) for every pair. It is an /isometric embedding/ if
\(d(f(x),f(x')) = d(x,x')\) for all \(x, x'\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-uniform-continuity-in-metric-spaces, def-cauchy-sequence, def-metric-convergence
#+NAME: prop-uniform-continuity-preserves-cauchy-sequences
#+begin_proposition
A uniformly continuous map sends Cauchy sequences to Cauchy sequences.
A merely continuous map need not.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \((x_{n})\) be Cauchy and \(\eps > 0\); take \(\delta\) from uniform
continuity and \(N\) with \(d(x_{n},x_{m}) < \delta\) for
\(n,m \ge N\). Then \(d(f(x_{n}),f(x_{m})) < \eps\) for \(n,m \ge N\).
For the second sentence, \(x \mapsto 1/x\) on \((0,1)\) is continuous
and sends the Cauchy sequence \((1/n)\) to \((n)\), which is not Cauchy.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-uniform-continuity-preserves-cauchy-sequences, prop-closure-by-sequences-in-a-metric-space, prop-metric-limits-unique
#+NAME: thm-extension-from-a-dense-subspace
#+begin_theorem
Let \(A\) be dense in a metric space \(X\), let \(Y\) be complete, and
let \(f \colon A \to Y\) be uniformly continuous. Then \(f\) extends to
a uniformly continuous \(F \colon X \to Y\), and to exactly one
continuous map on \(X\). If \(f\) is an isometric embedding, so is
\(F\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
For \(x \in X\) choose \(a_{n} \in A\) with \(a_{n}\to x\); the
sequence is Cauchy, so \((f(a_{n}))\) is Cauchy and converges in \(Y\).
The limit does not depend on the sequence chosen: interleaving two such
sequences gives another sequence converging to \(x\), whose image
converges, so the two limits agree. Define \(F(x)\) to be that limit;
for \(x \in A\) the constant sequence gives \(F(x) = f(x)\).

\(F\) is uniformly continuous: given \(\eps\), take \(\delta\) for
\(\eps/3\) in the uniform continuity of \(f\). If
\(d(x,x') < \delta/3\), choose \(a_{n}\to x\) and \(a_{n}'\to x'\) in
\(A\); for large \(n\), \(d(a_{n},a_{n}') < \delta\), so
\(d(f(a_{n}),f(a_{n}')) \le \eps/3\), and passing to the limit gives
\(d(F(x),F(x')) \le \eps/3 < \eps\).

Uniqueness: two continuous extensions agree on the dense set \(A\), and
each point of \(X\) is a limit of points of \(A\), so they agree
everywhere by sequential continuity and uniqueness of limits. Finally,
if \(f\) preserves distances then so does \(F\), the distance function
being continuous and the identity \(d(F(x),F(x')) = d(x,x')\) passing to
the limit.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-cauchy-sequence, def-metric-space, thm-extension-from-a-dense-subspace, thm-equivalence-partition
#+NAME: thm-completion-of-a-metric-space
#+begin_theorem
Every metric space \(X\) admits a /completion/: a complete metric space
\(\hat{X}\) together with an isometric embedding
\(\iota \colon X \to \hat{X}\) whose image is dense. It is unique up to
a unique isometry commuting with the embeddings.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
*Construction.* Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be the set of Cauchy sequences in
\(X\) and put \((x_{n}) \sim (y_{n})\) when \(d(x_{n},y_{n})\to 0\); this
is an equivalence relation, transitivity following from the triangle
inequality. For Cauchy sequences the real sequence \(d(x_{n},y_{n})\) is
Cauchy, since
\(\abs{d(x_{n},y_{n}) - d(x_{m},y_{m})} \le d(x_{n},x_{m}) + d(y_{n},y_{m})\),
hence convergent; define \(\hat{d}([x_{n}],[y_{n}]) = \lim_{n} d(x_{n},y_{n})\),
which is well defined by the same estimate. Symmetry and the triangle
inequality pass to the limit, and \(\hat{d} = 0\) is exactly the
relation \(\sim\), so \(\hat{d}\) is a metric on
\(\hat{X} = \mathcal{C}/{\sim}\). Let \(\iota(x)\) be the class of the
constant sequence: it is isometric, and its image is dense because
\(\hat{d}([x_{n}], \iota(x_{N})) = \lim_{n} d(x_{n},x_{N})\) is small for
large \(N\).

*Completeness.* Let \((\xi_{k})\) be Cauchy in \(\hat{X}\). By density
pick \(z_{k} \in X\) with \(\hat{d}(\xi_{k},\iota(z_{k})) < 1/k\). Then
\((\iota(z_{k}))\) is Cauchy, so \((z_{k})\) is Cauchy in \(X\) and
defines \(\xi = [z_{k}] \in \hat{X}\). Now
\(\hat{d}(\xi, \iota(z_{k})) = \lim_{j} d(z_{j}, z_{k})\) tends to \(0\)
as \(k \to \infty\), so \(\iota(z_{k}) \to \xi\) and therefore
\(\xi_{k}\to\xi\).

*Uniqueness.* Let \(\iota' \colon X \to \hat{X}'\) be another such
embedding. The isometry \(\iota'\circ\iota^{-1}\), defined on the dense
subspace \(\iota(X)\), is uniformly continuous into the complete space
\(\hat{X}'\), so it extends uniquely to an isometric
\(\Phi \colon \hat{X}\to\hat{X}'\) with \(\Phi\circ\iota = \iota'\).
Symmetrically there is \(\Psi\) the other way, and \(\Psi\circ\Phi\) is
a continuous map fixing the dense set \(\iota(X)\), hence the identity
by the uniqueness clause of the extension theorem; likewise
\(\Phi\circ\Psi\). So \(\Phi\) is a bijective isometry, and it is unique
because it is determined on \(\iota(X)\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The construction never uses anything about \(X\) beyond its metric, so
"the reals are the completion of the rationals" is a special case rather
than a definition -- and the field operations on \(\R\) are then obtained
from the extension theorem, since addition and multiplication are
uniformly continuous on bounded sets. That is also the reason
completeness of \(\R\) is an axiom in this archive and a theorem
elsewhere: which one it is depends on where the construction is entered.
#+end_remark
