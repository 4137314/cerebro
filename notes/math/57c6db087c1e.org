#+TITLE: Noetherian rings and the Hilbert basis theorem
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:commutative_algebra:
#+MSC: 13E05
#+HASH: 57c6db087c1e

Principal ideal domains were shown to admit no infinite ascending chain
of ideals, and that alone gave the existence half of unique
factorisation. Isolating the chain condition -- rather than the
principality -- gives a class of rings closed under quotients and, by
Hilbert's theorem, under adjoining a variable. That closure is why
algebraic geometry can work with finitely many equations.

#+DEPENDS: def-ideal, def-ring, lem-pid-has-no-infinite-ascending-chain
#+NAME: def-noetherian
#+begin_definition
A commutative ring \(R\) is /Noetherian/ if every ideal is finitely
generated: for each ideal \(I\) there are \(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\) with
\(I = a_{1}R + \cdots + a_{k}R\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-noetherian, thm-zorn
#+NAME: thm-noetherian-equivalences
#+begin_theorem
For a commutative ring \(R\) the following are equivalent: (i) \(R\) is
Noetherian; (ii) every ascending chain of ideals
\(I_{1}\subseteq I_{2}\subseteq\cdots\) is eventually constant;
(iii) every non-empty family of ideals has a maximal element.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
(i) implies (ii): the union \(U\) of an ascending chain is an ideal,
hence generated by finitely many elements, all of which lie in some
\(I_{N}\); then \(U = I_{N} = I_{n}\) for \(n \ge N\).

(ii) implies (iii): if a non-empty family had no maximal element, one
could choose, for each of its members, a strictly larger member, and
recursively build a strictly increasing chain -- contradicting (ii).

(iii) implies (i): given an ideal \(I\), let \(\mathcal{F}\) be the
family of finitely generated ideals contained in \(I\), non-empty since
it contains \(0\). Let \(J\) be maximal in \(\mathcal{F}\). If some
\(a \in I \setminus J\) existed, \(J + aR\) would be a finitely
generated ideal inside \(I\) and strictly larger; so \(J = I\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-noetherian, thm-quotient-ring, prop-kernel-is-an-ideal
#+NAME: prop-quotients-are-noetherian
#+begin_proposition
A quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian; so is any homomorphic
image.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Ideals of \(R/I\) are the \(J/I\) for ideals \(J \supseteq I\), by
taking preimages under the surjective projection. If \(J\) is generated
by \(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\) then \(J/I\) is generated by their classes. A
homomorphic image is isomorphic to a quotient by the homomorphism
theorem.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-noetherian, thm-noetherian-equivalences, def-polynomial-ring, thm-polynomial-division
#+NAME: thm-hilbert-basis
#+begin_theorem
If \(R\) is Noetherian then so is \(R[x]\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(I \subseteq R[x]\) be an ideal, and suppose it is not finitely
generated. Choose \(f_{1} \in I \setminus \set{0}\) of least degree,
and recursively \(f_{k+1} \in I \setminus (f_{1},\dots,f_{k})\) of least
degree -- possible, since \(I\) is not generated by finitely many
elements. Let \(a_{k}\) be the leading coefficient of \(f_{k}\) and
\(d_{k} = \deg f_{k}\), so \(d_{1}\le d_{2}\le\cdots\) by the choice of
least degree.

The chain \(a_{1}R \subseteq a_{1}R + a_{2}R \subseteq \cdots\) is
eventually constant, so \(a_{n+1} = \sum_{k \le n} r_{k}a_{k}\) for some
\(n\) and \(r_{k}\in R\). Consider
\(g = f_{n+1} - \sum_{k\le n} r_{k}x^{d_{n+1}-d_{k}}f_{k}\), which lies
in \(I\) and not in \((f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\), since \(f_{n+1}\) does not.
But its coefficient of \(x^{d_{n+1}}\) is
\(a_{n+1} - \sum_{k}r_{k}a_{k} = 0\), so \(\deg g < d_{n+1}\),
contradicting the minimality in the choice of \(f_{n+1}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-hilbert-basis, prop-quotients-are-noetherian, def-integral-domain
#+NAME: cor-finitely-generated-algebras-are-noetherian
#+begin_corollary
For a field \(K\), the ring \(K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\) is Noetherian, and
so is every quotient of it. Consequently any system of polynomial
equations over \(K\) has the same solution set as some finite
subsystem of it.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
A field is Noetherian, having only the ideals \(0\) and \(K\); apply
Hilbert's theorem \(n\) times, and the previous proposition for
quotients. Given a family \(\mathcal{S}\) of polynomials, the ideal it
generates is generated by finitely many polynomials, each a combination
of finitely many members of \(\mathcal{S}\); those finitely many members
generate the same ideal, hence have the same common zeros.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The last sentence is the reason the chain condition matters outside
algebra: an algebraic set is cut out by finitely many equations even
when it is defined by infinitely many, so the objects of algebraic
geometry are finite data. Note also what the proof of Hilbert's theorem
does /not/ do -- it produces no generators, only a contradiction from
their absence, which is characteristic of the whole subject after
Hilbert.
#+end_remark
