#+TITLE: Taylor's theorem with the Peano remainder
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26A24
#+HASH: a7c8cbb56cfd

The Lagrange remainder is an identity with an unknown point in it, and
it asks for one more derivative than the polynomial uses. The Peano
form asks for no extra derivative and gives no identity -- only the
rate at which the error vanishes. That is weaker, and it is the form
every limit computation and every local extremum test actually needs.

#+DEPENDS: def-taylor-polynomial, thm-lhopital, prop-limit-iff-one-sided
#+NAME: thm-taylor-peano
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f\) be \(n\) times differentiable at \(a\), with \(f^{(n-1)}\)
defined on a neighbourhood of \(a\). Then
\[ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - T_{n}(x)}{(x - a)^{n}} = 0, \]
where \(T_{n}\) is the Taylor polynomial of order \(n\) at \(a\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Put \(r(x) = f(x) - T_{n}(x)\) and \(q(x) = (x - a)^{n}\). By
construction \(r^{(k)}(a) = 0\) for \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n\), and
\(q^{(k)}(a) = 0\) for \(k < n\) while \(q^{(n)} \equiv n!\).

Work on one side of \(a\) at a time; the two-sided limit then follows
from the characterisation of a limit by its one-sided limits. On
\((a, a + \delta)\), apply de l'Hopital's rule \(n - 1\) times: at each
stage the numerator \(r^{(k)}\) and the denominator \(q^{(k)}\) both
tend to \(0\) at \(a\), and \(q^{(k+1)}\) is a non-zero multiple of
\((x - a)^{n - k - 1}\), which does not vanish for \(x \ne a\). This
reduces the claim to
\[ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{r^{(n-1)}(x)}{n! \, (x - a)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{1}{n!} \cdot \frac{r^{(n-1)}(x) - r^{(n-1)}(a)}{x - a} = \frac{r^{(n)}(a)}{n!} = 0, \]
using \(r^{(n-1)}(a) = 0\); the middle expression is exactly the
difference quotient defining \(r^{(n)}(a)\), which exists by hypothesis
and vanishes. The argument on \((a - \delta, a)\) is identical.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-taylor-peano, lem-interior-extremum
#+NAME: cor-second-derivative-test
#+begin_corollary
Let \(f\) be twice differentiable at an interior point \(c\) with
\(f'(c) = 0\). If \(f''(c) > 0\) then \(f\) has a strict local minimum
at \(c\); if \(f''(c) < 0\), a strict local maximum.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Suppose \(f''(c) > 0\). The Taylor polynomial of order two at \(c\) is
\(T_{2}(x) = f(c) + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(c) (x - c)^{2}\), since
\(f'(c) = 0\), so the theorem gives a function \(\eta\) with
\(\eta(x) \to 0\) and
\[ f(x) - f(c) = \Bigl( \frac{f''(c)}{2} + \eta(x) \Bigr) (x - c)^{2}. \]
Choose \(\delta > 0\) with \(\abs{\eta(x)} < f''(c)/2\) for
\(0 < \abs{x - c} < \delta\); the bracket is then positive, and
\((x-c)^{2} > 0\), so \(f(x) > f(c)\) on that punctured
neighbourhood. The case \(f''(c) < 0\) follows by applying this to
\(-f\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The test says nothing when \(f''(c) = 0\), and cannot: \(x^{4}\),
\(-x^{4}\) and \(x^{3}\) all have vanishing first and second
derivatives at the origin, with a minimum, a maximum and neither. What
decides those cases is the first non-vanishing higher derivative, and
the Peano form gives that argument verbatim with \(n\) in place of
\(2\).
#+end_remark
