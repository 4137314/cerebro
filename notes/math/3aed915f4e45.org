#+TITLE: Absolute convergence and the comparison test
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 40A05
#+HASH: 3aed915f4e45

Two tools, and the second is the reason the first is worth having: a
series of non-negative terms is a monotone sequence of partial sums, so
its convergence is decided by boundedness alone -- and absolute
convergence reduces the general case to that one.

#+DEPENDS: def-series-convergence, thm-monotone-convergence
#+NAME: thm-comparison-test
#+begin_theorem
Let \(0 \le a_{n} \le b_{n}\) for all \(n\). If
\(\sum_{n \ge 1} b_{n}\) converges then so does
\(\sum_{n \ge 1} a_{n}\); if \(\sum_{n \ge 1} a_{n}\) diverges then so
does \(\sum_{n \ge 1} b_{n}\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Write \(s_{N}\) and \(t_{N}\) for the partial sums of \((a_{n})\) and
\((b_{n})\). Both are increasing, and \(s_{N} \le t_{N}\) for every
\(N\). If \(\sum b_{n}\) converges then \((t_{N})\) is bounded above,
hence so is \((s_{N})\), and an increasing bounded sequence converges by
the monotone convergence theorem. The second statement is the
contrapositive of the first.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-series-convergence
#+NAME: def-absolute-convergence
#+begin_definition
The series \(\sum_{n \ge 1} a_{n}\) /converges absolutely/ if
\(\sum_{n \ge 1} \abs{a_{n}}\) converges.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-absolute-convergence, thm-cauchy-criterion
#+NAME: thm-absolute-convergence
#+begin_theorem
An absolutely convergent series converges.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(s_{N}\) and \(\sigma_{N}\) be the partial sums of \((a_{n})\) and
\((\abs{a_{n}})\). Since \((\sigma_{N})\) converges it is Cauchy, so
given \(\eps > 0\) there is \(N\) with
\(\sigma_{m} - \sigma_{n} < \eps\) for \(m > n \ge N\). By the triangle
inequality,
\[ \abs{s_{m} - s_{n}} = \Bigl| \sum_{k=n+1}^{m} a_{k} \Bigr| \le \sum_{k=n+1}^{m} \abs{a_{k}} = \sigma_{m} - \sigma_{n} < \eps, \]
so \((s_{N})\) is Cauchy and therefore converges.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The converse fails: \(\sum (-1)^{n+1}/n\) converges (its partial sums
interlace) while \(\sum 1/n\) does not. A convergent series that is not
absolutely convergent can be rearranged to sum to anything, which is why
absolute convergence is the hypothesis that appears everywhere later.
#+end_remark
