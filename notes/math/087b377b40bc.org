#+TITLE: The contraction mapping principle
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:general_topology:
#+MSC: 54E45, 34A12
#+HASH: 087b377b40bc

One theorem, proved once, in a complete metric space, and then spent
three times: on the existence and uniqueness of solutions of a
differential equation, on the inverse function theorem, and on the
implicit function theorem. What it says is that a map which shrinks
distances by a fixed factor has exactly one fixed point, and that the
point is the limit of iterating the map from anywhere at all -- so the
proof is also an algorithm.

#+DEPENDS: def-metric-space
#+NAME: def-contraction
#+begin_definition
A map \(T \colon X \to X\) on a metric space is a /contraction/ if
there is \(k < 1\) with \(d(Tx, Ty) \le k \, d(x,y)\) for all
\(x, y \in X\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-contraction, def-metric-convergence, thm-geometric-series, prop-metric-limits-unique
#+NAME: thm-banach-fixed-point
#+begin_theorem
A contraction on a non-empty complete metric space has exactly one
fixed point, and for every \(x_{0}\) the iterates
\(x_{n+1} = T x_{n}\) converge to it.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Fix \(x_{0}\) and put \(x_{n+1} = Tx_{n}\). By induction
\(d(x_{n+1}, x_{n}) \le k^{n} d(x_{1}, x_{0})\), so for \(m > n\) the
triangle inequality and the geometric series give
\[ d(x_{m}, x_{n}) \le \sum_{j=n}^{m-1} k^{j} d(x_{1}, x_{0}) \le \frac{k^{n}}{1-k} d(x_{1}, x_{0}). \]
Since \(k < 1\) this tends to \(0\), so \((x_{n})\) is Cauchy and, by
completeness, converges to some \(x\). A contraction is continuous
(take \(\delta = \eps\)), so \(Tx = \lim T x_{n} = \lim x_{n+1} = x\).
If \(Tx = x\) and \(Ty = y\) then
\(d(x,y) = d(Tx,Ty) \le k \, d(x,y)\), which forces \(d(x,y) = 0\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Completeness is where the fixed point comes from and is not removable:
on \(\Q\) a contraction can have its fixed point at an irrational and
the iterates converge to nothing in the space. That is the same
observation as the Cauchy criterion in the first course, and it is why
this theorem belongs to metric spaces rather than to \(\R^{n}\).
#+end_remark

#+DEPENDS: thm-banach-fixed-point
#+NAME: cor-contraction-error-bound
#+begin_corollary
With the notation above, \(d(x_{n}, x) \le \frac{k^{n}}{1-k} d(x_{1}, x_{0})\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Let \(m \to \infty\) in the displayed estimate of the proof, using that
\(d(\cdot, x_{m}) \to d(\cdot, x)\) by continuity of the distance.
#+end_proof
