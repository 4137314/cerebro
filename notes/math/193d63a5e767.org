#+TITLE: Equicontinuity and the Arzela-Ascoli theorem
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:functional_analysis:
#+MSC: 46E15, 54C35
#+HASH: 193d63a5e767

In a space of functions, closed and bounded is not enough for
compactness: the unit ball of a space of functions is never compact in
infinite dimension. Arzela-Ascoli identifies what has to be added --
uniform control of the modulus of continuity across the whole family --
and it is the compactness criterion that every existence proof by
approximation ultimately uses.

#+DEPENDS: def-uniform-continuity-in-metric-spaces, def-metric-space, def-uniform-convergence
#+NAME: def-equicontinuous
#+begin_definition
Let \(X\) be a metric space and \(\mathcal{F}\) a family of functions
\(X \to \R\). It is /equicontinuous at \(x\)/ if for every \(\eps>0\)
there is \(\delta>0\) with \(\abs{f(y)-f(x)}<\eps\) for all
\(f\in\mathcal{F}\) and all \(y\) with \(d(y,x)<\delta\), and
/uniformly equicontinuous/ if \(\delta\) may be chosen independently of
\(x\). It is /pointwise bounded/ if
\(\setst{f(x)}{f\in\mathcal{F}}\) is bounded for each \(x\), and
/uniformly bounded/ if bounded by one constant.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-equicontinuous, def-totally-bounded, def-metric-convergence
#+NAME: lem-a-compact-metric-space-is-separable
#+begin_lemma
A compact metric space has a countable dense subset.
#+end_lemma

#+begin_proof
For each \(n\), compactness gives a finite set \(D_{n}\) whose
\(1/n\)-balls cover \(X\). The union \(D = \bigcup_{n}D_{n}\) is
countable, and every ball \(B(x,\eps)\) contains a point of
\(D_{n}\) for \(1/n<\eps\), so \(D\) is dense.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: lem-a-compact-metric-space-is-separable, def-equicontinuous, thm-uniform-cauchy-criterion, thm-compactness-in-metric-spaces
#+NAME: thm-arzela-ascoli
#+begin_theorem
Let \(K\) be a compact metric space and \((f_{n})\) a sequence of
functions \(K \to \R\) that is pointwise bounded and equicontinuous at
every point. Then some subsequence converges uniformly on \(K\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(D = \set{x_{1},x_{2},\dots}\) be countable and dense in \(K\). The
sequence \((f_{n}(x_{1}))\) is bounded, so it has a convergent
subsequence; from that subsequence extract another along which
\((f_{n}(x_{2}))\) converges, and so on. The diagonal sequence
\(g_{n} = f_{n_{n}}\) converges at every point of \(D\), being
eventually a subsequence of each extracted sequence.

Now \((g_{n})\) is uniformly Cauchy. Given \(\eps>0\), equicontinuity
gives for each \(x\) a \(\delta_{x}>0\) with
\(\abs{f(y)-f(x)}<\eps/4\) for all \(f\) in the family and
\(d(y,x)<\delta_{x}\). The balls \(B(x,\delta_{x}/2)\) cover \(K\); take
a finite subcover with centres \(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\), and pick
\(z_{k}\in D\cap B(y_{k},\delta_{y_{k}}/2)\), possible by density.
Choose \(N\) with \(\abs{g_{n}(z_{k})-g_{p}(z_{k})}<\eps/4\) for all
\(k\le m\) and \(n,p\ge N\).

For any \(x\in K\), pick \(k\) with \(x \in B(y_{k},\delta_{y_{k}}/2)\);
then \(d(x,z_{k})<\delta_{y_{k}}\) as both are within
\(\delta_{y_{k}}/2\) of \(y_{k}\), so for \(n,p\ge N\),
\(\abs{g_{n}(x)-g_{p}(x)} \le \abs{g_{n}(x)-g_{n}(z_{k})} + \abs{g_{n}(z_{k})-g_{p}(z_{k})} + \abs{g_{p}(z_{k})-g_{p}(x)} < \eps\),
using equicontinuity at \(y_{k}\) twice, with \(x\) and \(z_{k}\) both
in \(B(y_{k},\delta_{y_{k}})\). By the uniform Cauchy criterion
\((g_{n})\) converges uniformly.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-arzela-ascoli, thm-heine-cantor, cor-heine-borel-in-euclidean-space
#+NAME: cor-bounded-derivatives-give-a-convergent-subsequence
#+begin_corollary
Let \((f_{n})\) be differentiable on \([a,b]\) with
\(\abs{f_{n}} \le M\) and \(\abs{f_{n}'}\le L\) for all \(n\). Then some
subsequence converges uniformly.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
The bound on the derivatives gives
\(\abs{f_{n}(y)-f_{n}(x)}\le L\abs{y-x}\) by the mean value theorem, so
the family is uniformly equicontinuous, with \(\delta = \eps/L\); it is
uniformly bounded by \(M\). Arzela-Ascoli applies, \([a,b]\) being
compact.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Equicontinuity is exactly what fails for the sequence
\(f_{n}(x) = \sin(nx)\), which is uniformly bounded with no uniformly
convergent subsequence. Read in reverse, the theorem says that the
compact subsets of the space of continuous functions on a compact space
are the closed, bounded, equicontinuous ones -- the correct
Heine-Borel statement in that setting, and the reason Peano's existence
theorem for differential equations works without any Lipschitz
hypothesis.
#+end_remark
