#+TITLE: Algebra

Algebra studies sets with operations, and it studies them through the
maps that respect the operations rather than through the elements. That
choice is what makes the subject repeat itself: the same three theorems
-- a quotient exists, a homomorphism factors through it, the pieces
reassemble -- are proved once for groups, once for rings, once for
vector spaces, once for modules, with the proofs differing only in what
has to be checked to be well defined.

Part of [[id:c2fcb100-fc5a-4670-9b5d-c068c0c325ff][Mathematics]].

* The subjects

- [[id:8362ccd4-d076-4645-abc2-abb2b4c4fc4d][Group theory]] -- one operation, invertible. The smallest structure
  in which anything can be undone, and the language of symmetry.
- [[id:bc698008-d192-4f92-9822-8e4b05bab125][Rings and commutative algebra]] -- two operations, with the
  interaction between them; ideals, factorisation, and the chain
  conditions.
- [[id:c264ae7e-28f0-4718-8b7f-0ad668d4d719][Field theory]] -- rings in which division is always possible, and the
  extensions that adjoin the roots a polynomial lacks.
- [[id:264810a5-aa5e-4f9b-934b-8e9d286eae4d][Linear algebra]] -- a field acting on an abelian group. The one case
  that is completely understood, and therefore the model for the rest.

* The one theorem, four times

The [[id:da2475a4-40d1-48a3-8f06-cef2ef4ad52a][canonical factorisation of a map]] says every function is a quotient
followed by an injection. Adding structure to the map turns it into the
first isomorphism theorem: [[id:e0e0ff63-6493-4efb-9eab-b96ff4d568ea][for groups]], [[id:f32dae57-e863-4228-b002-ca6db443f05f][for rings]], [[id:63a183cf-d837-4b64-9987-ea0bacf0c0b1][for vector spaces]]
(where it acquires a dimension count) and [[id:cb8c1dfe-ab80-4c47-bcda-ce71f8601866][for modules]]. In each case
the work is not the theorem but the identification of which
substructures can be divided out -- [[id:65032090-bc6b-4a06-a74a-ef155052a6df][normal subgroups]], [[id:f32dae57-e863-4228-b002-ca6db443f05f][two-sided
ideals]], subspaces, submodules -- and that identification is where the
four subjects genuinely differ.

* What the structure buys

- *Counting.* [[id:27ec50b5-1e22-4d41-8edb-d13292004a0f][Lagrange's theorem]] and the [[id:d1a61e90-f2ac-4e62-9cd8-8c742d43fdf2][orbit-stabiliser theorem]] turn
  questions about a finite group into arithmetic, and the [[id:bbbee435-e5f4-4473-8ac8-ab88186ba01b][Sylow
  theorems]] into a classification tool.
- *Factorisation.* [[id:94026fa9-c00a-4e36-845c-cadacd4d94ee][Euclidean domain, principal ideal domain, unique
  factorisation domain]] is a chain of strictly weakening hypotheses, and
  it explains why \(\Z\) and \(K[x]\) behave alike.
- *Finiteness.* The [[id:e423d662-1f19-48be-a1f8-8bdfba79235b][Hilbert basis theorem]] says that adjoining a variable
  preserves the ascending chain condition, so a system of polynomial
  equations is always a finite one.
- *Non-existence.* [[id:06b49b70-2706-41e5-b4e0-612723e3adcf][The tower law]] is a multiplicative constraint on
  degrees, and the classical impossibility proofs are that constraint
  and nothing else.

* What is missing

Galois theory, which is the point at which [[id:68aecb66-5e63-4fa6-bfad-5629a7a343d2][solvable groups]] and
[[id:d61e9b83-1185-46ed-9977-48c786495d24][splitting fields]] -- both present -- become one subject; representation
theory; homological algebra beyond [[id:cb8c1dfe-ab80-4c47-bcda-ce71f8601866][exact sequences]]; the structure
theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain,
which would subsume both the classification of finite abelian groups and
the Jordan normal form; tensor products; and category theory, which the
repetitions above are asking for.
