#+TITLE: Extrema in several variables and the second differential
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26B10, 26B12
#+HASH: 988fbdd36176

The one-variable story -- a vanishing derivative at an interior extremum,
a sign condition on the second derivative to decide which -- survives
verbatim, with the second derivative replaced by a *quadratic form*. The
new phenomenon is that a form can be neither positive nor negative
definite, and the point is then neither a maximum nor a minimum: a
possibility with no one-dimensional analogue.

#+DEPENDS: def-differentiable-multivariable, lem-interior-extremum
#+NAME: prop-stationary-at-an-interior-extremum
#+begin_proposition
If \(f \colon U \to \R\) is differentiable at an interior point \(c\) at
which it has a local extremum, then \(Df(c) = 0\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
For each direction \(v\) the function \(t \mapsto f(c + tv)\) is
defined near \(0\), differentiable there with derivative
\(Df(c)v\), and has a local extremum at \(t = 0\). By the interior
extremum lemma in one variable its derivative vanishes, so
\(Df(c)v = 0\) for every \(v\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-differentiable-multivariable
#+NAME: def-second-differential
#+begin_definition
Let \(f \colon U \to \R\) have continuous second partial derivatives.
Its /second differential/ at \(c\) is the quadratic form
\[ D^{2}f(c)[h] = \sum_{i,j} \partial_{i}\partial_{j} f(c) \, h_{i} h_{j}, \]
which is symmetric in \(i\) and \(j\). The form is /positive definite/
if \(D^{2}f(c)[h] > 0\) for every \(h \ne 0\), /negative definite/ if
the reverse, and /indefinite/ if it takes both signs.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-second-differential, prop-stationary-at-an-interior-extremum, thm-taylor-peano, thm-extreme-value
#+NAME: thm-second-derivative-test-multivariable
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f\) have continuous second partials near a stationary point
\(c\). If \(D^{2}f(c)\) is positive definite then \(c\) is a strict
local minimum; if negative definite, a strict local maximum; if
indefinite, neither.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Suppose \(D^{2}f(c)\) is positive definite. The unit sphere is closed
and bounded, and \(h \mapsto D^{2}f(c)[h]\) is continuous, so by the
extreme value theorem the form attains a minimum \(\mu > 0\) on it;
by homogeneity \(D^{2}f(c)[h] \ge \mu \norm{h}^{2}\) for all \(h\).
Taylor's theorem with the Peano remainder, applied to
\(t \mapsto f(c + th)\) and using \(Df(c) = 0\), gives
\[ f(c + h) - f(c) = \tfrac{1}{2} D^{2}f(c)[h] + \eta(h) \norm{h}^{2}, \qquad \eta(h) \to 0. \]
Choosing \(\norm{h}\) small enough that \(\abs{\eta(h)} < \mu/4\) makes
the right-hand side at least \((\mu/4)\norm{h}^{2} > 0\). The negative
definite case is this applied to \(-f\).

If the form is indefinite, pick \(u\) with \(D^{2}f(c)[u] > 0\) and
\(w\) with \(D^{2}f(c)[w] < 0\). The same expansion along \(tu\) makes
\(f\) exceed \(f(c)\) for small \(t\), and along \(tw\) fall below it,
so \(c\) is neither.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The test says nothing when the form is positive *semi*-definite and
singular, and cannot: the behaviour is then decided by higher-order
terms, exactly as \(f'' (c) = 0\) leaves the one-variable test silent.
What is genuinely new in several variables is the indefinite case --
a saddle -- which is not a failure of the test but a verdict.
#+end_remark
