#+TITLE: Metric spaces and completeness
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:general_topology:
#+MSC: 54E35, 54E45
#+HASH: 43f879a5940c

Every convergence argument of the first course used one thing about
\(\R\): a distance, and the triangle inequality that lets an estimate be
split through an intermediate point. Nothing in those arguments needed
the order, the field structure or the dimension. Abstracting the
distance is therefore not a generalisation for its own sake -- it is the
statement of what was actually used, and it is what makes the same
proofs serve sequences of functions, of matrices, and of points of
\(\R^{n}\).

#+NAME: def-metric-space
#+begin_definition
A /metric space/ is a set \(X\) with a function
\(d \colon X \times X \to \R\) such that for all \(x, y, z \in X\):
\(d(x,y) \ge 0\) with equality exactly when \(x = y\);
\(d(x,y) = d(y,x)\); and
\(d(x,z) \le d(x,y) + d(y,z)\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-metric-space, def-sequence-limit
#+NAME: def-metric-convergence
#+begin_definition
A sequence \((x_{n})\) in a metric space \(X\) /converges/ to
\(x \in X\) if \(d(x_{n}, x) \to 0\) in \(\R\); it is a /Cauchy
sequence/ if for every \(\eps > 0\) there is \(N\) with
\(d(x_{n}, x_{m}) < \eps\) for all \(n, m \ge N\). The space is
/complete/ if every Cauchy sequence in it converges in it.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-metric-convergence, thm-triangle-inequality
#+NAME: prop-metric-limits-unique
#+begin_proposition
A sequence in a metric space has at most one limit, and a convergent
sequence is Cauchy.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
If \(x_{n} \to x\) and \(x_{n} \to y\) then
\(d(x,y) \le d(x, x_{n}) + d(x_{n}, y) \to 0\), so \(d(x,y) = 0\) and
\(x = y\). If \(x_{n} \to x\) and \(\eps > 0\), choose \(N\) with
\(d(x_{n}, x) < \eps/2\) for \(n \ge N\); then
\(d(x_{n}, x_{m}) \le d(x_{n}, x) + d(x, x_{m}) < \eps\) for
\(n, m \ge N\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-metric-space
#+NAME: def-open-set
#+begin_definition
In a metric space \(X\), the /open ball/ of radius \(r > 0\) about
\(x\) is \(B(x,r) = \setst{y \in X}{d(x,y) < r}\). A set
\(U \subseteq X\) is /open/ if every point of \(U\) has an open ball
about it contained in \(U\); a set is /closed/ if its complement is
open.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-open-set, def-metric-convergence
#+NAME: prop-closed-iff-sequentially-closed
#+begin_proposition
A set \(C\) in a metric space is closed if and only if the limit of
every convergent sequence of points of \(C\) lies in \(C\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \(C\) be closed, \(x_{n} \in C\) with \(x_{n} \to x\), and suppose
\(x \notin C\). Then \(x\) lies in the open complement, so some
\(B(x,r)\) misses \(C\) entirely -- contradicting
\(d(x_{n}, x) \to 0\). Conversely, if \(C\) is not closed its
complement is not open, so some \(x \notin C\) has every \(B(x, 1/n)\)
meeting \(C\); picking \(x_{n} \in C \cap B(x, 1/n)\) gives a sequence
in \(C\) converging to \(x \notin C\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-metric-convergence, thm-cauchy-criterion
#+NAME: thm-euclidean-space-complete
#+begin_theorem
With \(d(x,y) = \norm{x - y}\), the space \(\R^{n}\) is complete.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Write \(x^{(k)}\) for a Cauchy sequence in \(\R^{n}\) and
\(x^{(k)}_{i}\) for its \(i\)-th coordinate. Since
\(\abs{x^{(k)}_{i} - x^{(m)}_{i}} \le \norm{x^{(k)} - x^{(m)}}\), each
coordinate sequence is Cauchy in \(\R\), hence converges to some
\(x_{i}\) by the Cauchy criterion. Put \(x = (x_{1}, \dots, x_{n})\).
Given \(\eps > 0\), choosing \(K\) so that each coordinate is within
\(\eps/\sqrt{n}\) of its limit for \(k \ge K\) gives
\(\norm{x^{(k)} - x} < \eps\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Completeness is a property of the *metric*, not of the set: \(\Q\) and
\(\R\) carry the same formula for \(d\) and only one of them is
complete. That is the whole reason the Cauchy criterion was worth
isolating in the first course -- it is the hypothesis under which "the
terms bunch together" is allowed to mean "the terms converge".
#+end_remark
