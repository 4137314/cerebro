#+TITLE: Hamilton's equations
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:mathematical_physics:classical_mechanics:
#+MSC: 70H05, 37J05
#+HASH: 74f387405d84

The Euler-Lagrange equations are \(n\) equations of second order on
configuration space. Legendre-transforming in the velocities replaces
them by \(2n\) equations of *first* order on phase space, and the gain is
structural rather than notational: a first-order system is a vector
field, its solutions are its flow, and the geometry of that flow is what
the rest of the subject is about.

#+DEPENDS: def-legendre-transform, thm-legendre-stationary-point, def-conjugate-momentum
#+NAME: def-hamiltonian
#+begin_definition
Let \(L(q, \dot q, t)\) be strictly convex in \(\dot q\). The
/Hamiltonian/ is the Legendre transform of \(L\) in the velocities,
\[ H(q, p, t) = \sup_{\dot q} \bigl( p \cdot \dot q - L(q, \dot q, t) \bigr) = p \cdot \dot q - L(q, \dot q, t), \]
the last expression evaluated at the unique \(\dot q\) with
\(p = \partial L / \partial \dot q\). The pair \((q,p)\) ranges over
/phase space/.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-hamiltonian, thm-euler-lagrange, thm-legendre-stationary-point, thm-derivative-algebra
#+NAME: thm-hamilton-equations
#+begin_theorem
A path satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equations for \(L\) if and only if
the corresponding path in phase space satisfies
\[ \dot q_{i} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}, \qquad \dot p_{i} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}}, \qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}. \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Differentiate \(H = p \cdot \dot q - L\) treating \(\dot q\) as the
function of \((q,p,t)\) determined by \(p = \partial L/\partial \dot q\).
In the \(p_{i}\) direction,
\[ \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}} = \dot q_{i} + p \cdot \frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial p_{i}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} \cdot \frac{\partial \dot q}{\partial p_{i}} = \dot q_{i}, \]
the last two terms cancelling by the defining relation -- this is the
Legendre transform's derivative identity. In the \(q_{i}\) direction the
same cancellation leaves
\(\partial H/\partial q_{i} = -\partial L/\partial q_{i}\), and the
Euler-Lagrange equation says \(\partial L / \partial q_{i} = \dot p_{i}\).
The \(t\) statement is the same computation in the remaining variable.
Every step is reversible, since the Legendre transform is invertible
under strict convexity.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-hamilton-equations, cor-vanishing-derivative
#+NAME: prop-hamiltonian-conserved-when-time-independent
#+begin_proposition
If \(H\) does not depend explicitly on \(t\), it is constant along every
solution.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Along a solution, the chain rule and Hamilton's equations give
\[ \frac{\dd H}{\dd t} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}} \dot q_{i} + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}} \dot p_{i} + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}} \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}} \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}} + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}, \]
summed over \(i\); the first two terms cancel identically. If
\(\partial H/\partial t = 0\) the derivative vanishes, so \(H\) is
constant.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The cancellation above is not an accident of the coordinates: it is the
antisymmetry of the bracket that Hamilton's equations define, and it is
why the flow preserves \(H\) whatever \(H\) is. Conservation of energy
is the special case where the conserved quantity is the generator of the
flow itself.
#+end_remark
