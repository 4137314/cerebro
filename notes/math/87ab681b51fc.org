#+TITLE: One-sided limits and the discontinuities of a monotone function
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26A15, 26A48
#+HASH: 87ab681b51fc

A limit either exists or it does not, and the two-sided definition
gives no vocabulary for /how/ it fails. Splitting it in two supplies
that vocabulary, and it immediately pays: a monotone function turns out
to have both one-sided limits everywhere, so the only discontinuity it
can have is a jump.

#+DEPENDS: def-function-limit
#+NAME: def-one-sided-limit
#+begin_definition
Let \(f \colon D \to \R\) and let \(c\) be an accumulation point of
\(D \cap (c, \infty)\). One writes \(\lim_{x \to c^{+}} f(x) = L\), the
/right-hand limit/, if for every \(\eps > 0\) there is \(\delta > 0\)
with \(\abs{f(x) - L} < \eps\) for every \(x \in D\) satisfying
\(c < x < c + \delta\). The /left-hand limit/
\(\lim_{x \to c^{-}} f(x)\) is defined symmetrically, on
\(c - \delta < x < c\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-one-sided-limit, def-function-limit
#+NAME: prop-limit-iff-one-sided
#+begin_proposition
Let \(c\) be an accumulation point of \(D\) from both sides. Then
\(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L\) if and only if both one-sided limits exist
and equal \(L\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
If the two-sided limit is \(L\), the \(\delta\) it supplies works for
each side, since each side's condition is a restriction of
\(0 < \abs{x - c} < \delta\). Conversely, given \(\eps > 0\) take
\(\delta_{+}\) and \(\delta_{-}\) from the two one-sided limits and put
\(\delta = \min(\delta_{+}, \delta_{-})\). Any \(x\) with
\(0 < \abs{x - c} < \delta\) lies strictly on one side of \(c\), so it
satisfies that side's estimate.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-one-sided-limit, ax-completeness, prop-supremum-characterisation
#+NAME: thm-monotone-one-sided
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f \colon (a,b) \to \R\) be increasing. Then both one-sided limits
exist at every \(c \in (a,b)\), and
\[ \lim_{x \to c^{-}} f(x) = \sup_{x < c} f(x) \le f(c) \le \inf_{x > c} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^{+}} f(x). \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The set \(\setst{f(x)}{a < x < c}\) is non-empty and bounded above by
\(f(c)\), so \(s = \sup_{x < c} f(x)\) exists by completeness and
\(s \le f(c)\). Let \(\eps > 0\). By the characterisation of the
supremum there is \(x_{0} < c\) with \(f(x_{0}) > s - \eps\), and for
every \(x\) with \(x_{0} < x < c\) monotonicity gives
\(s - \eps < f(x_{0}) \le f(x) \le s\). Taking
\(\delta = c - x_{0}\) shows \(\lim_{x \to c^{-}} f(x) = s\). The
right-hand statement is the same argument applied to the infimum.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-monotone-one-sided, prop-limit-iff-one-sided
#+NAME: cor-monotone-jump-discontinuities
#+begin_corollary
An increasing \(f \colon (a,b) \to \R\) is continuous at \(c\) if and
only if \(\sup_{x < c} f(x) = \inf_{x > c} f(x)\). Every discontinuity
of \(f\) is thus a /jump/: both one-sided limits exist and differ.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
By the theorem the two one-sided limits exist and are the stated
supremum and infimum, and they bracket \(f(c)\). By the proposition,
continuity at \(c\) is the existence of the two-sided limit together
with its equalling \(f(c)\), which happens exactly when the two agree
-- and then, being squeezed around \(f(c)\), they agree with it.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The corollary is the reason a monotone function has at most countably
many discontinuities: each jump contains a distinct rational, and the
rationals are countable. That argument needs the density of the
rationals and nothing else about \(f\), which is a fair summary of how
much structure monotonicity alone carries.
#+end_remark
