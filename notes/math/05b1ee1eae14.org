#+TITLE: The limit of a function and its sequential characterisation
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26A03
#+HASH: 05b1ee1eae14

The limit of a function is the same idea as the limit of a sequence with
the index replaced by a continuous variable, and the proposition below
says so precisely. It is the bridge that lets every theorem about
sequences be spent on functions.

#+NAME: def-accumulation-point
#+begin_definition
Let \(D \subseteq \R\). A point \(c \in \R\) is an /accumulation point/
of \(D\) if every interval \((c - \delta, c + \delta)\) with
\(\delta > 0\) contains a point of \(D\) other than \(c\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-accumulation-point
#+NAME: def-function-limit
#+begin_definition
Let \(f \colon D \to \R\) and let \(c\) be an accumulation point of
\(D\). One writes \(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L\) if for every \(\eps > 0\)
there is \(\delta > 0\) such that \(\abs{f(x) - L} < \eps\) for every
\(x \in D\) with \(0 < \abs{x - c} < \delta\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-function-limit, def-sequence-limit
#+NAME: prop-sequential-limit
#+begin_proposition
Let \(f \colon D \to \R\) and let \(c\) be an accumulation point of
\(D\). Then \(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L\) if and only if
\(f(x_{n}) \to L\) for every sequence \((x_{n})\) in
\(D \setminus \set{c}\) with \(x_{n} \to c\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Suppose the limit is \(L\) and let \(x_{n} \to c\) with
\(x_{n} \in D \setminus \set{c}\). Given \(\eps > 0\), take \(\delta\)
from the definition and then \(N\) with \(\abs{x_{n} - c} < \delta\) for
\(n \ge N\); since \(x_{n} \ne c\), this gives
\(\abs{f(x_{n}) - L} < \eps\) for \(n \ge N\).

Conversely, suppose the limit is not \(L\). Then some \(\eps > 0\)
admits no \(\delta\), so for every \(n\) the choice
\(\delta = 1/n\) produces \(x_{n} \in D\) with
\(0 < \abs{x_{n} - c} < 1/n\) and \(\abs{f(x_{n}) - L} \ge \eps\). This
sequence lies in \(D \setminus \set{c}\) and converges to \(c\), while
\(f(x_{n})\) does not converge to \(L\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The second half is the useful one in practice: to show a limit does /not/
exist, produce two sequences approaching \(c\) along which \(f\) tends to
different values.
#+end_remark
