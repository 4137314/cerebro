#+TITLE: Isometries of Euclidean space and the orthogonal group
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:geometry:group_theory:
#+MSC: 51F25, 20H15
#+HASH: 55069ec656e9

Euclidean geometry can be defined as the study of what is preserved by
distance-preserving maps, which presupposes knowing what those maps are.
The answer is as rigid as possible: a map that only preserves distances,
with no linearity assumed anywhere, is automatically a linear orthogonal
map followed by a translation. Nothing else can happen, and the proof is
the polarisation identity.

#+DEPENDS: def-inner-product-space, def-metric-space
#+NAME: def-euclidean-isometry
#+begin_definition
Let \(E = \R^{n}\) with the standard inner product and the metric
\(d(x,y) = \norm{x-y}\). An /isometry/ of \(E\) is a map
\(f \colon E \to E\) with \(d(f(x), f(y)) = d(x,y)\) for all \(x, y\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-euclidean-isometry, prop-polarisation
#+NAME: prop-isometry-preserves-the-inner-product
#+begin_proposition
If \(f\) is an isometry with \(f(0) = 0\), then
\(\inner{f(x)}{f(y)} = \inner{x}{y}\) for all \(x,y\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Taking \(y = 0\) gives \(\norm{f(x)} = \norm{x}\). Expanding
\(\norm{f(x)-f(y)}^{2} = \norm{x-y}^{2}\) by bilinearity,
\[ \norm{f(x)}^{2} - 2\inner{f(x)}{f(y)} + \norm{f(y)}^{2} = \norm{x}^{2} - 2\inner{x}{y} + \norm{y}^{2}, \]
and the norm terms cancel in pairs, leaving
\(\inner{f(x)}{f(y)} = \inner{x}{y}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-isometry-preserves-the-inner-product, thm-gram-schmidt, prop-pythagoras
#+NAME: thm-isometry-fixing-the-origin-is-linear
#+begin_theorem
An isometry of \(\R^{n}\) fixing the origin is linear.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\) be the standard orthonormal basis. By the
previous proposition the vectors \(f(e_{i})\) are orthonormal, hence
independent, hence a basis of \(\R^{n}\). For any \(x\) and each \(i\),
\(\inner{f(x)}{f(e_{i})} = \inner{x}{e_{i}}\), so expanding \(f(x)\) in
the orthonormal basis \((f(e_{i}))\) gives
\[ f(x) = \sum_{i} \inner{f(x)}{f(e_{i})}\, f(e_{i}) = \sum_{i} \inner{x}{e_{i}}\, f(e_{i}). \]
The right-hand side is linear in \(x\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-isometry-fixing-the-origin-is-linear, def-group, prop-subgroup-criterion
#+NAME: def-orthogonal-group
#+begin_definition
The /orthogonal group/ \(\Orth(n)\) is the set of linear maps
\(A \colon \R^{n}\to\R^{n}\) with \(\inner{Ax}{Ay} = \inner{x}{y}\) for
all \(x,y\), equivalently those whose matrix satisfies
\(A^{\mathsf{T}}A = I\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-orthogonal-group, prop-subgroup-criterion, cor-injective-iff-surjective-in-finite-dimension
#+NAME: prop-orthogonal-group-is-a-group
#+begin_proposition
\(\Orth(n)\) is a group under composition.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
The identity preserves the inner product. A composition of two such
maps does. Each \(A \in \Orth(n)\) is injective, since \(Ax = 0\) gives
\(\norm{x}^{2} = \inner{Ax}{Ax} = 0\), hence bijective in finite
dimension, and its inverse preserves the inner product because
\(\inner{A^{-1}u}{A^{-1}v} = \inner{A A^{-1}u}{A A^{-1}v} = \inner{u}{v}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-isometry-fixing-the-origin-is-linear, prop-orthogonal-group-is-a-group, def-affine-map
#+NAME: thm-classification-of-euclidean-isometries
#+begin_theorem
Every isometry of \(\R^{n}\) has the form \(f(x) = Ax + b\) with
\(A \in \Orth(n)\) and \(b \in \R^{n}\); conversely every such map is an
isometry. The isometries form a group, and \(f \mapsto A\) is a
surjective homomorphism onto \(\Orth(n)\) whose kernel is the group of
translations.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Given an isometry \(f\), put \(b = f(0)\) and \(g(x) = f(x) - b\). Then
\(g\) is an isometry fixing the origin, hence linear by the theorem
above, and it preserves the inner product, so \(g = A \in \Orth(n)\) and
\(f(x) = Ax + b\). Conversely
\(\norm{(Ax+b)-(Ay+b)} = \norm{A(x-y)} = \norm{x-y}\).

Composition gives \((A,b)(A',b') = (AA', Ab'+b)\), so the isometries are
closed under composition and inverses -- the inverse of \((A,b)\) being
\((A^{-1}, -A^{-1}b)\) -- and \(f \mapsto A\) is a homomorphism, clearly
surjective, whose kernel consists of the maps \(x \mapsto x + b\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
So an isometry is an affine map whose linear part is orthogonal, and the
group is a semidirect product of translations by \(\Orth(n)\). The
subgroup \(\SO(n)\) of orthogonal maps of determinant \(1\) is what
separates the isometries that can be reached continuously from the
identity -- rotations -- from the reflections, and it is the first place
where a geometric question is answered by a connectedness argument.
#+end_remark
