#+TITLE: Projective transformations and the cross-ratio
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:geometry:algebraic_geometry:
#+MSC: 51N15
#+HASH: 58d7e7556448

A projective transformation can move any three points of a line to any
other three, so no function of three points is invariant. Four points
have exactly one invariant, the cross-ratio, and it is therefore the
only quantity projective geometry can measure -- which is precisely why
it is the quantity that perspective drawings preserve.

#+DEPENDS: def-projective-space, def-linear-map, cor-injective-iff-surjective-in-finite-dimension
#+NAME: def-projective-transformation
#+begin_definition
Let \(V\) be a vector space over \(K\). An invertible linear
\(A \colon V \to V\) induces a map \(\PP(A)\colon \PP(V)\to\PP(V)\),
\([v]\mapsto[Av]\); such maps are the /projective transformations/, and
they form a group \(\PGL(V)\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-projective-transformation, prop-projective-space-is-a-quotient
#+NAME: prop-when-two-matrices-agree
#+begin_proposition
\(\PP(A)\) is well defined and bijective, \(\PP(AB) = \PP(A)\PP(B)\),
and \(\PP(A) = \PP(B)\) if and only if \(B = \lambda A\) for some
\(\lambda \ne 0\). Hence \(\PGL(V) \iso \GL(V)/K^{\times}\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Well-definedness: \(A(\lambda v) = \lambda Av\), and \(Av \ne 0\) for
\(v \ne 0\) by invertibility. Functoriality is immediate, and
\(\PP(A^{-1})\) inverts \(\PP(A)\).

If \(B = \lambda A\) the induced maps agree. Conversely, suppose they
agree: for every \(v \ne 0\) there is \(\mu(v)\) with
\(Bv = \mu(v)Av\). For independent \(v, w\),
\(\mu(v+w)(Av+Aw) = B(v+w) = \mu(v)Av+\mu(w)Aw\), and \(Av, Aw\) are
independent, so \(\mu(v) = \mu(v+w) = \mu(w)\). For dependent non-zero
\(v,w\) the values agree because \(\mu(\lambda v) = \mu(v)\). So
\(\mu\) is a constant \(\lambda\), and \(B = \lambda A\). The last
statement is the homomorphism \(\GL(V)\to\PGL(V)\), surjective by
definition with kernel the non-zero scalars.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-projective-transformation, cor-extension-to-a-basis, prop-basis-gives-unique-coordinates
#+NAME: thm-three-transitivity-on-a-line
#+begin_theorem
Given two triples of pairwise distinct points of \(\PP^{1}(K)\), there
is exactly one projective transformation carrying the first to the
second, in order.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
It suffices to send an arbitrary triple \((P_{0},P_{1},P_{2})\) to
\(([1:0],[0:1],[1:1])\), uniquely. Choose representatives
\(v_{0},v_{1}\) of \(P_{0},P_{1}\); they are independent, since the
points differ, hence a basis of \(K^{2}\). A representative \(v_{2}\) of
\(P_{2}\) is \(\alpha v_{0}+\beta v_{1}\) with \(\alpha,\beta\ne0\),
because \(P_{2}\) differs from the first two. The linear map sending
\(\alpha v_{0}\mapsto e_{0}\) and \(\beta v_{1}\mapsto e_{1}\) is
invertible and carries the triple to the required one.

Uniqueness: if \(\PP(A)\) fixes the three standard points, then
\(Ae_{0} = \lambda e_{0}\), \(Ae_{1} = \mu e_{1}\) and
\(A(e_{0}+e_{1}) = \nu(e_{0}+e_{1})\), so \(\lambda = \nu = \mu\) and
\(A\) is a scalar, that is \(\PP(A) = \id\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-three-transitivity-on-a-line, prop-affine-charts
#+NAME: def-cross-ratio
#+begin_definition
For four pairwise distinct points \(P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},P_{4}\) of
\(\PP^{1}(K)\), the /cross-ratio/ \((P_{1},P_{2};P_{3},P_{4})\) is the
image of \(P_{4}\) under the unique projective transformation sending
\(P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\) to \([0:1],[1:0],[1:1]\), read in the affine
coordinate \(z = x_{0}/x_{1}\) -- so that the three reference points
become \(0\), \(\infty\) and \(1\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-cross-ratio, thm-three-transitivity-on-a-line
#+NAME: thm-cross-ratio-is-the-invariant
#+begin_theorem
The cross-ratio is invariant under every projective transformation, and
in the affine coordinate it is
\[ (z_{1},z_{2};z_{3},z_{4}) = \frac{(z_{4}-z_{1})(z_{3}-z_{2})}{(z_{4}-z_{2})(z_{3}-z_{1})}. \]
Two quadruples of distinct points are related by a projective
transformation if and only if they have the same cross-ratio.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(g\) be a projective transformation and \(h\) the unique one
sending \(P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\) to the standard triple. Then
\(h g^{-1}\) sends \(gP_{1},gP_{2},gP_{3}\) to the standard triple, so
it is the transformation defining the cross-ratio of the image
quadruple, whose value is \(hg^{-1}(gP_{4}) = h(P_{4})\) -- the same as
before. This is invariance.

For the formula, the map
\(z \mapsto \frac{(z-z_{1})(z_{3}-z_{2})}{(z-z_{2})(z_{3}-z_{1})}\) is
induced by an invertible matrix -- its determinant is a non-zero
multiple of \((z_{1}-z_{2})(z_{3}-z_{1})(z_{3}-z_{2})\) -- and it sends
\(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\) to \(0,\infty,1\), that is to
\([0:1],[1:0],[1:1]\); by uniqueness it is the transformation of the
definition, and evaluating it at \(z_{4}\) gives the displayed
expression.

Finally, if two quadruples have the same cross-ratio, composing the two
normalising transformations carries one to the other; the converse is
invariance.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Over \(K = \R\) the cross-ratio explains why a photograph of a railway
distorts every length and yet determines the geometry: lengths and
ratios of lengths are not projective invariants, but the ratio of ratios
is. Over \(K = \C\) the same formula, applied to points of the Riemann
sphere, is what makes the cross-ratio real exactly for concyclic
quadruples -- the projective statement of a classical theorem in
circles.
#+end_remark
