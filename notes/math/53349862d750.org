#+TITLE: Extreme points and Minkowski's theorem on convex bodies
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:geometry:convex_geometry:
#+MSC: 52A20, 52A05
#+HASH: 53349862d750

A compact convex set is determined by very few of its points: the ones
that lie on no segment inside it. Minkowski's theorem says those points
suffice to rebuild the whole set by taking convex combinations, which is
why a linear function on a polytope is optimised at a vertex -- the
statement linear programming is built on.

#+DEPENDS: def-convex-set, def-convex-hull
#+NAME: def-extreme-point
#+begin_definition
Let \(C\) be a convex subset of \(\R^{n}\). A point \(x \in C\) is
/extreme/ if \(x = (1-t)u + tv\) with \(u,v \in C\) and \(t\in(0,1)\)
forces \(u = v = x\). The set of extreme points is written
\(\operatorname{ext} C\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-extreme-point, cor-supporting-hyperplane, cor-extreme-value-on-a-compact-space
#+NAME: prop-a-supporting-face-contains-an-extreme-point
#+begin_proposition
Let \(C \subseteq \R^{n}\) be compact, convex and non-empty, and
\(u \ne 0\). Then the /face/
\(F = \setst{x\in C}{\inner{u}{x} = \max_{y\in C}\inner{u}{y}}\) is
non-empty, compact and convex, and every extreme point of \(F\) is an
extreme point of \(C\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
The linear function \(x \mapsto \inner{u}{x}\) is continuous on the
compact \(C\), so it attains its maximum \(\mu\); hence \(F\) is
non-empty, and it is closed in \(C\), hence compact, and convex as the
intersection of \(C\) with a hyperplane.

Let \(x\) be extreme in \(F\) and suppose \(x = (1-t)y+tz\) with
\(y,z\in C\), \(t\in(0,1)\). Then
\(\mu = \inner{u}{x} = (1-t)\inner{u}{y}+t\inner{u}{z}\) with both terms
at most \(\mu\), which forces \(\inner{u}{y} = \inner{u}{z} = \mu\), so
\(y,z \in F\); extremality in \(F\) gives \(y = z = x\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-a-supporting-face-contains-an-extreme-point, def-extreme-point, thm-separating-hyperplane
#+NAME: thm-minkowski
#+begin_theorem
Every non-empty compact convex \(C \subseteq \R^{n}\) is the convex hull
of its extreme points; in particular it has some.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Induct on \(n\), the case \(n = 0\) being a point. Let \(K\) be the
closed convex hull of \(\operatorname{ext} C\); it is contained in
\(C\), and we show the reverse inclusion.

First, \(\operatorname{ext} C \ne \emptyset\): if \(C\) is a single
point that point is extreme; otherwise take \(u \ne 0\), and by the
previous proposition the face \(F\) in direction \(u\) is a non-empty
compact convex set lying in a hyperplane, hence -- after an affine
identification of that hyperplane with \(\R^{n-1}\) -- it has an extreme
point by induction, which is extreme in \(C\).

Now suppose some \(x \in C\) is not in \(K\). Since \(K\) is compact and
convex, the separating hyperplane theorem gives \(u\) and \(\alpha\)
with \(\inner{u}{y} < \alpha < \inner{u}{x}\) for every \(y \in K\).
The face \(F\) of \(C\) in direction \(u\) then satisfies
\(\inner{u}{z} \ge \inner{u}{x} > \alpha\) for \(z \in F\), so \(F\)
misses \(K\); but \(F\) contains an extreme point of \(C\) by the
argument above applied to \(F\), and every extreme point of \(C\) lies
in \(K\) -- a contradiction. Hence \(C = K\), and \(K\) is already the
convex hull of \(\operatorname{ext} C\), which is compact.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-convex-set, thm-caratheodory, cor-heine-borel-in-euclidean-space
#+NAME: def-lattice
#+begin_definition
A /lattice/ in \(\R^{n}\) is \(\Lambda = A\Z^{n}\) for an invertible
linear \(A\); its /covolume/ is \(\abs{\det A}\). A set
\(C \subseteq \R^{n}\) is /symmetric/ if \(-C = C\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-lattice, def-convex-set, cor-heine-borel-in-euclidean-space, def-measure, thm-basic-properties-of-a-measure
#+NAME: thm-minkowski-convex-body
#+begin_theorem
Let \(\Lambda\) be a lattice of covolume \(d\) and \(C\) a convex,
symmetric, compact subset of \(\R^{n}\) of volume greater than
\(2^{n}d\). Then \(C\) contains a point of \(\Lambda\) other than the
origin.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Volume here is Lebesgue measure, and the two properties used are the
ones a measure has by definition together with translation invariance:
countable additivity and \(\vol(A + v) = \vol(A)\).

Consider \(C' = \tfrac{1}{2}C\), of volume greater than \(d\). If the
translates \(C' + \lambda\), \(\lambda\in\Lambda\), were pairwise
disjoint, then a fundamental domain \(P\) for \(\Lambda\) -- the image
of \([0,1)^{n}\) under \(A\), of volume \(d\), which tiles \(\R^{n}\)
under translation by \(\Lambda\) -- would receive the pieces
\((C'+\lambda)\cap P\) whose total volume is that of \(C'\), by
translating each back; that exceeds \(d\), which is impossible inside
\(P\).

So some two translates meet: there are \(u,v\in C'\) and
\(\lambda \ne \mu\) in \(\Lambda\) with \(u+\lambda = v+\mu\), hence
\(0 \ne \mu - \lambda = u - v\). Now \(u = \tfrac{1}{2}c_{1}\) and
\(v = \tfrac{1}{2}c_{2}\) with \(c_{i}\in C\), so
\(u - v = \tfrac{1}{2}c_{1} + \tfrac{1}{2}(-c_{2})\) is a midpoint of
two points of \(C\), using symmetry, hence lies in \(C\) by convexity.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The volume bound \(2^{n}d\) cannot be improved: the open cube of side
\(2\) about the origin has volume exactly \(2^{n}\) for
\(\Lambda = \Z^{n}\) and contains no non-zero lattice point.
Compactness in the statement is what allows the closed cube instead,
and the theorem is the standard route from geometry to number theory --
Lagrange's four-square theorem and the finiteness of the class number
are both proved by choosing the right lattice and the right body.
#+end_remark
