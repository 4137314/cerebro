#+TITLE: Maps, invertibility and the canonical factorisation
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:foundations:set_theory:
#+MSC: 03E20
#+HASH: 8862850c34db

Injectivity and surjectivity are usually introduced as two properties a
map may happen to have. They are better read as the two halves of one
statement: every map is a surjection followed by an injection, and the
surjection is a quotient. The isomorphism theorems of algebra are that
sentence, repeated once per structure.

#+NAME: def-injective-surjective
#+begin_definition
A map \(f \colon X \to Y\) is /injective/ if \(f(x) = f(x')\) implies
\(x = x'\), /surjective/ if every \(y \in Y\) is \(f(x)\) for some
\(x \in X\), and /bijective/ if it is both. Its /image/ is
\(f(X) = \setst{f(x)}{x \in X}\), and the /fibre/ over \(y\) is
\(f^{-1}(y) = \setst{x \in X}{f(x) = y}\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-injective-surjective
#+NAME: prop-bijective-iff-invertible
#+begin_proposition
A map \(f \colon X \to Y\) is bijective if and only if there is a map
\(g \colon Y \to X\) with \(g \circ f = \id_{X}\) and
\(f \circ g = \id_{Y}\), and then \(g\) is unique.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \(f\) be bijective. For \(y \in Y\) surjectivity provides some
\(x\) with \(f(x) = y\) and injectivity makes it unique, so
\(g(y) = x\) defines a map; by construction \(f(g(y)) = y\), and
\(g(f(x)) = x\) because \(x\) is the unique preimage of \(f(x)\).
Conversely, given such a \(g\): if \(f(x) = f(x')\) then applying \(g\)
gives \(x = x'\), and every \(y\) equals \(f(g(y))\).

For uniqueness, if \(g\) and \(h\) both work then
\(g = g \circ (f \circ h) = (g \circ f) \circ h = h\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-injective-surjective, def-equivalence-relation
#+NAME: prop-fibres-are-an-equivalence-relation
#+begin_proposition
For any map \(f \colon X \to Y\), the relation \(x \sim_{f} x'\)
defined by \(f(x) = f(x')\) is an equivalence relation, and its classes
are exactly the non-empty fibres of \(f\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity are those of equality in \(Y\).
The class of \(x\) is \(\setst{x'}{f(x') = f(x)} = f^{-1}(f(x))\), a
fibre containing \(x\), and every non-empty fibre \(f^{-1}(y)\) is of
this form since \(y = f(x)\) for any of its elements.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-fibres-are-an-equivalence-relation, thm-universal-property-of-a-quotient-set, prop-bijective-iff-invertible
#+NAME: thm-canonical-factorisation
#+begin_theorem
Every map \(f \colon X \to Y\) factors as
\[ X \xrightarrow{\ \pi\ } X/{\sim_{f}} \xrightarrow{\ \bar{f}\ } f(X) \xrightarrow{\ \iota\ } Y, \]
where \(\pi\) is the canonical projection, \(\iota\) the inclusion, and
\(\bar{f}\) a bijection; and this factorisation is unique.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The map \(x \mapsto f(x)\), viewed into \(f(X)\), is constant on the
classes of \(\sim_{f}\) by the definition of that relation, so the
universal property of the quotient gives a unique
\(\bar{f} \colon X/{\sim_{f}} \to f(X)\) with \(\bar{f} \circ \pi = f\).
It is surjective because \(f\) is onto \(f(X)\) and \(\pi\) is
surjective; it is injective because \(\bar{f}([x]) = \bar{f}([x'])\)
says \(f(x) = f(x')\), which is exactly \([x] = [x']\). Composing with
the inclusion gives the displayed factorisation.

Uniqueness: in any such factorisation the middle map must satisfy
\(\bar{f}(\pi(x)) = f(x)\), and \(\pi\) is surjective, so \(\bar{f}\) is
determined.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The three arrows are "forget what \(f\) cannot distinguish", "the
bijection that is left", and "remember where the values live". Read in
that order, the first isomorphism theorem for groups, for rings and for
vector spaces are all this statement with the observation that
\(\sim_{f}\) is compatible with the structure -- which for a
homomorphism means its classes are the cosets of the kernel.
#+end_remark
