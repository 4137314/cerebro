#+TITLE: Eigenvalues, the characteristic polynomial and Cayley-Hamilton
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:linear_algebra:
#+MSC: 15A18
#+HASH: 4ac5ee2ab58e

A linear map is understood when a basis is found in which it acts
independently on each coordinate. Eigenvectors are the candidates for
such a basis, and the determinant turns the search for them into a
question about the roots of one polynomial -- after which everything
depends on whether the field contains those roots and whether they are
distinct.

#+DEPENDS: def-linear-map, thm-determinant-detects-invertibility, def-polynomial-ring
#+NAME: def-eigenvalue
#+begin_definition
Let \(f \colon V \to V\) be linear, \(V\) a vector space over \(K\). A
scalar \(\lambda \in K\) is an /eigenvalue/ of \(f\) if
\(f(v) = \lambda v\) for some \(v \ne 0\), which is then an
/eigenvector/; the /eigenspace/ is
\(V_{\lambda} = \ker(f - \lambda\,\id)\). For \(\dim V = n\) and a
matrix \(A\) of \(f\), the /characteristic polynomial/ is
\(\chi_{A}(x) = \det(x I - A) \in K[x]\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-eigenvalue, thm-determinant-is-multiplicative
#+NAME: prop-eigenvalues-are-the-roots
#+begin_proposition
\(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(f\) if and only if
\(\chi_{A}(\lambda) = 0\); and \(\chi_{A}\) is monic of degree \(n\) and
does not depend on the basis chosen.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
\(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue exactly when \(f - \lambda\id\) has
non-trivial kernel, that is is not invertible, that is
\(\det(\lambda I - A) = 0\). Expanding the defining sum of
\(\det(xI - A)\), the term of \(\sigma = \id\) is
\(\prod_{i}(x - a_{ii})\), of degree \(n\) and monic, and every other
term omits at least two diagonal entries, hence has degree at most
\(n-2\).

A change of basis replaces \(A\) by \(P^{-1}AP\), and
\(\det(xI - P^{-1}AP) = \det(P^{-1}(xI-A)P) = \det(xI-A)\) by
multiplicativity.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-eigenvalue, def-span-and-independence, cor-extension-to-a-basis
#+NAME: prop-eigenvectors-for-distinct-eigenvalues
#+begin_proposition
Eigenvectors for pairwise distinct eigenvalues are linearly
independent. Hence a map on an \(n\)-dimensional space with \(n\)
distinct eigenvalues has a basis of eigenvectors.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Suppose not, and take a dependence
\(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}v_{i} = 0\) with all \(\alpha_{i}\ne 0\) and
\(k\) minimal, the \(v_{i}\) eigenvectors for distinct
\(\lambda_{i}\). Applying \(f\) gives
\(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\lambda_{i}v_{i} = 0\); subtracting
\(\lambda_{k}\) times the original relation leaves
\(\sum_{i<k}\alpha_{i}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})v_{i} = 0\), a shorter
dependence with non-zero coefficients -- contradicting minimality
(for \(k = 1\) the relation reads \(\alpha_{1}v_{1} = 0\) with
\(v_{1}\ne0\), already impossible).

With \(n\) distinct eigenvalues, the corresponding eigenvectors are
\(n\) independent vectors, hence a basis.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-eigenvectors-for-distinct-eigenvalues, def-eigenvalue
#+NAME: thm-diagonalisability-criterion
#+begin_theorem
\(f\) has a basis of eigenvectors if and only if
\(V = \bigoplus_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}\), the sum over the eigenvalues; and
then the matrix of \(f\) in that basis is diagonal.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
A basis of eigenvectors partitions into the eigenspaces, so \(V\) is
their sum, and the sum is direct because a non-trivial relation between
vectors from distinct eigenspaces would contradict the previous
proposition. Conversely, taking a basis of each \(V_{\lambda}\) and
concatenating gives a basis of the direct sum, consisting of
eigenvectors. In such a basis the \(j\)-th column of the matrix is
\(\lambda_{j}e_{j}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-eigenvalue, thm-laplace-expansion-and-adjugate, def-polynomial-ring
#+NAME: thm-cayley-hamilton
#+begin_theorem
\(\chi_{A}(A) = 0\) for every square matrix \(A\) over a commutative
ring.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Work in the matrices over \(K[x]\) and put
\(B(x) = \operatorname{adj}(xI - A)\). Its entries are determinants of
\((n-1)\times(n-1)\) matrices whose entries are of degree at most \(1\),
so \(B(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}B_{i}x^{i}\) with \(B_{i}\) matrices over
the base ring. The adjugate identity gives
\[ (xI - A)\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}B_{i}x^{i}\Bigr) = \chi_{A}(x) I = \Bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{n}c_{j}x^{j}\Bigr) I. \]
Comparing coefficients of \(x^{j}\): \(B_{j-1} - AB_{j} = c_{j}I\), with
the conventions \(B_{-1} = B_{n} = 0\).

Multiply the equation for \(x^{j}\) on the left by \(A^{j}\) and sum
over \(j\). The left-hand side telescopes to \(0\), each term
\(A^{j}B_{j-1}\) cancelling against \(-A^{j-1}\cdot AB_{j-1}\) from the
next equation; the right-hand side is \(\sum_{j}c_{j}A^{j} = \chi_{A}(A)\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The proof cannot be shortened to "substitute \(x = A\) in
\(\det(xI-A)\)": the entries of \(xI - A\) are scalars-plus-\(x\), and
substituting a matrix for \(x\) is not a ring homomorphism when the
matrices involved do not commute. Comparing coefficients first, and only
then multiplying by powers of \(A\) on a fixed side, is exactly what
repairs that gap -- and it is why the theorem holds over any commutative
ring, with no eigenvalues in sight.
#+end_remark
