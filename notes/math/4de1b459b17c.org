#+TITLE: Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26A24
#+HASH: 4de1b459b17c

A polynomial that matches \(n\) derivatives at a point is easy to write
down; what makes it useful is a formula for the error, and Taylor's
theorem gives one that is exact, at an unknown point.

#+DEPENDS: def-derivative
#+NAME: def-taylor-polynomial
#+begin_definition
Let \(f\) be \(n\) times differentiable at \(a\). Its /Taylor polynomial
of order/ \(n\) at \(a\) is
\[ T_{n}(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!} (x - a)^{k}. \]
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-taylor-polynomial, thm-rolle
#+NAME: thm-taylor-lagrange
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f\) be \(n+1\) times differentiable on an open interval containing
\(a\) and \(x\). Then there is \(\xi\) strictly between \(a\) and \(x\)
with
\[ f(x) - T_{n}(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!} (x - a)^{n+1}. \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Fix \(x \ne a\) and choose \(M\) so that
\(f(x) - T_{n}(x) = M (x - a)^{n+1}\). Define, for \(t\) between \(a\)
and \(x\),
\[ g(t) = f(x) - \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(t)}{k!} (x - t)^{k} - M (x - t)^{n+1}. \]
Then \(g(x) = 0\) by construction and \(g(a) = 0\) by the choice of
\(M\). The sum telescopes on differentiation: all terms cancel in pairs
except the last, giving
\[ g'(t) = -\frac{f^{(n+1)}(t)}{n!} (x - t)^{n} + (n+1) M (x - t)^{n}. \]
By Rolle's theorem there is \(\xi\) strictly between \(a\) and \(x\)
with \(g'(\xi) = 0\), and since \((x - \xi)^{n} \ne 0\) this forces
\(M = f^{(n+1)}(\xi)/(n+1)!\), which is the claim.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The remainder is stated with an existential \(\xi\) that is never
computed. What one uses is a bound on \(f^{(n+1)}\) over the interval,
which turns the identity into the estimate that actually appears in
applications.
#+end_remark
