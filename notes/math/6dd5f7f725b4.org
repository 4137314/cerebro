#+TITLE: Uniform bounds on the Taylor remainder
#+DATE: [2026-08-06 Thu]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26A24
#+HASH: 6dd5f7f725b4

Taylor's theorem gives the remainder at an unproved point \(\xi\), which
is not a number anyone can produce. What turns it into an estimate is a
bound on the derivatives, and if that bound is uniform in the order the
Taylor polynomials converge to the function outright. Stated for a
hypothesis on the derivatives rather than for any particular function:
the sine and the exponential are the cases one meets, and neither is the
content.

#+DEPENDS: thm-taylor-lagrange, def-taylor-polynomial
#+NAME: thm-taylor-remainder-bound
#+begin_theorem
Let \(I\) be an interval containing \(a\), let \(f\) be \(n+1\) times
differentiable on \(I\), and suppose \(\abs{f^{(n+1)}} \le M\) on
\(I\). Then for every \(x \in I\),
\[ \abs{f(x) - T_{n}(x)} \le \frac{M}{(n+1)!} \abs{x - a}^{n+1}. \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
By Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder there is \(\xi\)
strictly between \(a\) and \(x\) with
\(f(x) - T_{n}(x) = f^{(n+1)}(\xi) (x - a)^{n+1} / (n+1)!\). Since
\(\xi \in I\) we have \(\abs{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)} \le M\), and taking
absolute values gives the bound.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-taylor-remainder-bound, thm-squeeze
#+NAME: cor-taylor-converges-under-a-uniform-bound
#+begin_corollary
Suppose \(f\) is infinitely differentiable on \(I\) and there is one
\(M\) with \(\abs{f^{(k)}} \le M\) on \(I\) for *every* \(k\). Then
\(T_{n}(x) \to f(x)\) for each \(x \in I\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Fix \(x \in I\) and put \(r = \abs{x - a}\). The theorem gives
\(\abs{f(x) - T_{n}(x)} \le M r^{n+1}/(n+1)!\). For fixed \(r\) the
sequence \(r^{n+1}/(n+1)!\) tends to \(0\): from the index \(n_{0} > 2r\)
onwards each factor multiplies the previous term by
\(r/(n+1) < 1/2\), so the terms are eventually dominated by a geometric
sequence with ratio \(1/2\). Squeezing between \(0\) and that bound
gives \(\abs{f(x) - T_{n}(x)} \to 0\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The hypothesis is what does the work, and it is strong: one bound for
*all* derivatives. Without it a function can be infinitely
differentiable and have every Taylor coefficient at a point vanish
while the function does not -- so the polynomials converge, to something
that is not the function. Taylor's theorem is a statement about
approximation of finite order; convergence of the whole series is a
separate hypothesis, never a consequence of smoothness alone.
#+end_remark
