#+TITLE: Integral domains, fields, prime and maximal ideals
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:commutative_algebra:
#+MSC: 13A15, 13C05
#+HASH: d15295001ab8

Two properties a ring may have -- no zero divisors, every non-zero
element invertible -- turn out to be properties of *ideals* of a larger
ring, read off from the quotient. That translation is what makes
commutative algebra geometric: a prime ideal is a point of a space, and
the dictionary below is the first entry of it.

#+DEPENDS: def-ring
#+NAME: def-integral-domain
#+begin_definition
A commutative ring \(R\) with \(1 \ne 0\) is an /integral domain/ if
\(ab = 0\) implies \(a = 0\) or \(b = 0\). It is a /field/ if every
non-zero element is a unit.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-integral-domain
#+NAME: prop-a-field-is-a-domain
#+begin_proposition
Every field is an integral domain, and every finite integral domain is
a field.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
If \(ab = 0\) in a field with \(a \ne 0\), multiplying by \(a^{-1}\)
gives \(b = 0\). Let now \(R\) be a finite domain and \(a \ne 0\). The
map \(x \mapsto ax\) is injective, since \(ax = ay\) gives
\(a(x-y) = 0\) and hence \(x = y\); an injective map of a finite set to
itself is surjective, so \(ax = 1\) for some \(x\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-integral-domain, def-ideal
#+NAME: prop-ideals-of-a-field
#+begin_proposition
A commutative ring \(R\) with \(1 \ne 0\) is a field if and only if its
only ideals are \(\set{0}\) and \(R\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \(R\) be a field and \(I \ne \set{0}\) an ideal, say \(a \in I\)
with \(a \ne 0\). Then \(1 = a^{-1}a \in I\), so \(r = r \cdot 1 \in I\)
for every \(r\) and \(I = R\). Conversely, if the only ideals are the
trivial ones and \(a \ne 0\), the principal ideal \((a)\) is not
\(\set{0}\), hence equals \(R\), so \(1 = ab\) for some \(b\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-ideal, def-integral-domain
#+NAME: def-prime-and-maximal
#+begin_definition
Let \(R\) be commutative. A proper ideal \(P \subsetneq R\) is /prime/
if \(ab \in P\) implies \(a \in P\) or \(b \in P\); a proper ideal
\(M \subsetneq R\) is /maximal/ if no ideal lies strictly between \(M\)
and \(R\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-prime-and-maximal, thm-quotient-ring
#+NAME: thm-prime-iff-quotient-is-a-domain
#+begin_theorem
A proper ideal \(P\) of a commutative ring \(R\) is prime if and only if
\(R/P\) is an integral domain.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
\(R/P\) is commutative with \(1 + P \ne 0 + P\) exactly when \(P\) is
proper. Now \((a+P)(b+P) = 0 + P\) means \(ab \in P\), and
\(a + P = 0 + P\) means \(a \in P\). So "no zero divisors in \(R/P\)"
is literally the statement that \(ab \in P\) implies \(a \in P\) or
\(b \in P\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-prime-and-maximal, thm-quotient-ring, prop-ideals-of-a-field
#+NAME: thm-maximal-iff-quotient-is-a-field
#+begin_theorem
A proper ideal \(M\) of a commutative ring \(R\) is maximal if and only
if \(R/M\) is a field.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The ideals of \(R/M\) are exactly the \(J/M\) for ideals \(J\) with
\(M \subseteq J \subseteq R\): the preimage under \(\pi\) of an ideal of
\(R/M\) is such a \(J\), and \(\pi(J)\) recovers it, since \(\pi\) is
surjective and \(\pi^{-1}(\pi(J)) = J\) when \(J \supseteq M\). So
\(R/M\) has only the two trivial ideals precisely when there is no ideal
strictly between \(M\) and \(R\); by the previous proposition that is
exactly when \(R/M\) is a field.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-prime-iff-quotient-is-a-domain, thm-maximal-iff-quotient-is-a-field, prop-a-field-is-a-domain
#+NAME: cor-maximal-implies-prime
#+begin_corollary
Every maximal ideal of a commutative ring is prime.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
If \(M\) is maximal then \(R/M\) is a field, hence an integral domain,
hence \(M\) is prime.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-prime-and-maximal, thm-zorn
#+NAME: thm-krull
#+begin_theorem
Every commutative ring with \(1 \ne 0\) has a maximal ideal; more
generally every proper ideal is contained in one.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(I\) be a proper ideal and order by inclusion the set \(P\) of
proper ideals containing \(I\); it is non-empty, containing \(I\). Let
\(\mathcal{C}\) be a chain in \(P\) and \(U\) the union of its members.
\(U\) is an ideal: two elements of \(U\) lie in a common member of the
chain, so their difference lies in \(U\), and absorption is inherited.
\(U\) is proper, because \(1 \in U\) would put \(1\) in some member of
the chain, which is proper. So \(U \in P\) is an upper bound, and Zorn's
lemma gives a maximal element of \(P\) -- an ideal maximal among the
proper ideals containing \(I\), which is maximal outright since any
ideal strictly containing it is proper or all of \(R\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
"\(1 \in U\) would put \(1\) in some member of the chain" is the whole
reason the proof works, and the reason ideals are required to be proper:
properness is a *finitary* condition, so it survives unions of chains.
The same argument proves that every vector space has a basis and that
every finitely satisfiable set of formulas has a maximal such extension
-- three instances of one Zorn argument.
#+end_remark
