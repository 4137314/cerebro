#+TITLE: Cauchy sequences and the completeness criterion
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 40A05
#+HASH: cf37cebb4003

The definition of convergence names the limit, so it cannot be used to
/prove/ that a limit exists without producing a candidate first. The
Cauchy condition removes that obstacle: it speaks only about the terms
of the sequence, and in \(\R\) it is equivalent to convergence.

#+DEPENDS: def-sequence-limit
#+NAME: def-cauchy-sequence
#+begin_definition
A sequence \((a_{n})\) of real numbers is a /Cauchy sequence/ if for
every \(\eps > 0\) there is \(N \in \N\) such that
\(\abs{a_{n} - a_{m}} < \eps\) for all \(n, m \ge N\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-cauchy-sequence
#+NAME: prop-convergent-implies-cauchy
#+begin_proposition
Every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence, and every Cauchy
sequence is bounded.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
If \(a_{n} \to L\), fix \(\eps > 0\) and choose \(N\) with
\(\abs{a_{n} - L} < \eps/2\) for \(n \ge N\); then for \(n, m \ge N\),
\(\abs{a_{n} - a_{m}} \le \abs{a_{n} - L} + \abs{L - a_{m}} < \eps\).
For boundedness, take \(\eps = 1\) in the Cauchy condition and let \(N\)
be as given: for \(n \ge N\) one has
\(\abs{a_{n}} \le \abs{a_{N}} + 1\), so
\(C = \max(\abs{a_{1}}, \dots, \abs{a_{N-1}}, \abs{a_{N}} + 1)\) bounds
every term.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-convergent-implies-cauchy, thm-bolzano-weierstrass
#+NAME: thm-cauchy-criterion
#+begin_theorem
A sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is a Cauchy
sequence.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
One direction is the proposition above. Conversely, let \((a_{n})\) be
Cauchy. It is bounded, so by Bolzano--Weierstrass some subsequence
\((a_{n_{k}})\) converges, say to \(L\). Fix \(\eps > 0\) and choose
\(N\) with \(\abs{a_{n} - a_{m}} < \eps/2\) for \(n, m \ge N\), and then
\(k\) with \(n_{k} \ge N\) and \(\abs{a_{n_{k}} - L} < \eps/2\). For
every \(n \ge N\),
\[ \abs{a_{n} - L} \le \abs{a_{n} - a_{n_{k}}} + \abs{a_{n_{k}} - L} < \eps, \]
so \(a_{n} \to L\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
This is the point at which \(\R\) and \(\Q\) part company for the second
time: the sequence of decimal truncations of \(\sqrt{2}\) is Cauchy in
\(\Q\) and converges to nothing there. Completeness, stated as an axiom
about suprema, has just been shown to imply completeness in the metric
sense.
#+end_remark
