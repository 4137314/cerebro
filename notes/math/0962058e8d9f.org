#+TITLE: Free groups and presentations
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:group_theory:
#+MSC: 20E05, 20F05
#+HASH: 0962058e8d9f

Every group so far has been given by exhibiting it. A presentation does
the opposite: it names generators and relations and asserts that
/nothing else holds/. Making that precise requires a group in which no
relation holds at all, and a universal property saying that maps out of
it are unconstrained -- which is exactly what determines it up to unique
isomorphism.

#+DEPENDS: def-group, def-group-homomorphism
#+NAME: def-free-group
#+begin_definition
Let \(S\) be a set. A group \(F\) with a map \(\iota \colon S \to F\) is
/free on \(S\)/ if for every group \(G\) and every map
\(f \colon S \to G\) there is exactly one homomorphism
\(\bar{f} \colon F \to G\) with \(\bar{f}\circ\iota = f\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-free-group
#+NAME: prop-free-groups-are-unique
#+begin_proposition
Any two groups free on the same set \(S\) are isomorphic by a unique
isomorphism commuting with the maps from \(S\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \((F,\iota)\) and \((F',\iota')\) both be free on \(S\). The
universal property of \(F\) applied to \(\iota'\) gives
\(u \colon F \to F'\) with \(u\iota = \iota'\), and symmetrically
\(u' \colon F'\to F\) with \(u'\iota' = \iota\). Then \(u'u\) is a
homomorphism \(F \to F\) with \(u'u\iota = \iota\); the identity is
another, and the uniqueness clause forces \(u'u = \id\). Likewise
\(uu' = \id\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-free-group, prop-generated-subgroup-as-words
#+NAME: thm-free-group-exists
#+begin_theorem
For every set \(S\) there is a group free on \(S\): the set \(F(S)\) of
/reduced words/ -- finite strings \(s_{1}^{\eps_{1}}\cdots s_{k}^{\eps_{k}}\)
with \(s_{i}\in S\), \(\eps_{i}\in\set{1,-1}\), and no two adjacent
letters of the form \(s^{1}s^{-1}\) or \(s^{-1}s^{1}\) -- under
concatenation followed by cancellation.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(F(S)\) be the set of reduced words, and for \(s \in S\) let
\(\lambda_{s}\) be the map of \(F(S)\) to itself prefixing \(s\) and
cancelling if the first letter is \(s^{-1}\); define \(\lambda_{s^{-1}}\)
symmetrically. Each \(\lambda_{s}\) is a bijection with inverse
\(\lambda_{s^{-1}}\), by inspection of the two cases. Let \(F\) be the
subgroup of \(\Sym(F(S))\) generated by the \(\lambda_{s}\).

The map sending a reduced word to the corresponding composite of
\(\lambda\)'s applied to the empty word is a bijection \(F(S) \to\)
(orbit of the empty word), and it identifies the product of reduced
words with concatenation-and-cancellation; so that operation makes
\(F(S)\) a group isomorphic to \(F\). Associativity, which is the only
delicate point for words, is inherited from composition of maps.

Universality: given \(f \colon S \to G\), the only possible value of a
homomorphism on \(s_{1}^{\eps_{1}}\cdots s_{k}^{\eps_{k}}\) is
\(f(s_{1})^{\eps_{1}}\cdots f(s_{k})^{\eps_{k}}\), which proves
uniqueness. That formula is well defined on reduced words and respects
the product, since cancelling \(s s^{-1}\) in a word corresponds to
cancelling \(f(s)f(s)^{-1}\) in \(G\); so it is the required
homomorphism.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-free-group-exists, thm-first-isomorphism-groups, def-normal-subgroup
#+NAME: def-presentation
#+begin_definition
Let \(S\) be a set and \(R \subseteq F(S)\). The group /presented/ by
\(\langle S \mid R\rangle\) is \(F(S)/N\), where \(N\) is the smallest
normal subgroup containing \(R\) -- the intersection of all of them,
equivalently the subgroup generated by all conjugates of elements of
\(R\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-presentation, thm-free-group-exists, thm-quotient-group
#+NAME: thm-von-dyck
#+begin_theorem
Every group is presented by some \(\langle S \mid R\rangle\). Moreover,
if \(G = \langle S \mid R\rangle\) and \(H\) is a group with elements
\(h_{s}\), \(s \in S\), such that every relation in \(R\) holds among
the \(h_{s}\), then there is a unique homomorphism \(G \to H\) sending
the class of \(s\) to \(h_{s}\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
For the first claim take \(S = G\) and let \(R\) be the kernel of the
canonical \(\bar{f}\colon F(G)\to G\) extending the identity map; that
kernel is normal and \(G \iso F(G)/\ker\bar{f}\) by the first
isomorphism theorem.

For the second, the universal property of \(F(S)\) gives a homomorphism
\(u\colon F(S)\to H\) with \(u(s) = h_{s}\). By hypothesis \(u\) kills
every element of \(R\), hence every conjugate of one and every product
of such -- that is, all of \(N\). So \(u\) factors through
\(F(S)/N = G\), uniquely since the classes of the \(s\) generate.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The theorem is what makes a presentation usable: to define a map out of
a presented group one checks finitely many relations, and nothing else.
What it does not give is any way to decide whether two words present the
same element -- the word problem is undecidable for finitely presented
groups, which is the sharpest illustration in algebra of the
undecidability results of computability theory.
#+end_remark
