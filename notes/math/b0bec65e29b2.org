#+TITLE: The continuous image of an interval and the continuous inverse
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26A15, 26A48
#+HASH: b0bec65e29b2

The intermediate value theorem and the extreme value theorem are
usually stated as facts about individual values. Put together they say
something structural -- a continuous function maps intervals to
intervals, and compact intervals to compact intervals -- and that is
the form needed to invert one. The existence of \(\log\), of the
inverse trigonometric functions and of every \(n\)-th root as a
/continuous/ function is this theorem, not a construction.

#+DEPENDS: thm-intermediate-value, thm-extreme-value
#+NAME: cor-continuous-image-interval
#+begin_corollary
The image of an interval under a continuous function is an interval,
and the image of a closed bounded interval \([a,b]\) is a closed
bounded interval \([m, M]\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Let \(I\) be an interval and \(f\) continuous on it. If \(u < y < v\)
with \(u, v \in f(I)\), say \(u = f(p)\) and \(v = f(q)\), then the
intermediate value theorem applied on the interval with endpoints
\(p, q\) -- contained in \(I\) -- produces \(c\) with \(f(c) = y\), so
\(y \in f(I)\). A set containing every point between any two of its
points is an interval. For \(I = [a,b]\), the extreme value theorem
gives \(p, q\) with \(m = f(p)\) the minimum and \(M = f(q)\) the
maximum, so \(f([a,b]) \subseteq [m, M]\), and the first part gives the
reverse inclusion.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-intermediate-value
#+NAME: prop-injective-is-strictly-monotone
#+begin_proposition
A continuous injective function on an interval \(I\) is strictly
monotone.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Suppose \(f\) is neither strictly increasing nor strictly decreasing.
Then there are \(x < y < z\) in \(I\) for which \(f(y)\) is not strictly
between \(f(x)\) and \(f(z)\); since \(f\) is injective the three values
are distinct, so \(f(y)\) is either larger than both or smaller than
both. Say \(f(y) > f(x)\) and \(f(y) > f(z)\), and pick \(w\) with
\(\max(f(x), f(z)) < w < f(y)\). The intermediate value theorem gives
\(c_{1} \in (x,y)\) and \(c_{2} \in (y,z)\) with
\(f(c_{1}) = f(c_{2}) = w\), and \(c_{1} \ne c_{2}\) -- contradicting
injectivity. The other case is the same argument applied to \(-f\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-injective-is-strictly-monotone, cor-continuous-image-interval, thm-monotone-one-sided
#+NAME: thm-continuous-inverse
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f\) be continuous and injective on an interval \(I\), and let
\(J = f(I)\). Then \(f^{-1} \colon J \to I\) exists, is strictly
monotone in the same sense as \(f\), and is continuous.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
By the proposition \(f\) is strictly monotone, so it is a bijection
onto \(J\), which is an interval by the corollary; the inverse is
strictly monotone in the same sense, since \(f\) preserves the order.
Let \(g = f^{-1}\) and suppose \(g\) were discontinuous at some
\(y_{0} \in J\). Being monotone, \(g\) has both one-sided limits there,
and by the jump description they cannot both equal \(g(y_{0})\): some
one-sided limit differs, say
\(\lim_{y \to y_{0}^{-}} g(y) = u < g(y_{0})\).
Then \(g\) omits every value strictly between \(u\) and
\(g(y_{0})\), so \(g(J) = I\) misses an interval of points lying
between two of its own -- impossible, since \(I\) is an interval.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Continuity of the inverse is exactly what fails without the interval
hypothesis. Restrict a continuous bijection to a domain that is a union
of two pieces -- \([0,1) \cup [2,3]\) mapped onto \([0,2]\) in the
obvious order-preserving way -- and the inverse jumps at the seam. The
theorem is therefore a statement about the /domain/ as much as about
the function.
#+end_remark
