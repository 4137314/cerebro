#+TITLE: Subspace, product and quotient topologies
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:topology:general_topology:
#+MSC: 54B05, 54B10, 54B15
#+HASH: e1c1d7108655

Three constructions produce almost every space one meets, and each is
defined the same way: by the maps it is required to make continuous.
Stating them by their universal properties rather than by a formula is
what keeps the proofs short -- and it is what shows that the product
topology, which looks like the wrong definition for infinite products,
is the only reasonable one.

#+DEPENDS: def-topological-space, def-continuous-map
#+NAME: def-subspace-topology
#+begin_definition
Let \(A \subseteq X\) with \(X\) a topological space. The /subspace
topology/ on \(A\) has as open sets the \(U \cap A\) with \(U\) open in
\(X\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-subspace-topology, prop-continuity-tested-on-a-subbasis
#+NAME: thm-subspace-universal-property
#+begin_theorem
The subspace topology is a topology; the inclusion
\(\iota \colon A \to X\) is continuous; and it is the coarsest topology
with that property. A map \(f \colon Z \to A\) is continuous if and only
if \(\iota \circ f \colon Z \to X\) is.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
\(\emptyset\) and \(A\) arise from \(\emptyset\) and \(X\), and
intersecting with \(A\) commutes with unions and finite intersections,
so the family is a topology. Continuity of \(\iota\) is the statement
\(\iota^{-1}(U) = U\cap A\), and any topology making \(\iota\)
continuous must contain every such set, so the subspace topology is the
coarsest.

If \(f\) is continuous so is \(\iota\circ f\). Conversely, if
\(\iota\circ f\) is continuous and \(U \cap A\) is open in \(A\), then
\(f^{-1}(U\cap A) = (\iota\circ f)^{-1}(U)\) is open in \(Z\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-topological-space, def-basis-of-a-topology
#+NAME: def-product-topology
#+begin_definition
Let \((X_{i})_{i \in I}\) be topological spaces. The /product topology/
on \(\prod_{i}X_{i}\) is generated by the subbasis of sets
\(\pi_{j}^{-1}(U)\), for \(j \in I\) and \(U \subseteq X_{j}\) open,
where \(\pi_{j}\) is the projection onto the \(j\)-th factor. A basis is
therefore given by the sets \(\prod_{i}U_{i}\) with \(U_{i}\) open and
\(U_{i} = X_{i}\) for all but finitely many \(i\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-product-topology, prop-continuity-tested-on-a-subbasis
#+NAME: thm-product-universal-property
#+begin_theorem
Each projection \(\pi_{j}\) is continuous, and the product topology is
the coarsest with that property. A map \(f \colon Z \to \prod_{i}X_{i}\)
is continuous if and only if every \(\pi_{i}\circ f\) is.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Continuity of \(\pi_{j}\) and minimality hold because the generating
subbasis consists precisely of the sets a topology must contain to make
the projections continuous.

If \(f\) is continuous, so is each \(\pi_{i}\circ f\) as a composition.
Conversely, suppose every \(\pi_{i}\circ f\) is continuous. For a
subbasic set,
\(f^{-1}(\pi_{j}^{-1}(U)) = (\pi_{j}\circ f)^{-1}(U)\) is open; by the
subbasis criterion \(f\) is continuous.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The finiteness restriction in the basis is what the universal property
forces. Declaring all products \(\prod_{i}U_{i}\) of open sets to be
basic gives a strictly finer topology for infinite \(I\) -- the box
topology -- for which the theorem above is false, and with it Tychonoff's
theorem. The definition is chosen to make the maps behave, not the sets
look natural.
#+end_remark

#+DEPENDS: def-topological-space, def-equivalence-relation, def-continuous-map
#+NAME: def-quotient-topology
#+begin_definition
Let \(\sim\) be an equivalence relation on a space \(X\) and
\(\pi \colon X \to X/{\sim}\) the projection. The /quotient topology/
declares \(V \subseteq X/{\sim}\) open exactly when \(\pi^{-1}(V)\) is
open in \(X\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-quotient-topology, thm-universal-property-of-a-quotient-set
#+NAME: thm-quotient-universal-property
#+begin_theorem
The quotient topology is a topology, \(\pi\) is continuous, and it is
the finest topology with that property. A map
\(g \colon X/{\sim} \to Z\) is continuous if and only if
\(g \circ \pi\) is; hence a continuous \(f \colon X \to Z\) that is
constant on classes induces a unique continuous
\(\bar{f} \colon X/{\sim} \to Z\) with \(\bar{f}\circ\pi = f\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Preimage commutes with unions and intersections, so the family of \(V\)
with \(\pi^{-1}(V)\) open is a topology; \(\pi\) is continuous by
definition, and any topology on \(X/{\sim}\) making \(\pi\) continuous
consists of such \(V\), hence is coarser.

If \(g\) is continuous so is \(g \circ \pi\). Conversely, if
\(g\circ\pi\) is continuous and \(W \subseteq Z\) is open, then
\(\pi^{-1}(g^{-1}(W)) = (g\circ\pi)^{-1}(W)\) is open, so
\(g^{-1}(W)\) is open by the definition of the quotient topology. The
last claim combines this with the universal property of the quotient
set, which supplies \(\bar{f}\) as a map.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Subspaces and products are defined by mapping /into/ them, quotients by
mapping /out/ of them, and the two directions correspond to taking the
coarsest and the finest topology. That is the whole reason the same
three-line proof works for all of them, and the reason a quotient of a
well-behaved space can be badly behaved: nothing in the construction
preserves separation properties.
#+end_remark
