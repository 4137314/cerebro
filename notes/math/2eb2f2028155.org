#+TITLE: The fundamental theorem of calculus
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26A42
#+HASH: 2eb2f2028155

Differentiation and integration were defined independently, by limits of
difference quotients and by limits of sums. The two theorems below say
they undo each other -- which is what makes the integral computable at
all.

#+DEPENDS: def-riemann-integral, def-derivative, thm-continuous-is-integrable, thm-integral-additivity, prop-integral-monotonicity
#+NAME: thm-ftc-first
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f \colon [a,b] \to \R\) be continuous and define
\(F(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) \dd t\). Then \(F\) is differentiable on
\((a,b)\) and \(F' = f\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Fix \(c \in (a,b)\) and \(\eps > 0\). By continuity at \(c\) there is
\(\delta > 0\) with \(\abs{f(t) - f(c)} < \eps\) for
\(\abs{t - c} < \delta\). For \(0 < \abs{h} < \delta\), additivity of
the integral gives \(F(c + h) - F(c) = \int_{c}^{c+h} f(t) \dd t\),
hence
\[ \Bigl| \frac{F(c+h) - F(c)}{h} - f(c) \Bigr| = \Bigl| \frac{1}{h} \int_{c}^{c+h} (f(t) - f(c)) \dd t \Bigr| \le \eps, \]
since the integrand is bounded by \(\eps\) on an interval of length
\(\abs{h}\). As \(\eps\) was arbitrary, \(F'(c) = f(c)\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-ftc-first, cor-vanishing-derivative
#+NAME: thm-ftc-second
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f \colon [a,b] \to \R\) be continuous and let \(G\) be any
antiderivative of \(f\) on \([a,b]\). Then
\[ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \dd x = G(b) - G(a). \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(F(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f\). By the first part \(F' = f = G'\) on
\((a,b)\), so \(F - G\) is constant, say \(F - G \equiv k\), by the
corollary to the mean value theorem. Since \(F(a) = 0\) we get
\(k = -G(a)\), and evaluating at \(b\) gives
\(\int_{a}^{b} f = F(b) = G(b) - G(a)\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The second statement is what one computes with, and it needs an
antiderivative to exist; the first statement is what guarantees one
does, for every continuous integrand. Neither is a definition: the
integral was already defined, by Darboux sums, before either was proved.
#+end_remark
