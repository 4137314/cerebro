#+TITLE: Propositional logic: formulas, assignments and compactness
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:foundations:logic:
#+MSC: 03B05
#+HASH: 6f4f5bf3fad9

Logic becomes mathematics at the point where formulas stop being
notation for arguments and become objects that a proof can quantify
over. The whole of propositional logic is then two inductive
definitions -- one building formulas, one evaluating them -- and the
theorem that makes the subject infinite rather than merely finitary:
satisfiability is decided by finite subsets.

#+NAME: def-propositional-formula
#+begin_definition
Fix a set \(V\) of /propositional variables/. The set \(\mathrm{Form}\)
of /formulas/ over \(V\) is the smallest set of finite strings such that
every \(p \in V\) belongs to it and, whenever \(\varphi, \psi\) belong
to it, so do \(\neg \varphi\) and \((\varphi \wedge \psi)\). One writes
\((\varphi \vee \psi)\) for \(\neg(\neg \varphi \wedge \neg \psi)\) and
\((\varphi \to \psi)\) for \(\neg(\varphi \wedge \neg \psi)\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-propositional-formula
#+NAME: prop-induction-on-formulas
#+begin_proposition
Let \(S \subseteq \mathrm{Form}\) contain every variable and satisfy:
\(\varphi, \psi \in S\) implies \(\neg \varphi \in S\) and
\((\varphi \wedge \psi) \in S\). Then \(S = \mathrm{Form}\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
"Smallest" in the definition means the intersection of all sets of
strings with the two closure properties, which is itself such a set.
\(S\) is one of the sets being intersected, so
\(\mathrm{Form} \subseteq S\); the reverse inclusion is the hypothesis
\(S \subseteq \mathrm{Form}\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-propositional-formula, prop-induction-on-formulas
#+NAME: def-truth-assignment
#+begin_definition
A /truth assignment/ is a map \(v \colon V \to \set{0,1}\). It extends
to \(\hat{v} \colon \mathrm{Form} \to \set{0,1}\) by
\(\hat{v}(p) = v(p)\), \(\hat{v}(\neg \varphi) = 1 - \hat{v}(\varphi)\)
and
\(\hat{v}((\varphi \wedge \psi)) = \min(\hat{v}(\varphi), \hat{v}(\psi))\).
One writes \(v \models \varphi\) for \(\hat{v}(\varphi) = 1\) and says
\(v\) /satisfies/ \(\varphi\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-truth-assignment
#+NAME: def-satisfiable
#+begin_definition
A set \(\Sigma \subseteq \mathrm{Form}\) is /satisfiable/ if some
assignment satisfies every member of it, and /finitely satisfiable/ if
every finite subset of it is satisfiable. A formula satisfied by every
assignment is a /tautology/.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-satisfiable, thm-zorn
#+NAME: lem-lindenbaum
#+begin_lemma
Every finitely satisfiable \(\Sigma\) is contained in a set \(M\) that
is finitely satisfiable and maximal with that property, and such an
\(M\) contains exactly one of \(\varphi\), \(\neg \varphi\) for every
formula \(\varphi\).
#+end_lemma

#+begin_proof
Order the finitely satisfiable supersets of \(\Sigma\) by inclusion.
The union of a chain of them is finitely satisfiable, since a finite
subset of the union already lies in one member of the chain; and the
family is non-empty. Zorn's lemma gives a maximal element \(M\).

Not both \(\varphi\) and \(\neg \varphi\) lie in \(M\), since the finite
set \(\set{\varphi, \neg \varphi}\) is unsatisfiable. Suppose neither
does. By maximality \(M \cup \set{\varphi}\) and
\(M \cup \set{\neg \varphi}\) fail to be finitely satisfiable, so there
are finite \(A, B \subseteq M\) with \(A \cup \set{\varphi}\) and
\(B \cup \set{\neg \varphi}\) unsatisfiable. But \(A \cup B\) is a
finite subset of \(M\), hence satisfied by some \(v\); and \(v\)
satisfies \(\varphi\) or \(\neg \varphi\), contradicting one of the two.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: lem-lindenbaum, prop-induction-on-formulas
#+NAME: thm-propositional-compactness
#+begin_theorem
A set of propositional formulas is satisfiable if and only if it is
finitely satisfiable.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
One direction is trivial. Let \(\Sigma\) be finitely satisfiable and
let \(M \supseteq \Sigma\) be maximal finitely satisfiable, as in the
lemma. Define \(v(p) = 1\) exactly when \(p \in M\). We show by
induction on formulas that \(v \models \varphi\) if and only if
\(\varphi \in M\).

For a variable this is the definition of \(v\). For a negation:
\(v \models \neg \varphi\) means \(v \not\models \varphi\), which by
induction means \(\varphi \notin M\), which by the lemma means
\(\neg \varphi \in M\). For a conjunction, suppose first
\((\varphi \wedge \psi) \in M\); if \(\varphi \notin M\) then
\(\neg \varphi \in M\), and \(\set{(\varphi \wedge \psi), \neg \varphi}\)
is an unsatisfiable finite subset of \(M\) -- so \(\varphi \in M\), and
likewise \(\psi \in M\), whence \(v \models \varphi \wedge \psi\) by
induction. Conversely if \(\varphi, \psi \in M\) but
\((\varphi \wedge \psi) \notin M\), then
\(\neg(\varphi \wedge \psi) \in M\) and
\(\set{\varphi, \psi, \neg(\varphi \wedge \psi)}\) is an unsatisfiable
finite subset. By the induction principle the equivalence holds for
every formula, so \(v\) satisfies every member of \(M\), in particular
every member of \(\Sigma\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The theorem is where the axiom of choice enters logic: for an
uncountable set of variables the proof above is Zorn's lemma, and the
statement is in fact equivalent to a weak form of choice (the ultrafilter
lemma). Its consequences are the ones that make model theory possible --
a property of arbitrary structures that holds in all finite
approximations holds outright.
#+end_remark
