#+TITLE: Conics and quadrics: classification by signature
#+DATE: [2026-08-10 Mon]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:geometry:linear_algebra:
#+MSC: 51N20, 15A63
#+HASH: acf0be70cbe6

An ellipse, a hyperbola and a pair of lines are given by equations of
the same shape and are told apart by no property of the equation that
survives a change of coordinates -- except one. Sylvester's law supplies
it: the signature of the associated symmetric form is a complete
invariant, so the classification of quadrics is linear algebra with a
homogenisation in front of it.

#+DEPENDS: def-bilinear-form, def-affine-space, def-projective-space
#+NAME: def-quadric
#+begin_definition
Let \(K\) be a field of characteristic \(\ne 2\). A /quadric/ in
\(\PP^{n}(K)\) is the zero set
\(Q = \setst{[x] \in \PP^{n}(K)}{q(x) = 0}\) of a non-zero quadratic
form \(q\) on \(K^{n+1}\); an /affine quadric/ in \(K^{n}\) is the zero
set of a polynomial of degree \(2\). The quadric is /non-degenerate/ if
the associated symmetric bilinear form is.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-quadric, prop-projective-space-is-a-quotient
#+NAME: prop-quadrics-are-well-defined
#+begin_proposition
The condition \(q(x) = 0\) does not depend on the representative of
\([x]\), so a quadric is a well-defined subset of projective space; and
two proportional forms define the same quadric.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
\(q(\lambda x) = \lambda^{2}q(x)\) by bilinearity of the associated
form, and \(\lambda \ne 0\), so \(q(\lambda x) = 0\) exactly when
\(q(x) = 0\). Multiplying \(q\) by a non-zero scalar does not change its
zero set.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-quadric, thm-diagonalisation-of-symmetric-forms, thm-sylvester-law-of-inertia
#+NAME: thm-projective-classification-of-quadrics
#+begin_theorem
Over \(\R\), every quadric in \(\PP^{n}(\R)\) is, in suitable
homogeneous coordinates,
\(x_{0}^{2}+\cdots+x_{p-1}^{2} - x_{p}^{2}-\cdots-x_{p+m-1}^{2} = 0\)
with \(p \ge m\) and \(p + m \le n+1\); and the pair \((p,m)\), up to
exchanging the two entries, is determined by the quadric together with
its defining form. Over an algebraically closed field the normal form is
\(x_{0}^{2}+\cdots+x_{r-1}^{2} = 0\), with \(r\) the rank.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
A change of homogeneous coordinates is an invertible linear change on
\(K^{n+1}\), so by the diagonalisation theorem \(q\) becomes a sum of
squares with coefficients, and over \(\R\) rescaling brings those to
\(\pm1\); Sylvester's law says \((p,m)\) does not depend on the
diagonalising basis. Replacing \(q\) by \(-q\), which defines the same
quadric, exchanges \(p\) and \(m\), and one may then assume
\(p \ge m\). Over an algebraically closed field every non-zero
coefficient has a square root, so all of them can be scaled to \(1\),
and only the rank \(r = p+m\) remains.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-projective-classification-of-quadrics, def-affine-subspace, prop-affine-charts
#+NAME: thm-affine-conics
#+begin_theorem
Every affine conic in \(\R^{2}\) is, after an affine change of
coordinates, one of: \(x^{2}+y^{2}=1\), \(x^{2}-y^{2}=1\),
\(y = x^{2}\), \(x^{2}+y^{2}=0\), \(x^{2}-y^{2}=0\), \(x^{2}=1\),
\(x^{2}=0\), or the empty set \(x^{2}+y^{2}=-1\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Write the equation as \(q(v) + \ell(v) + c\) with \(q\) a quadratic
form, \(\ell\) linear and \(c\) constant. By the diagonalisation
theorem, a linear change of coordinates makes \(q\) diagonal with
entries in \(\set{1,-1,0}\). In each coordinate where the coefficient of
the square is non-zero, completing the square absorbs the corresponding
linear term into a translation; this leaves an equation in which the
linear part involves only the coordinates absent from \(q\).

If \(q\) has rank \(2\) the linear part is gone and, rescaling, the
equation is \(\pm x^{2}\pm y^{2} = c\) with \(c \in \set{1,0,-1}\) after
dividing by \(\abs{c}\) when \(c \ne 0\); the listed cases are what
remains after exchanging the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) and multiplying by
\(-1\). If \(q\) has rank \(1\), say \(q = x^{2}\), the surviving linear
term is \(\beta y\): for \(\beta \ne 0\) a translation and a rescaling
of \(y\) give \(y = x^{2}\), and for \(\beta = 0\) the equation is
\(x^{2} = c\), giving \(x^{2}=1\) or \(x^{2}=0\) after rescaling (the
case \(c<0\) being empty and already listed). The form \(q\) is non-zero
by hypothesis, so its rank is not \(0\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Projectively the list collapses: ellipse, parabola and hyperbola are the
same non-degenerate conic, and they differ only in how the line at
infinity meets it -- not at all, tangentially, or in two points. That is
the standard demonstration that a classification depends on which group
of transformations is allowed, and the affine list above is the
projective one refined by the choice of a line to call infinite.
#+end_remark
