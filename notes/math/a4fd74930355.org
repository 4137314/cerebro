#+TITLE: Cosets, the index and Lagrange's theorem
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:group_theory:
#+MSC: 20A05, 20D60
#+HASH: a4fd74930355

A subgroup cuts the group into translated copies of itself, all of the
same size. That single observation is the whole of Lagrange's theorem,
and it is the reason finite group theory is a subject with arithmetic in
it: the order of a subgroup, and hence the order of every element,
divides the order of the group before any structure is examined.

#+DEPENDS: def-subgroup, def-equivalence-relation
#+NAME: def-coset
#+begin_definition
Let \(H \le G\) and \(a \in G\). The /left coset/ of \(H\) by \(a\) is
\(aH = \setst{ah}{h \in H}\), and the /right coset/ is
\(Ha = \setst{ha}{h \in H}\). The set of left cosets is written
\(G/H\).
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-coset, thm-equivalence-partition
#+NAME: prop-cosets-partition
#+begin_proposition
The relation \(a \sim b\) defined by \(a^{-1}b \in H\) is an equivalence
relation on \(G\) whose classes are the left cosets of \(H\). In
particular the left cosets partition \(G\), and \(aH = bH\) if and only
if \(a^{-1}b \in H\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Reflexivity is \(a^{-1}a = e \in H\); symmetry follows because
\((a^{-1}b)^{-1} = b^{-1}a\) and \(H\) is closed under inverses;
transitivity because \(a^{-1}c = (a^{-1}b)(b^{-1}c)\) and \(H\) is
closed under products. The class of \(a\) is
\(\setst{b}{a^{-1}b \in H} = \setst{ah}{h \in H} = aH\). The classes of
an equivalence relation partition the set, and two classes coincide
exactly when their representatives are related.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-coset, prop-bijective-iff-invertible
#+NAME: prop-cosets-are-equinumerous
#+begin_proposition
For every \(a \in G\) the map \(h \mapsto ah\) is a bijection
\(H \to aH\); so all left cosets of \(H\) have the same cardinality as
\(H\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
It is surjective by the definition of \(aH\) and injective because
\(ah = ah'\) gives \(h = h'\) on multiplying by \(a^{-1}\). Its inverse
is \(x \mapsto a^{-1}x\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: prop-cosets-partition, prop-cosets-are-equinumerous
#+NAME: def-index
#+begin_definition
The /index/ \([G : H]\) of \(H\) in \(G\) is the cardinality of
\(G/H\), the number of distinct left cosets.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-index, prop-cosets-partition, prop-cosets-are-equinumerous
#+NAME: thm-lagrange
#+begin_theorem
If \(G\) is a finite group and \(H \le G\), then
\(\abs{G} = [G : H] \cdot \abs{H}\). In particular \(\abs{H}\) divides
\(\abs{G}\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
The left cosets partition \(G\), so \(\abs{G}\) is the sum of their
cardinalities; there are \([G : H]\) of them and each has \(\abs{H}\)
elements.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-lagrange, prop-order-and-powers
#+NAME: cor-order-of-an-element-divides
#+begin_corollary
In a finite group \(G\), the order of every element divides \(\abs{G}\),
and \(a^{\abs{G}} = e\) for all \(a \in G\).
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
The subgroup \(\langle a \rangle\) has exactly \(\ord(a)\) elements, so
\(\ord(a)\) divides \(\abs{G}\) by Lagrange. Writing
\(\abs{G} = \ord(a) k\) gives \(a^{\abs{G}} = (a^{\ord(a)})^{k} = e\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: cor-order-of-an-element-divides, thm-classification-of-cyclic-groups
#+NAME: cor-groups-of-prime-order
#+begin_corollary
A group of prime order \(p\) is cyclic, and every element other than
the identity generates it.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Take \(a \ne e\). Then \(\ord(a)\) divides \(p\) and is not \(1\), so it
is \(p\); hence \(\langle a \rangle\) has \(p\) elements and equals
\(G\). By the classification of cyclic groups \(G \iso \Z/p\Z\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
The converse of Lagrange's theorem is false -- a divisor of \(\abs{G}\)
need not be the order of a subgroup -- and repairing it partially is what
the Sylow theorems do. That the failure is only partial is why the
theorem above is a genuine constraint rather than a classification.
#+end_remark
