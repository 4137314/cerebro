#+TITLE: Cauchy's mean value theorem and de l'Hopital's rule
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26A24
#+HASH: 2bf288a8abef

The mean value theorem compares a function with the identity. Cauchy's
version compares two functions with each other, and that single change
of viewpoint is what makes an indeterminate quotient tractable: the
ratio of the increments becomes the ratio of the derivatives at one
common point. De l'Hopital's rule is that observation with a limit
taken.

#+DEPENDS: thm-rolle
#+NAME: thm-cauchy-mean-value
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f, g\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on
\((a,b)\). Then there is \(c \in (a,b)\) with
\[ \bigl( f(b) - f(a) \bigr) g'(c) = \bigl( g(b) - g(a) \bigr) f'(c). \]
If moreover \(g'\) never vanishes on \((a,b)\), then \(g(b) \ne g(a)\)
and the conclusion may be written as a ratio.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Put \(h(x) = (f(b) - f(a)) g(x) - (g(b) - g(a)) f(x)\). It is
continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and
\[ h(a) = f(b) g(a) - f(a) g(b) = h(b), \]
as a direct expansion of both sides shows. Rolle's theorem gives
\(c \in (a,b)\) with \(h'(c) = 0\), which is the claim. If \(g'\) never
vanishes then \(g(b) = g(a)\) would give, by Rolle applied to \(g\), a
zero of \(g'\); so \(g(b) \ne g(a)\) and one may divide.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: thm-cauchy-mean-value, def-function-limit
#+NAME: thm-lhopital
#+begin_theorem
Let \(f, g\) be differentiable on \((a, b)\) with \(g'\) nowhere zero
there, and suppose \(f(x) \to 0\) and \(g(x) \to 0\) as
\(x \to a^{+}\). If \(f'(x)/g'(x) \to L\) as \(x \to a^{+}\), then
\[ \lim_{x \to a^{+}} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = L. \]
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Extend \(f\) and \(g\) to \([a,b)\) by setting \(f(a) = g(a) = 0\);
the hypothesis on the limits makes both extensions continuous at
\(a\). Let \(\eps > 0\) and choose \(\delta > 0\) with
\(\abs{f'(t)/g'(t) - L} < \eps\) for \(a < t < a + \delta\). Fix \(x\)
with \(a < x < a + \delta\). On \([a, x]\) the Cauchy mean value
theorem applies and produces \(c \in (a, x)\) with
\[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{g(x) - g(a)} = \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}, \]
the denominators being non-zero because \(g'\) does not vanish. Since
\(c \in (a, a + \delta)\), the right-hand side is within \(\eps\) of
\(L\), hence so is \(f(x)/g(x)\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Two hypotheses are load-bearing and routinely dropped. First, the rule
concludes /from/ the limit of \(f'/g'\), so its failing to exist says
nothing: with \(f(x) = x^{2} \sin(1/x)\) and \(g(x) = x\) at the
origin, \(f/g \to 0\) while \(f'/g'\) oscillates. Second, \(g'\) must
not vanish, which is what lets a single \(c\) serve both increments.
The rule is a consequence of Cauchy's theorem, not a definition of the
quotient, and treating it as an algorithm is what produces the circular
proof that \(\sin(x)/x \to 1\).
#+end_remark
