#+TITLE: Monotonicity and the sign of the derivative
#+DATE: [2026-08-07 Fri]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:analysis:real_analysis:
#+MSC: 26A24, 26A48
#+HASH: ccdb4acc4a81

The derivative is defined pointwise and monotonicity is a statement
about pairs of points, so nothing connects them locally. The mean value
theorem is the bridge, and it is the only one: every "the function
increases because the derivative is positive" argument in the course is
this corollary, and the asymmetry in it -- the strict version goes one
way only -- is what the standard mistake gets wrong.

#+DEPENDS: thm-mean-value
#+NAME: cor-monotone-from-derivative
#+begin_corollary
Let \(f\) be continuous on an interval \(I\) and differentiable on its
interior. If \(f' \ge 0\) throughout the interior then \(f\) is
increasing on \(I\); if \(f' \le 0\) it is decreasing; and \(f' > 0\)
makes \(f\) strictly increasing.
#+end_corollary

#+begin_proof
Let \(x < y\) in \(I\). The mean value theorem on \([x,y]\) gives
\(c \in (x,y)\) with \(f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)(y - x)\). The factor
\(y - x\) is positive, so the sign of \(f(y) - f(x)\) is the sign of
\(f'(c)\): non-negative, non-positive or strictly positive according to
the hypothesis.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: cor-monotone-from-derivative
#+NAME: prop-strict-monotone-derivative-converse
#+begin_proposition
A differentiable strictly increasing function satisfies \(f' \ge 0\),
and this cannot be improved to \(f' > 0\).
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
For \(h > 0\) the quotient \((f(c+h) - f(c))/h\) is positive and for
\(h < 0\) it is again positive, so its limit \(f'(c)\) is
non-negative; a limit of positive numbers need not be positive, which
is exactly the loss. That the loss is real is shown by \(f(x) = x^{3}\),
strictly increasing on \(\R\) with \(f'(0) = 0\).
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: cor-monotone-from-derivative, lem-interior-extremum
#+NAME: prop-first-derivative-test
#+begin_proposition
Let \(f\) be continuous at \(c\) and differentiable on
\((c - \delta, c) \cup (c, c + \delta)\) for some \(\delta > 0\). If
\(f' > 0\) on the left piece and \(f' < 0\) on the right, then \(f\) has
a local maximum at \(c\); with the signs reversed, a local minimum.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
On \((c - \delta, c]\) the function is continuous and has positive
derivative on the interior, so it is increasing there by the corollary,
giving \(f(x) \le f(c)\) for those \(x\). Symmetrically \(f\) is
decreasing on \([c, c + \delta)\), so \(f(x) \le f(c)\) there too.
Hence \(f(c)\) is a maximum over the whole neighbourhood. The other
case follows by applying this to \(-f\).
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Note which direction each statement runs. The interior extremum lemma
says a local extremum at an interior differentiable point forces
\(f'(c) = 0\); it does /not/ say a zero of \(f'\) is an extremum, and
\(x^{3}\) at the origin is again the counterexample. The first
derivative test above is the converse that is actually true, and it
asks for the sign of \(f'\) on both sides rather than its value at the
point.
#+end_remark
