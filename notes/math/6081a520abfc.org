#+TITLE: Euclidean domains, principal ideals and unique factorisation
#+DATE: [2026-08-09 Sun]
#+FILETAGS: :growing:algebra:commutative_algebra:
#+MSC: 13F07, 13G05
#+HASH: 6081a520abfc

Unique factorisation in \(\Z\) is usually proved from Euclid's lemma,
and Euclid's lemma from the division algorithm. Each of those steps is a
separate property of the ring, and separating them shows what the
theorem costs: division gives principal ideals, principal ideals make
irreducible elements prime, and primality gives uniqueness. The same
chain applies verbatim to polynomials over a field.

#+DEPENDS: def-integral-domain, def-ideal
#+NAME: def-euclidean-domain
#+begin_definition
An integral domain \(R\) is a /Euclidean domain/ if there is a function
\(\delta\) from \(R \setminus \set{0}\) to \(\N\) such that for all
\(a, b \in R\) with \(b \ne 0\) there are \(q, r\) with \(a = qb + r\)
and either \(r = 0\) or \(\delta(r) < \delta(b)\). It is a /principal
ideal domain/ (PID) if every ideal is principal.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-euclidean-domain
#+NAME: thm-euclidean-is-a-pid
#+begin_theorem
Every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain. In particular
\(\Z\) is one, and so is \(K[x]\) for a field \(K\).
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
Let \(I \ne \set{0}\) be an ideal and choose \(b \in I \setminus \set{0}\)
with \(\delta(b)\) least. For \(a \in I\) write \(a = qb + r\); then
\(r = a - qb \in I\), and \(r \ne 0\) would contradict minimality since
\(\delta(r) < \delta(b)\). So \(a \in (b)\) and \(I = (b)\); the zero
ideal is \((0)\). For \(\Z\) take \(\delta(n) = \abs{n}\), and for
\(K[x]\) take \(\delta = \deg\), the division theorem applying to every
non-zero divisor because a field allows scaling to a monic polynomial.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-integral-domain, def-prime-and-maximal
#+NAME: def-irreducible-and-prime
#+begin_definition
Let \(R\) be a domain and \(p\) a non-zero non-unit. Then \(p\) is
/irreducible/ if \(p = ab\) forces \(a\) or \(b\) to be a unit, and
/prime/ if \(p \mid ab\) implies \(p \mid a\) or \(p \mid b\) --
equivalently, if the ideal \((p)\) is prime.
#+end_definition

#+DEPENDS: def-irreducible-and-prime
#+NAME: prop-prime-implies-irreducible
#+begin_proposition
In an integral domain every prime element is irreducible.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \(p\) be prime and \(p = ab\). Then \(p \mid ab\), so \(p\) divides
one factor, say \(a = pc\). Then \(p = pcb\), and cancelling \(p\) in
the domain gives \(1 = cb\), so \(b\) is a unit.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-irreducible-and-prime, def-euclidean-domain, thm-maximal-iff-quotient-is-a-field, cor-maximal-implies-prime
#+NAME: prop-irreducible-is-prime-in-a-pid
#+begin_proposition
In a principal ideal domain every irreducible element is prime.
#+end_proposition

#+begin_proof
Let \(p\) be irreducible; \((p)\) is proper because \(p\) is not a unit.
If \((p) \subseteq J = (c) \subseteq R\), then \(p = cd\), so by
irreducibility either \(c\) is a unit, giving \(J = R\), or \(d\) is a
unit, giving \(J = (p)\). Hence \((p)\) is maximal, therefore prime, and
\(p\) is a prime element.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: def-euclidean-domain, def-ideal
#+NAME: lem-pid-has-no-infinite-ascending-chain
#+begin_lemma
In a principal ideal domain there is no strictly increasing infinite
chain of ideals \(I_{1} \subsetneq I_{2} \subsetneq \cdots\).
#+end_lemma

#+begin_proof
Let \(U = \bigcup_{n} I_{n}\). It is an ideal: two of its elements lie
in a common \(I_{n}\), so their difference does, and absorption is
inherited. Being a PID, \(U = (c)\) for some \(c\), and \(c\) lies in
some \(I_{N}\); then \(U = (c) \subseteq I_{N} \subseteq U\), so the
chain is constant from \(N\) on.
#+end_proof

#+DEPENDS: lem-pid-has-no-infinite-ascending-chain, prop-irreducible-is-prime-in-a-pid, prop-prime-implies-irreducible
#+NAME: thm-pid-is-a-ufd
#+begin_theorem
In a principal ideal domain every non-zero non-unit is a product of
irreducible elements, and the factorisation is unique up to the order of
the factors and multiplication by units.
#+end_theorem

#+begin_proof
*Existence.* Suppose some non-zero non-unit \(a\) admits no such
factorisation. Then \(a\) is not irreducible, so \(a = a_{1}b_{1}\) with
both factors non-units, and at least one of them -- call it \(a_{1}\) --
again admits no factorisation. Since \(b_{1}\) is not a unit,
\((a) \subsetneq (a_{1})\). Iterating produces a strictly increasing
chain \((a) \subsetneq (a_{1}) \subsetneq (a_{2}) \subsetneq \cdots\),
contradicting the lemma.

*Uniqueness.* Let \(p_{1}\cdots p_{m} = q_{1} \cdots q_{n}\) with all
factors irreducible, and induct on \(m\). Then \(p_{1}\) is prime and
divides the right-hand product, so it divides some \(q_{j}\); after
reordering, \(q_{1} = up_{1}\), and \(u\) is a unit because \(q_{1}\) is
irreducible and \(p_{1}\) is not a unit. Cancelling \(p_{1}\) leaves
\(p_{2}\cdots p_{m} = (u q_{2}) q_{3} \cdots q_{n}\), where \(uq_{2}\)
is again irreducible. By induction \(m - 1 = n - 1\) and the remaining
factors match up to order and units.
#+end_proof

#+begin_remark
Each implication in the chain is strict. There are principal ideal
domains that are not Euclidean, and unique factorisation domains that
are not principal -- \(\Z[x]\) is one, since the ideal generated by
\(2\) and \(x\) is not principal. So the theorem is a sufficient
condition, and the reason \(\Z\) and \(K[x]\) behave alike is that both
sit at the top of the chain rather than that unique factorisation is
common.
#+end_remark
