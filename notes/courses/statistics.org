* Description

A first rigorous course in mathematical statistics: the statistical
model as a family of probability measures, estimation and its two error
criteria, sufficiency and the reduction of data, the two theorems that
bound how good an estimator can be, the Neyman-Pearson theory of
testing, and the linear model. The recurring theme is that every
optimality statement in the subject is a geometric one in \(L^{2}\):
Rao-Blackwell, Cramer-Rao and Gauss-Markov are Pythagoras, Cauchy-Schwarz
and Pythagoras again.

* Objectives

Having read it, one can:
- set up a statistical model and distinguish a statistic from an
  estimator;
- decompose a mean squared error into variance and bias, and argue about
  the trade-off between them;
- recognise a sufficient statistic by the factorisation theorem, and
  prove sufficiency from the definition;
- state and prove the Rao-Blackwell theorem, and explain why
  sufficiency is exactly the hypothesis it needs;
- derive the Cramer-Rao bound and say which regularity conditions it
  rests on and what happens without them;
- construct a most powerful test for two simple hypotheses and prove its
  optimality;
- fit a linear model by least squares and prove the Gauss-Markov theorem.

* Schedule

1. Statistical models, samples, statistics and estimators; bias,
   variance and mean squared error; consistency.
2. Sufficiency; the factorisation theorem.
3. Improving an estimator: the Rao-Blackwell theorem.
4. Fisher information; the Cramer-Rao lower bound.
5. Hypothesis testing: the two errors, size and power; the
   Neyman-Pearson lemma.
6. The linear model: least squares, the normal equations, the
   Gauss-Markov theorem.
