Real analysis

The largest part of the archive, and the one whose order matters most: almost every note here is used by the next. The sequence below is that order. It has one turning point – the completeness axiom – and everything before it is definitions, everything after it is consequences.

mentioned in Analysis

Part of Analysis.

The line

Supremum and infimum is the definition that makes the axiom statable; completeness of the real numbers is the axiom, and the only thing separating from . Its first two consequences are the Archimedean property and the density of the rationals; the absolute value and the triangle inequality is the estimate every later proof splits through.

Sequences

Limit of a sequence, then the algebra of limits, which is what makes limits computable. Then the three ways completeness produces one: monotone convergence, Bolzano-Weierstrass and the Cauchy criterion – the last being the one that mentions no order and therefore travels to metric spaces. Limit superior and limit inferior exist for every bounded sequence, which is why they are the right tool when convergence is what one is trying to prove.

Series

Series and convergence of the partial sums reduces series to sequences, and everything after it is a criterion: the Cauchy criterion, the harmonic series and condensation, absolute convergence and the comparison test, the ratio and root tests. The alternating series test is the one that uses cancellation rather than absolute values, and hence the source of every conditionally convergent example. Power series and the radius of convergence is where series stop being numbers and become functions.

Continuity

Limits and continuity and the limit of a function and its sequential characterisation – the second is what lets every theorem about sequences be spent twice. Then the algebra of continuous functions and composition, one-sided limits and the discontinuities of a monotone function.

The three theorems on an interval are where completeness reappears: the intermediate value theorem, continuous functions on a compact interval (bounded, and attaining their bounds), and uniform continuity and the Heine-Cantor theorem. The continuous image of an interval and the continuous inverse is the pair of statements that becomes, in topology, "connectedness and compactness are preserved by continuous maps".

Differentiation

The derivative and the rules of differentiation, then the one theorem the subject rests on: the mean value theorem. Its consequences are monotonicity and the sign of the derivative, Cauchy's mean value theorem and de l'Hopital's rule, and convex functions and the second derivative.

Taylor's theorem appears twice on purpose, because the two remainders answer different questions: the Peano remainder is a local statement about the order of the error, the Lagrange remainder a global one with a value, and uniform bounds on the Taylor remainder is what turns either into a convergent series.

Integration

The Riemann integral by upper and lower sums; linearity, additivity and monotonicity; monotone functions are integrable, and the mean value theorem for integrals. The fundamental theorem of calculus is the statement that the two operations of the subject are inverse, and integration by parts and substitution is that theorem read backwards as a technique.

Limits of functions, and several variables

Pointwise and uniform convergence is the note that separates the two modes and explains which theorems survive each. In several variables, differentiability is defined by approximation by a linear map – not by partial derivatives, which is the distinction the subject exists to make – and extrema and the second differential is the second-order test. The Legendre transform is filed here for its convexity, and used in mechanics.

Beyond one variable

The second-year continuation is filed under Analysis rather than here: the inverse and implicit function theorems, Lagrange multipliers, Fubini's theorem, and the divergence and Stokes theorems.

What is missing

Rearrangement of series; the Riemann criterion in terms of the measure of the discontinuity set, which would connect this subject to measure theory; and the elementary functions – the exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions are used throughout the archive and defined nowhere in it.
