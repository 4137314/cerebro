Functional analysis

Functional analysis is linear algebra on spaces where the unit ball is not compact. Every difference from the finite-dimensional theory traces back to that one fact, and every theorem below is a substitute for something compactness used to provide: a basis, a limit, a bound, a separating functional.

The dividing line

Normed spaces and the equivalence of norms in finite dimension is the note that draws it: in finite dimension all norms are equivalent, the sphere is compact, subspaces are closed, and linear maps are automatically continuous. Each of those four statements fails in infinite dimension, and the subject is the list of what replaces them.

The replacements

  • For a separating functional: the Hahn-Banach theorem, which extends a dominated functional off a subspace by Zorn's lemma. It is the reason the dual space is large enough to be useful.
  • For a bound: uniform boundedness, open mapping and closed graph – three theorems, one proof scheme, all resting on Baire's theorem and therefore on completeness.
  • For a convergent subsequence: the Arzela-Ascoli theorem, which says what has to be added to boundedness – equicontinuity – to recover it in a space of functions.
  • For coordinates: Hilbert spaces, where an inner product survives and with it orthogonal projection, the representation of every functional, and orthonormal bases.

Why Hilbert space is the easy case

The orthogonal decomposition of a finite-dimensional inner product space carries over verbatim once the subspace is closed, because the nearest point in a closed convex set still exists – and that argument uses the parallelogram identity, which is exactly what a norm coming from an inner product has and a general norm does not. Everything special about Hilbert spaces is downstream of that identity.

What is missing

spaces, which are the examples the general theory was built for and which need measure theory to define; weak topologies and the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, which is where Tychonoff pays off; spectral theory for operators, of which only the finite-dimensional symmetric case is present; compact operators; and Fourier analysis, which would be the first serious use of orthonormal bases.
