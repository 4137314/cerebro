General topology

The axioms of a topological space are three closure conditions on a family of sets, and they are deliberately far too weak: almost every theorem here needs an extra hypothesis, and the subject is largely the study of which hypothesis buys what. The two that recur are compactness and separation.

The language

Topological spaces, bases and generated topologies, then interior, closure, boundary and density – all defined by intersections, so that they exist before anything is constructed. Continuous maps, homeomorphisms and topological invariants fixes what the subject is about, and subspace, product and quotient topologies defines the three constructions by the maps they must make continuous rather than by a formula – which is why their proofs are three lines each.

Convergence without sequences

In a general space sequences are too few to detect the closure. Nets repair this by replacing with an arbitrary directed set, and filters by dropping the index set altogether; the two are the same content in two notations, and the ultrafilter version is what makes Tychonoff's theorem a short proof.

The two big hypotheses

  • Compactness: compactness and the Heine-Borel theorem, and its arbitrary products in Tychonoff's theorem. Local compactness and the one-point compactification is how a space that lacks it is given it for the price of a single point.
  • Separation: Hausdorff spaces make limits unique and compact sets closed; regular and normal spaces, and Urysohn's lemma go further and produce continuous functions, which a topology otherwise need not have; the Tietze extension theorem extends them off a closed subspace.

Connectedness, components and path-connectedness is the third invariant, and the one that generalises the intermediate value theorem.

Metric spaces

The special case where a distance generates the topology, and the reason the general theory looks the way it does. Metric spaces and completeness and the metric topology set up the translation; total boundedness and compactness in metric spaces proves that the three notions of compactness agree here and nowhere else; uniform continuity, extension and completion shows that every metric space embeds densely in a complete one. The two theorems that make completeness pay are the contraction mapping principle and the Baire category theorem.

What is missing

Metrisation theorems – the conditions under which a topology comes from a metric – which is the natural endpoint of the separation and countability axioms; the countability axioms themselves; uniform spaces; paracompactness and partitions of unity, which is what geometry needs from this subject; and function spaces with the compact-open topology.
