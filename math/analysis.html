Analysis

Analysis is one subject because it has one hypothesis: completeness. Every existence theorem below – a limit, a supremum, a root, a fixed point, an integral, a solution of a differential equation – is completeness of some space, applied to a sequence built for the purpose. Everything else is an estimate.

The subjects

  • Real analysis – the line, sequences and series, continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, uniform convergence. The bulk of the archive, and what everything later abstracts.
  • Complex analysis – the same definition of the derivative in the plane, and a subject that behaves nothing like the real one.
  • Functional analysis – the same arguments with the points replaced by functions, where the unit ball is no longer compact.
  • Partial differential equations – the three model equations, each met through the statement that decides uniqueness for it.

Two smaller threads have no hub of their own yet, and are listed here so they are not lost:

  • Measure and integration: sigma-algebras and measures, the Lebesgue integral up to the convergence theorems, and the completeness of – which is where this thread meets functional analysis and probability.
  • Differential equations and variations: existence and uniqueness for ordinary differential equations, and the action functional and the first variation; also Weierstrass approximation.

Where completeness enters, in order

  1. As an axiom about , from which the supremum exists at all.
  2. As monotone convergence and Bolzano-Weierstrass, the two ways of producing a limit from a bound.
  3. As the Cauchy criterion, which is the form that survives the loss of the order and hence generalises to metric spaces.
  4. As compactness, once "complete and totally bounded" is recognised as the same hypothesis; and as Baire's theorem, which is what makes completeness pay in infinite dimension.

Read that list backwards and it is the history of the subject: the theorems came first, the hypothesis was isolated afterwards.

The two integrals

The Riemann integral partitions the domain and integrates what is nearly continuous; the Lebesgue integral partitions the range and integrates what is measurable. The whole difference is visible in the convergence theorems: Riemann needs uniform convergence to exchange limit and integral, Lebesgue needs only a dominating function. Neither subsumes the other in this archive – the Riemann theory is where the fundamental theorem of calculus lives, and the Lebesgue theory has no construction of its measure yet.

Calculus in several variables

The second-year material, which is where analysis stops being about one variable: the inverse and implicit function theorems – the contraction principle in disguise, and the licence for every coordinate change – constrained extrema and Lagrange multipliers, product measures, Tonelli and Fubini, and the two theorems that turn a derivative over a region into data on its boundary: line integrals and Green's theorem and the divergence and Stokes theorems.

Harmonic analysis

Fourier series are an orthonormal expansion in , so Parseval's identity is free once Fejer's theorem supplies completeness; the Fourier transform does the same on the line, with inversion and Plancherel; and Sturm-Liouville problems are what separation of variables produces.

What is missing

spaces for and the duality that goes with them; the construction of Lebesgue measure, which is used throughout and built nowhere; distributions; the spectral theorem for unbounded or compact operators, which is what the eigenfunction expansions here are quietly assuming.
