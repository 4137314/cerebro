Logic

Logic begins when a formula stops being notation for an argument and becomes an object a theorem can quantify over. From that point the subject has a fixed shape: define what it means for a formula to be true in a structure, define separately what it means to be derivable, and then compare the two. Everything below is that comparison, or a consequence of it.

Propositional logic

Propositional logic: formulas, assignments and compactness gives the semantics – two inductive definitions, one building formulas and one evaluating them – and the theorem that makes the subject infinite rather than finitary: satisfiability is decided by finite subsets.

A proof system: soundness and the deduction theorem gives the syntax, as a finite combinatorial object, and the easy half of the comparison. Completeness of propositional logic gives the hard half, by building a model out of the syntax itself – after which compactness reappears as a corollary, for the reason that a derivation is finite.

First-order logic

First-order languages, structures and satisfaction makes " satisfies the group axioms" a mathematical statement, and with it statements about all structures satisfying a theory. Ultraproducts and the theorem of Los then prove compactness at this level without a proof system at all, by assembling a model out of a family of models along an ultrafilter – the only property used being that "large" is closed under complement, which is exactly what negation needs.

Elementary substructures and the Lowenheim-Skolem theorems say how little a first-order theory controls: every infinite structure has a countable elementary substructure and arbitrarily large elementary extensions, so no such theory pins down the size of its models.

The limits

Computability, the halting problem and Rice's theorem is the other kind of limitative result: not "the theory has too many models" but "no procedure decides this". The two undecidability proofs there use only two properties of any reasonable model of computation – a program can simulate a program, and a parameter can be frozen – which is why the conclusions do not depend on the machine chosen.

What is missing

Godel's completeness theorem for first-order logic, whose consequence was obtained here by ultraproducts instead; the incompleteness theorems, and with them arithmetic as an object of study; quantifier elimination and the model theory of specific theories; intuitionistic and modal logic; and the connection between computability and definability.
