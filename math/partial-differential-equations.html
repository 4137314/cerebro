Partial differential equations

There is no theory of partial differential equations, only theories of particular ones, and the division that organises them is by how information travels. Three model equations exhaust the second-order linear cases, and each is met here through the statement that decides uniqueness for it – a maximum principle, an explicit formula, an energy identity.

The four notes

  • First-order equations and the method of characteristics – the equation constrains the derivative along one direction, so following that direction reduces it to an ordinary equation. Where the data surface is characteristic, the problem is ill posed, and this is the cleanest place to see why.
  • The heat equation (parabolic) – separation of variables and the maximum principle. Instant smoothing forwards, ill posedness backwards, both readable in one exponential factor.
  • The wave equation (hyperbolic) – d'Alembert's formula, finite propagation speed, and the energy identity that gives uniqueness with no formula at all.
  • Harmonic functions and the Dirichlet problem (elliptic) – the mean value property, the maximum principle, and the rigidity that makes harmonic functions analytic.

Two techniques, used throughout

Separation of variables produces a Sturm-Liouville problem whose eigenfunctions are the building blocks, and reassembling the solution needs their completeness – which is stated there and not proved. The Fourier transform does the same job on the whole line, turning differentiation into multiplication and the equations into algebra.

What is missing

Everything modern: weak solutions and Sobolev spaces, which is where the subject actually lives; existence for the Dirichlet problem, of which only uniqueness is proved here; Green's functions and fundamental solutions; the classification of second-order equations by their principal symbol; and nonlinear equations of any kind. What is present is the classical core – the three model equations and what can be said about them with the Fubini theorem and the fundamental theorem of calculus.
