Rings and commutative algebra

A ring has two operations, and the whole subject is about their interaction – which is why the substructure one may divide by is not a subring but an ideal, absorption rather than closure being what the quotient needs. After that the questions are arithmetic: does factorisation exist, is it unique, and is every ideal finitely generated.

The basic constructions

Rings, ideals and the quotient ring; then integral domains, fields, prime and maximal ideals, where a property of an ideal is read off from its quotient – prime means the quotient is a domain, maximal means it is a field. That dictionary is what makes the subject geometric, and the Chinese remainder theorem is its first pay-off: comaximal ideals split a ring into a product.

Modules, quotients and exact sequences repeat the vector-space theory over a ring, and record what is lost: without inverses a spanning set need not contain a basis, so dimension disappears and exact sequences take its place as the bookkeeping.

Factorisation

Polynomial rings, division and roots gives the division algorithm, and with it the two facts everything else uses: the degree is additive over a domain, and a polynomial of degree has at most roots. Euclidean domains, principal ideals and unique factorisation then separates the three hypotheses that are usually run together – Euclidean gives principal, principal makes irreducible elements prime, primality gives uniqueness – and the chain is strict at every step.

Finiteness

Noetherian rings and the Hilbert basis theorem isolate the ascending chain condition, which was already doing the work in the existence half of unique factorisation, and prove that adjoining a variable preserves it. The consequence – any system of polynomial equations is equivalent to a finite subsystem – is what makes the objects of algebraic geometry finite data.

What is missing

Localisation, which is the other half of the dictionary between rings and spaces; the Nullstellensatz; primary decomposition; integral extensions and the going-up theorem; Dedekind domains, which is where this subject meets number theory; and the structure theorem for modules over a principal ideal domain.
