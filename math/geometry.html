Geometry

A geometry is fixed by choosing which transformations are allowed; its theorems are the statements invariant under them. That is the organising principle of everything below, and it is why the same object appears several times with different content: a conic is one thing projectively, three affinely, and infinitely many metrically.

The geometries, by the group allowed

  • Affine – affine spaces and affine maps: a vector space with the origin forgotten. Invariants: parallelism, ratios along a line, barycentres.
  • Euclidean – orthogonality and Gram-Schmidt and the isometries of , which are exactly the affine maps with orthogonal linear part. Invariants: length and angle.
  • Projective – projective space and its charts and the cross-ratio. Adding one point per direction removes the exception from "two lines meet"; four points on a line have exactly one invariant, and that is the whole of the metric content that survives.
  • Convex – convex sets, the separating hyperplane theorem, Caratheodory's theorem and extreme points. Not invariance under a group but a condition on a set, and the one part of geometry that is also optimisation.
  • Differential – curves, surfaces and smooth manifolds. Here the allowed transformations are the diffeomorphisms, and the invariants are found by differentiating.

Classification is linear algebra

Twice in this area a geometric classification is settled by a normal form for a symmetric bilinear form. Sylvester's law of inertia classifies conics and quadrics by signature, and the spectral theorem diagonalises the shape operator of a surface, producing the principal curvatures. The two are different theorems on purpose: Sylvester allows any change of basis and can preserve only signs, the spectral theorem allows only orthonormal ones and preserves the eigenvalues. Which one applies is exactly the question of which group the geometry is over.

Where geometry meets the rest

Minkowski's convex body theorem proves statements about integers by choosing a lattice and a symmetric body – geometry used as number theory. The tangent space is defined algebraically, as derivations of the ring of smooth functions, so that it needs no coordinates. And canonical transformations are the symplectic geometry of mechanics.

What is missing

Riemannian metrics and geodesics; the Theorema Egregium, which is stated as a remark and not proved; differential forms and Stokes' theorem; curvature of manifolds rather than of surfaces in ; algebraic geometry beyond the projective linear case – there are no varieties and no Nullstellensatz, although the Hilbert basis theorem is in place; and hyperbolic geometry.
