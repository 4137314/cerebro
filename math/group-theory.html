Group theory

A group is one associative, invertible operation and nothing else, which is little enough that the axioms fit on a line and strong enough that finite groups have an arithmetic. The subject splits cleanly in two: what a single group is made of, and how a group acts on something else – with the second usually answering questions about the first.

The definitions

Binary operation, then group and abelian group; uniqueness of the identity element is the first thing the axioms imply, and the reason one may say the identity. Group homomorphism is the definition that makes the rest of the subject possible: a group is studied through the maps out of it.

Inside one group

Subgroups, the subgroup criterion and generation; the order of an element and cyclic groups, the one family that is classified completely. Then the two structural theorems: cosets, the index and Lagrange's theorem – a subgroup cuts the group into equal pieces, so sizes divide – and normal subgroups and the quotient group, which identifies the subgroups one may divide by as exactly the kernels. The isomorphism theorems are the consequences.

Acting on a set

Group actions, orbits and the orbit-stabiliser theorem converts counting problems into index computations, and it is the engine of everything that follows: Cauchy's theorem and the Sylow theorems are four applications of one fixed-point lemma. The symmetric group and the sign of a permutation is where a group acts on a polynomial to produce an invariant, and the orthogonal group is where a group is met as symmetries in the first place.

Building and taking apart

Direct and semidirect products say when a group is assembled from two of its subgroups, and show that a normal subgroup and its quotient do not determine it. In the other direction, composition series, Jordan-Holder and solvable groups give a well-defined list of simple factors. Free groups and presentations is the opposite move again: a group named by generators and relations, with nothing else assumed.

What is missing

The classification of finitely generated abelian groups; the simplicity of for , which is what makes the insolubility of the quintic a theorem rather than a hope; group actions on themselves beyond the class equation; representation theory; and the Galois correspondence, which is the reason most of this exists.
