Algebra

Algebra studies sets with operations, and it studies them through the maps that respect the operations rather than through the elements. That choice is what makes the subject repeat itself: the same three theorems – a quotient exists, a homomorphism factors through it, the pieces reassemble – are proved once for groups, once for rings, once for vector spaces, once for modules, with the proofs differing only in what has to be checked to be well defined.

The subjects

  • Group theory – one operation, invertible. The smallest structure in which anything can be undone, and the language of symmetry.
  • Rings and commutative algebra – two operations, with the interaction between them; ideals, factorisation, and the chain conditions.
  • Field theory – rings in which division is always possible, and the extensions that adjoin the roots a polynomial lacks.
  • Linear algebra – a field acting on an abelian group. The one case that is completely understood, and therefore the model for the rest.

The one theorem, four times

The canonical factorisation of a map says every function is a quotient followed by an injection. Adding structure to the map turns it into the first isomorphism theorem: for groups, for rings, for vector spaces (where it acquires a dimension count) and for modules. In each case the work is not the theorem but the identification of which substructures can be divided out – normal subgroups, two-sided ideals, subspaces, submodules – and that identification is where the four subjects genuinely differ.

What the structure buys

  • Counting. Lagrange's theorem and the orbit-stabiliser theorem turn questions about a finite group into arithmetic, and the Sylow theorems into a classification tool.
  • Factorisation. Euclidean domain, principal ideal domain, unique factorisation domain is a chain of strictly weakening hypotheses, and it explains why and behave alike.
  • Finiteness. The Hilbert basis theorem says that adjoining a variable preserves the ascending chain condition, so a system of polynomial equations is always a finite one.
  • Non-existence. The tower law is a multiplicative constraint on degrees, and the classical impossibility proofs are that constraint and nothing else.

What is missing

Galois theory, which is the point at which solvable groups and splitting fields – both present – become one subject; representation theory; homological algebra beyond exact sequences; the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, which would subsume both the classification of finite abelian groups and the Jordan normal form; tensor products; and category theory, which the repetitions above are asking for.
