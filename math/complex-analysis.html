Complex analysis

One hypothesis – that a difference quotient converges along every direction of the plane rather than along a line – and the subject becomes rigid in a way real analysis never is. A function differentiable once is differentiable infinitely often; its values on a small arc determine it everywhere; it cannot have an interior maximum of modulus; and a single number per singularity computes an integral. Each of those is a corollary of the same integral formula.

The chain

  1. Holomorphic functions and the Cauchy-Riemann equations – what complex differentiability asks for, as two equations on the real and imaginary parts.
  2. Complex integration and Cauchy's theorem – the integral over a closed curve vanishes. Goursat's proof assumes only differentiability, which is what lets the next step prove more than it assumes.
  3. The Cauchy integral formula and analyticity – values inside a disc from values on the circle, hence power series, hence Liouville's theorem and the fundamental theorem of algebra.
  4. Zeros, the identity theorem and the maximum principle – the two rigidity statements.
  5. Laurent series and isolated singularities – the expansion adapted to an annulus, and the trichotomy removable / pole / essential.
  6. The residue theorem and the argument principle – what the integral is when there is something inside, and how to count zeros.

Why the subject is a tool

The residue theorem turns contour integration into arithmetic, which is why complex analysis is the working language of mathematical physics: real integrals with no elementary primitive are evaluated by closing a contour, and the poles of a transfer function decide whether a system is stable, by Rouche's theorem. In the other direction, harmonic functions in the plane are exactly the real parts of holomorphic ones, so the Dirichlet problem in two dimensions is complex analysis.

What is missing

Conformal mapping and the Riemann mapping theorem, which is the geometric half of the subject and the reason it solves plane boundary value problems; analytic continuation and Riemann surfaces; the Poisson kernel; infinite products and the elementary special functions – the gamma and zeta functions in particular, with which this would begin to touch number theory; and Picard's theorems, of which only the Casorati-Weierstrass shadow is here.
