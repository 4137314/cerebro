Statistics

Probability computes forwards, from a law to the behaviour of a sample; statistics computes backwards, from a sample to the law. The formal difference is a single quantifier – the measure is not given but ranges over a family – and it changes what a theorem can say: no procedure is right, so every result is a bound on how wrong one can be, or a proof that no procedure does better.

Estimation

Statistical models, estimators and consistency sets up the vocabulary and proves the two things one can say about an estimator with no further hypotheses: the mean squared error splits into variance and squared bias, and the sample mean is consistent by the law of large numbers.

Then two theorems that say where the good estimators are and how good they can be:

  • Sufficiency and the factorisation theorem – a statistic that keeps everything relevant to the parameter, recognised by inspecting how the density depends on it.
  • The Rao-Blackwell theorem – conditioning any unbiased estimator on a sufficient statistic never makes it worse. The proof is Pythagoras in , which is why conditional expectation had to be built geometrically first.
  • Fisher information and the Cramer-Rao bound – a floor on the variance of every unbiased estimator, depending on the model alone.

Read together they are one argument: improve towards a sufficient statistic, and stop when the Cramer-Rao floor is reached.

Decisions

Hypothesis tests and the Neyman-Pearson lemma is the other half of the subject: two kinds of error that cannot both be made small, a convention that fixes one and minimises the other, and a complete answer for two simple hypotheses – reject where the likelihood ratio is large.

Linear models

Least squares and the Gauss-Markov theorem is where statistics is linear algebra: the fitted values are an orthogonal projection, the normal equations are that projection in coordinates, and optimality among linear unbiased estimators follows from Pythagoras again. Notably no distributional assumption is used.

What is missing

Maximum likelihood, its equivariance and its asymptotic normality – the single largest gap, and the method most used in practice; the Lehmann-Scheffe theorem, which turns Rao-Blackwell into a uniqueness statement; confidence intervals and their duality with tests; the chi-squared, and distributions, which need the normal theory of quadratic forms; Bayesian inference; and nonparametric methods.
