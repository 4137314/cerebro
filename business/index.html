Organization PDF

Foundations PDF

  • Organizations, resources and performance definition
    An organization deliberately divides and coordinates work to reach objectives; effectiveness compares actual with expected results, efficiency compares outputs with inputs.
  • Ownership, liability and governance definition
    Partnerships give partners unlimited liability, corporations limit it to the capital invested; governance assigns roles to the shareholders' meeting, board, statutory auditors, CEO and general manager.

Structures PDF

  • Processes and coordination definition
    The process logic groups activities into cross-functional key processes owned by a process owner and aimed at value perceived by the customer; coordination mechanisms reconnect divided work.
  • Simple, functional, divisional and matrix structures definition
    The four consolidated structure models group work by entrepreneur, input (function), output (division) or both (matrix); each trades efficiency, effectiveness and coordination differently.

Theories PDF

  • Taylor, Fayol and Weber definition
    The classical school (deterministic, focused on the subject) explains performance through scientific task design (Taylor), administrative management (Fayol) and legal-rational bureaucracy (Weber).
  • Contingency and organizational fit definition
    Contingency theory (1960–1970) says there is no one best organization: the right structure depends on environment, strategy, technology and size.
  • Human relations, motivation and leadership definition
    The human-relations school (Mayo, Hawthorne) and motivational theories (Maslow, Herzberg) look at the individual's needs; Likert classifies four leadership styles from authoritarian to participative.
  • Transaction costs and make-or-buy definition
    Williamson's transaction cost economics (1985) chooses between market, hybrid forms and hierarchy by minimizing transaction costs under bounded rationality, opportunism and transaction frequency.

Strategy PDF

Competitive Strategy PDF

  • Strategy, objectives and competitive advantage definition
    Strategy links external opportunities with internal resources; it answers "where to compete" (corporate) and "how to compete" (business), and evolved from the S-C-P paradigm to Porter's Value School and the resource-based view.
  • Porter: the five competitive forces model
    Industry profitability depends on rivalry among existing firms, the bargaining power of suppliers and buyers, the threat of new entrants and the threat of substitutes.
  • Cost leadership, differentiation and focus definition
    Two sources of advantage (low cost, differentiation) times two scopes (whole industry, one segment) give Porter's generic strategies; being stuck in the middle is a strategic error.
  • Resources, capabilities and competitive advantage definition
    The resource-based view sees the firm as a heterogeneous bundle of resources and competences; capabilities (organizational routines) turn scarce, durable, non-transferable and non-replicable resources into sustained advantage.
  • The value chain and strategic groups definition
    Strategic groups map competitors on the variables that separate them; the value chain breaks a business unit into primary and support activities so that the margin (value minus cost) can be traced to each activity.

Marketing PDF

Market Analysis PDF

  • BCG, Ansoff and the business portfolio definition
    The BCG matrix classifies business units by market growth and relative share to balance cash flows; Ansoff's matrix crosses existing/new products and markets to choose a growth path.
  • Customer value and the marketing process definition
    Marketing creates value for customers and builds profitable relationships to capture value in return; it starts from needs, wants and demand and splits into strategic (analysis) and operational (action) marketing.
  • Market segmentation definition
    Segmentation splits a market into groups homogeneous inside and heterogeneous outside; segments are found with mathematical or heuristic methods such as successive eliminations, and lead to undifferentiated, differentiated or concentrated marketing.
  • Targeting, SWOT and GE-McKinsey definition
    Targeting evaluates each segment's attractiveness (external: demand, PESTLE, five forces) and the firm's competitiveness (internal: value chain); SWOT summarizes them and the GE-McKinsey 3×3 matrix turns weighted scores into investment decisions.
  • Positioning, perception maps and service gaps definition
    Positioning decides how to compete in the chosen segment — customer-perceived value relative to price and to competitors — using perception maps, Slack's importance–performance matrix and SERVQUAL gap analysis.

Marketing Mix PDF

  • The marketing mix and product decisions definition
    The 4P (product, price, place, promotion) are the tactical levers that deliver value to the target; product extends to service and experience, the brand carries equity, channels can be direct, indirect, vertical or horizontal, and promotion is a coordinated mix of five tools.
  • Pricing and contribution definition
    Price is the only revenue-generating P; it lies between a cost floor and a perceived-value ceiling, adjusted to competition, with launch strategies (skimming, penetration), product-mix pricing and adjustment manoeuvres.

Product Development PDF

  • New product development and Stage-Gate definition
    New product development is a process from concept to final design (Stage-Gate) defining concept, package and process; launches fail for predictable reasons, and modularity, commonality and concurrent engineering reduce cost and time.
  • Functional analysis, FAST and value definition
    Value is the ratio between satisfaction of needs and resources used (UNI EN 12973); value analysis is a team-based, functional and economic method organized in a five-phase job plan, and FAST diagrams order functions with How?/Why? logic.
  • QFD, FMEA and design improvement definition
    QFD (house of quality) translates weighted customer "whats" into technical "hows"; FMEA links each failure mode to its causes and effects and to prevention, detection and recommended actions.

Procurement PDF

Purchasing PDF

  • Industrial purchasing and the buying process definition
    Industrial (B2B) purchasing is professional, interactive and relationship-based; its six phases go from specifications to supplier evaluation, the first three being strategic and the last three operational.
  • Buying situations and the Buygrid model definition
    Robinson, Faris and Wind (1967) classify purchases as new task, modified rebuy or straight rebuy by novelty and uncertainty, and describe eight buying phases whose importance depends on the class.
  • The profit impact of purchasing savings definition
    Because material cost reductions raise the margin and cut inventory at the same time, a small purchasing saving can raise ROA more than a large sales increase; 1/ROS measures the leverage.
  • Total cost of ownership method
    TCO compares suppliers on all costs of buying, using and disposing of an item over its life — pre-transaction, transaction and post-transaction — not only the purchase price.
  • Vendor grids and weighted supplier ratings method
    A vendor grid scores each supplier on weighted product and supplier criteria; the weighted sum ranks offers, as in Enel's vendor-rating index.

Supplier Management PDF

  • ABC analysis of purchasing spend method
    ABC (Pareto) classification sorts suppliers by annual spend to find the few critical ones: class A ≈ 80% of value, B ≈ 15%, C ≈ 5%.
  • Single, sole, dual and multiple sourcing definition
    Single sourcing uses one replaceable supplier, sole sourcing one monopolist, dual sourcing two parallel sources and multi-sourcing many; the trend is fewer suppliers and closer relations, supported by TQM and JIT.
  • Fujimoto and buyer-supplier dependence definition
    Fujimoto crosses single/multi sourcing with dedicated/independent suppliers; Scott and Westbrook's power matrix places each case in interdependence, supplier dominance, buyer dominance or independence.
  • Kraljic and the supplier portfolio model
    Kraljic (1983) classifies purchase categories by strategic importance and difficulty of managing the supply situation into non-critical, leverage, bottleneck and strategic items, each with its own sourcing policy; Olsen and Ellram add relationship analysis and action plans.

Operations PDF

Process Design PDF

  • Operations, the four Vs and performance objectives definition
    Operations management designs, plans, controls and improves the processes that transform inputs into goods and services; operations differ by volume, variety, variation and visibility, and are judged on quality, speed, dependability, flexibility, cost and sustainability.
  • The volume-variety matrix model
    Process types follow the volume–variety diagonal: project, jobbing, batch, mass (line) and continuous for manufacturing; professional services, service shops and mass services for services.
  • Fixed-position, functional, cellular and line layouts definition
    Layout is the physical arrangement of resources; fixed-position, functional, cellular and line layouts fit increasing volume and decreasing variety.
  • The order decoupling point, BOM, MRP and ERP definition
    The decoupling point separates forecast-driven (push) from order-driven (pull) activities, giving MTS, ATO, MTO, PTO and ETO; the bill of materials, MRP and ERP turn demand into material and production plans.

Quality PDF

  • Quality, service gaps and quality costs definition
    Quality can be defined in five ways (Garvin) plus the gap view, has several dimensions, is certified by ISO 9001, and has prevention, appraisal, internal and external failure costs whose optimum shifts towards prevention in the total-quality view.
  • Process variation and control charts definition
    Natural process variation often follows a normal distribution; control limits at ±3σ separate random from assignable causes, and capability Cp compares the specification range with 6σ.
  • TQM, continuous improvement and BPR definition
    Breakthrough improvement (BPR) makes big, abrupt, technology-driven changes; continuous improvement (TQM, Lean, Six Sigma) makes small steps with everyone; each approach mixes emphasis on solutions vs methods and rapid vs gradual change.
  • Six Sigma and DMAIC method
    Six Sigma (Motorola, 1980s) reduces variation to near-zero defects (3.4 DPMO); it uses customer-driven goals, the DMAIC cycle, evidence-based tools and a belt organization.

Lean PDF

  • Lean, waste and value-stream mapping definition
    Lean (Toyota Production System) pursues zero waste, zero defects and low inventory through respect for people; seven wastes are attacked with value-stream mapping, which classifies activities as VA, NVA or BNVA and measures process efficiency.
  • 5S, production leveling and improvement routines definition
    5S organizes the workplace (sort, set in order, shine, standardize, sustain); heijunka levels volume and mix; SMED shortens set-ups; gemba walks put managers where the work is.
  • Push, pull, JIT and Kanban definition
    JIT synchronizes production with demand; push plans work on forecasts, pull triggers it from actual consumption; Kanban signals authorize upstream production; lower inventory exposes problems and speeds defect detection.

Digitalization PDF

  • Bounded rationality and digital transformation definition
    Operations decisions are made under uncertainty and bounded rationality, often on tacit experience; digitalization makes information and rules explicit, Industry 4.0 technologies support or automate decisions, as in the BLM and Novotic cases.

Supply Chain PDF

  • Supply-chain integration and global performance definition
    Supply-chain management integrates suppliers, plants, warehouses and stores so goods are produced and distributed in the right quantities, places and times at minimum system cost; it faces conflicting objectives, complexity and uncertainty.
  • Fisher: efficient and responsive supply chains model
    Fisher (1997) matches functional products (predictable demand) with physically efficient (lean) chains and innovative products (unpredictable demand) with market-responsive (agile) chains; mismatches destroy value.
  • The bullwhip effect definition
    Order variability is amplified moving upstream in the supply chain; it raises production, inventory, transport costs and lead times, and is reduced by sharing demand information, shorter lead times, collaborative forecasting, VMI and supply contracts.

Accounting PDF

Financial Statements PDF

  • Financial statements and accrual accounting definition
    The financial statements represent the firm's assets, financing and economic result at the end of a period; drafted with prudence, accrual and consistency, they consist of the balance sheet, income statement and explanatory notes, linked through profit and equity.
  • Accruals and deferrals across periods definition
    Ratei record amounts that belong to the current period but are settled later; risconti defer amounts already settled that belong to later periods; both enforce the accrual principle for time-based items.
  • The balance sheet and the accounting equation definition
    The balance sheet lists assets (fixed and current) against liabilities and equity; every transaction keeps assets equal to liabilities plus equity, as the Inventata sas walk-through shows.
  • Depreciation and carrying amount definition
    Depreciation spreads the cost of a fixed asset over its useful life; with the straight-line method the annual charge is (cost − residual value)/life and the carrying amount falls by that charge every year.
  • Income, inventory and operating subtotals definition
    The statutory income statement runs from production value (A) and production costs (B) to financial items, value adjustments and taxes; the value-added reclassification derives value added, EBITDA, EBIT, pre-tax and net result.

Financial Analysis PDF

  • Liquidity, working capital and treasury margin definition
    The financial reclassification sorts uses (assets) and sources (liabilities and equity) by 12-month liquidity or maturity; treasury margin, net working capital and the acid-test ratio measure the ability to meet short-term obligations.
  • Funding structure and fixed-asset coverage definition
    The primary structure ratio (equity / fixed assets) measures self-coverage of long-term investments; the solidity ratio (liabilities / equity) measures dependence on external funding.
  • ROS, ROI, ROA, ROE and financing cost definition
    ROI measures operating return on invested capital, ROS return on sales, ROA on total assets, ROE on equity and ROD the cost of debt; ROI = ROS × turnover links margin and capital efficiency.

Projects PDF

Project Management PDF

  • Projects, planning and the WBS definition
    A project is a temporary, unique effort with defined objectives, schedule and budget; project management balances time, cost, resources and quality through planning, scheduling and control, starting from a work breakdown structure.
  • Critical path, float and crashing method
    CPM uses activities, durations and dependencies to compute earliest and latest times; the longest path is critical (zero float), float can level resources, and crashing buys time on critical activities at rising marginal cost.
  • Earned value and project variances definition
    Earned value compares planned cost of scheduled work (BCWS), budgeted cost of work performed (BCWP) and actual cost (ACWP) to separate schedule and cost deviations.
  • RACI, the project manager and the PMO definition
    Projects borrow resources from functions through a matrix; RACI assigns who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed; the project manager coordinates resources towards time, cost and quality goals; the PMO governs the project portfolio.
