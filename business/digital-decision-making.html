Bounded rationality and digital transformation

Operations decisions are made under uncertainty and bounded rationality, often on tacit experience; digitalization makes information and rules explicit, Industry 4.0 technologies support or automate decisions, as in the BLM and Novotic cases.

Definition

Uncertainty: lack of available information relative to what is needed to complete a task (Tushman and Nadler 1987); decisions rely on forecasts with errors. Absolute rationality (homo economicus) assumes perfect logic; real decision makers have limited information (attention, memory, understanding, communication), cannot foresee consequences, cannot control all behaviour. Bounded rationality (Herbert Simon): decisions are satisficing, not optimal. Tacit knowledge: experience-based, hard to formalize — a senior technician "hearing" a machine about to fail, tricks of the trade, knowing whom to involve outside the org chart. Limits of experience-based decisions: low replicability, dependence on key people, limited use of large data, cognitive biases, hard systematic learning, low transparency, high variability of outcomes, poor handling of complexity.

Digitalization: using digital technologies to improve processes — a change in how information, activities and decisions are organized, not just software or replacing people. Prerequisites: reliable data, integrated information systems, understandable processes, adequate skills; otherwise technology amplifies existing problems. Manage the balance standardization vs flexibility: decide what to formalize and what to leave to human judgement. Decision support: responsibility stays with the person interpreting the output. Decision automation: needs controls, thresholds, accountability, error handling; the more critical, ambiguous or irreversible the decision, the more human supervision and explainability. When to digitalize: frequent, repetitive but complex processes; many simultaneous variables; high cost of error; need for traceability; critical cross-functional coordination; knowledge concentrated in few people. Harder when data are scarce/unreliable, the process keeps changing, or judgement is unobservable. Impacts: operational efficiency (automation, ERP), strategic effectiveness (forecasting, BI), better products/services (connected products), better customer experience (virtual assistants).

Industry 4.0

1.0 (1780) mechanization, steam → 2.0 (1870) electricity, mass production → 3.0 (1970) computers, automation → 4.0 (2011, German government) cyber-physical systems, IoT → 5.0 (2020) mass customization, human–cyber-physical systems. Enabling technologies: big data and analytics, AI, cloud, augmented reality, industrial IoT, 3D printing, robots, digital twins/simulation, blockchain. IIoT reduces uncertainty on machine state; digital twins simulate scenarios; AI predicts and prioritizes; cloud enables collaboration; blockchain gives traceability and trust; AR supports remote assistance and training. Across the value chain: e.g. procurement → analytics on suppliers, AI negotiation, blockchain controls, cloud platforms.

Worked example

Adige BLM Group (Levico, tube and sheet-metal machines): lots of data in fragmented databases (SAP, C4C), decisions by experience. Project BIG: AI demand forecasting with historical and market data → accuracy 84%, 92% and 88% on three product lines (LT7, LT-FIB, LT8), better production planning. Project SARA: Llama-based AI agent with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) searching assembly instructions; solutions found are added to its database. Novotic (Rovereto, custom robotic cells): Forge assistant (Microsoft Graph, SharePoint, RAG) to reuse documents for offers; AI quality control classifying assembly curves; DataBlade turning DXF drawings into features for quoting; a transformer-based API proposing corrective straightening sequences — an operational decision-support system.

Pitfall

Data availability does not guarantee better decisions (AI can invent analytics). Digitalization without process clarity amplifies chaos.

Recall
