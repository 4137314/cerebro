ROS, ROI, ROA, ROE and financing cost

ROI measures operating return on invested capital, ROS return on sales, ROA on total assets, ROE on equity and ROD the cost of debt; ROI = ROS × turnover links margin and capital efficiency.

Invested capital must be defined explicitly: a common operating convention is operating assets minus non-interest-bearing operating liabilities. ("Assets minus all liabilities" equals equity, which is not an operating IC.) ROD with all liabilities understates the rate paid on interest-bearing debt.

Worked example

Course example: revenue 3,000,000; operating costs 1,500,000; depreciation 200,000 → RO = 1,300,000. Total assets 4,500,000; current liabilities 1,200,000; short-term financial liabilities 300,000 (interest-bearing, so added back): invested capital = 4,500,000 − 1,200,000 + 300,000 = 3,600,000.

(The printed calculation shows 3,000,000 and ROI 43.33% — an arithmetic slip: the stated items sum to 3,600,000.) Interpretation: each euro invested yields about 36 cents of operating income. Simple case: sales 1,000, EBIT 100, IC 500, total assets 800, equity 300, net income 60 → ROS 10%, turnover 2, ROI 20%, ROA 12.5%, ROE 20%. Link with purchasing: see purchasing leverage (ROA = ROS × asset turnover).

Pitfall

High ROE can come from leverage, not operations. ROI can rise with unchanged ROS if turnover rises. State whether averages or closing balances are used.

Recall

Write the five ratios; decompose ROI; redo the course example with the correct invested capital.

Picture

Profitability comes from margins (ROS) and from capital efficiency (turnover).
