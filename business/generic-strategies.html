Cost leadership, differentiation and focus

Two sources of advantage (low cost, differentiation) times two scopes (whole industry, one segment) give Porter's generic strategies; being stuck in the middle is a strategic error.

Model

Competitive advantage arises from the value a firm creates for its customers. Value is what buyers are willing to pay; superior value comes from offering the same benefits at lower prices (cost leadership) or unique benefits that more than offset a higher price (differentiation).

Cost leadership rests on a more efficient structure; differentiation on a generic feature valued by customers (e.g. brand); cost focus on an exclusive cost source; differentiation focus on an inimitable element (e.g. hand-made).

Cost leadership

Goal: become the lowest-cost producer in the industry, serving many segments. Sources: low wages, economies of scale, money-saving technologies and processes, privileged access to raw materials, automation, eliminating services the customer sees as marginal (free drinks on flights). Three instruments: process innovation (less scrap, materials, labour, stock, lead and set-up time), removal of useless costs along the value chain, outsourcing of non-core activities. Cases: IKEA — huge retail volumes, flat-pack furniture designed for self-assembly (cheaper transport), customers pick up, pay, transport and assemble → prices about 20% below competitors. Southwest Airlines — no meals, short turnaround, smaller crew, no seat assignment. Wal-Mart — purchasing power, cheap locations, inventory IT. Toys "R" Us — "category killer". Caution: a cost leader must keep parity or proximity in differentiation; Texas Instruments left digital watches despite cost leadership. Several firms all chasing lowest cost → savage competition (airlines).

Differentiation

Be unique on attributes important to customers, rewarded with a premium price. Instruments: better perceived quality (Caterpillar: quality and service), innovative product, product integrated with a service (home delivery), innovative marketing (Intel's "Crush" campaign against Motorola and Zilog). Cases: Smart, Ferrari, fashion brands, five-star hotels. The differentiator must keep cost parity or proximity. Several differentiation strategies can coexist.

Focus

Choose a narrow segment or niche (typical of SMEs) that broad competitors serve badly: cost focus exploits different cost behaviour in the segment; differentiation focus exploits special needs (adventure tourism).

Stuck in the middle

Not choosing a strategy means remaining "stuck in the middle", unable to gain any specific advantage. A firm cannot pursue more than one generic strategy unless: competition is also stuck in the middle; costs are strongly driven by market share and interrelations among businesses; or the firm achieves a non-imitable innovation. "Everything for everyone" is the recipe for strategic mediocrity.

Worked example

US airline industry, strategic groups on cost per ASM × quality (AQR): differentiation group American, United, Delta (high quality); cost leadership America West (low cost, low quality); focus Southwest; stuck in the middle TWA, Northwest, USAir, Continental. See strategic groups.

Pitfall

Low price is not the strategy; low cost is. Focus is defined by the narrow scope, then split in cost or differentiation.
