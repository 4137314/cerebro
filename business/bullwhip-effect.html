The bullwhip effect

Order variability is amplified moving upstream in the supply chain; it raises production, inventory, transport costs and lead times, and is reduced by sharing demand information, shorter lead times, collaborative forecasting, VMI and supply contracts.

Definition

The bullwhip effect: orders to the supplier fluctuate more than sales to the customer, and the distortion propagates upstream in amplified form. Like the "butterfly effect": a small change downstream becomes a large swing upstream. It is a supply-chain inefficiency; information plays the critical role. Uncertainty sources beyond demand: lead times, production yields, transport times, natural disasters, component availability.

Model

Effects on performance: total profit falls; production costs rise (excess capacity or stock to absorb swings); inventory costs rise; replenishment lead times lengthen, with shortages when capacity and stock cannot cover orders; transport costs rise (excess transport capacity).

Countermeasure How it helps
E-commerce, internet upstream sees accurate demand; shorter order
  processing
Express delivery shorter lead time, less demand variance
Collaborative forecasting one shared forecast of end-customer demand
Vendor-managed inventory supplier sees downstream demand directly, not
  only orders
Supply contracts aligned incentives, agreed supply limits

Typical causes (standard literature): demand forecast updating at each stage, order batching, price fluctuations and promotions, rationing and shortage gaming.

Worked example

Weekly consumer demand 100, 100, 120, 100. The retailer, reading +20% as a trend and covering two weeks of lead time, orders 100, 100, 160, 80; the wholesaler, doing the same on the retailer's orders, orders 100, 100, 220, 40; the factory sees swings of ±120 from a ±20 change. Sharing point-of-sale data lets every stage plan on the true 100–120.

Pitfall

Bullwhip is not caused by end-customer volatility alone: it is created by the chain's own decision rules and lead times.
