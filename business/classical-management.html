Map of organizational theories

Early theories arise with the Industrial Revolution and the factory system. Theories can study the subject (people inside structures) or systems (entities formed by interactions), with a deterministic approach (economic, bureaucratic or administrative rationality) or a possibilistic one (individual motivations, perceptions, behaviour).

Focus Deterministic Possibilistic
Subject Classical school Motivational; organizational action
Systems Functionalism, neo-institut. Contingency; transaction costs

The classical school contains three theories: Taylor's scientific management, Fayol's administrative theory, Weber's bureaucratic theory.

Taylor — Scientific Management (1911)

Born in North America, aimed at the industrial workforce. Four principles:

  1. development of knowledge on a scientific basis: break down and measure the content of each job; workers' know-how is formalized into rules, laws and objective formulas;
  2. scientific selection of the workforce: know skills, knowledge, output, limits and potential;
  3. training and improvement of workers on scientific bases (examine → instruct → train);
  4. cooperation between managers and workforce: workers execute predetermined rules with will and determination; employers judge fairly.

In each principle the movement is from empirical to scientific organization. Operating mechanisms: time and methods study, standardization of operations, material flow design, functional structure, principle of exception (only exceptions go up the hierarchy), defined task paid by piece rate (cottimo), production cost calculation. Iconic image: the Ford assembly line. Critique: based on technological and scientific constraints; ignores human, social and relational aspects; the organization is not a linear, planned machine; unpredictability requires learning and adaptation.

Fayol — Administrative theory (1841–1925)

French, interested in the managerial class; makes direction scientific: forecasting, organizing, commanding, coordinating and controlling. Six functions of the enterprise:

Function Content
Technical production, transformation
Commercial purchasing, sales
Security protection of goods and people
Administrative accounting, financial statements, inventory, costs, stats
Financial raising and managing capital
Directive planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, control

Principles: centralization (division of work, discipline, unity of command, unity of direction); order (hierarchy); equity, subordination of the particular interest to the general one; stability and cohesion of personnel; initiative. He proposes scientific criteria for organizing work and the management bodies of the firm.

Weber — Bureaucratic theory

Bureaucracy is the administrative apparatus for exercising legal authority. Elements: division of labour regulated by general rules; hierarchy of offices; general rules governing decisions and actions; uniform behaviour; impersonal internal and external relations. Weber studies the structure of authority and bureaucracy.

Example

Situation Classical tool
repetitive assembly with high variance Taylor: time and methods study
a launch needing several functions aligned Fayol: unity of direction
traceable spending in public procurement Weber: rules, authorization limits

Pitfall

Taylor's unit of analysis is the task/worker, Fayol's the whole firm's management, Weber's the authority structure. Do not credit Taylor with participative management: his "cooperation" means executing scientific rules.
