Supply-chain integration and global performance

Supply-chain management integrates suppliers, plants, warehouses and stores so goods are produced and distributed in the right quantities, places and times at minimum system cost; it faces conflicting objectives, complexity and uncertainty.

Definition

SCM ensures the firm is supplied with what it needs to deliver what it sells: source (procurement) → make (operations) → deliver (logistics) → return (reverse logistics: collect, transport, sort for remanufacturing, refurbishing, reuse, recycling or disposal — key for the circular economy). "A supply chain consists of all parties involved, directly or indirectly, in fulfilling a customer request" (Chopra and Meindl 2004): manufacturers, suppliers, carriers, warehouses, retailers, customers; flows of products, money, information and services. Goal: efficient integration of suppliers, plants, warehouses and stores so goods are made and distributed in the right quantities, at the right places, at the right time, to minimize total system cost while meeting service requirements.

Model

Complexity: Boeing 787

About 2.3 million parts; some (fuselage) made in-house, others (landing gear) by suppliers; 5,400 supplier plants including sub-suppliers; over 750 million parts and assemblies bought in 2012; 500,000 people along the chain; rigorous incoming inspection.

Key outcomes (Melnyk et al. 2010)

Outcome Content
Cost cost reduction, efficiency, quality, delivery
Responsiveness flexibility to demand, mix, volume, location changes
Security physical security, traceability, fraud, safety
Sustainability green chain, emissions, resources, social compliance
Resilience risk management, continuity, fast recovery
Innovation new products and processes, innovative collaboration

Reference model: SCOR (plan, source, make, deliver, return).

Conflicting objectives

Unit Wants
Purchasing stable volumes, flexible delivery times, little mix
  variation, large quantities
Manufacturing long runs, high quality and productivity, low cost
Warehousing low stock, low transport cost, fast replenishment
Customers short lead times, high availability, huge variety, low
  prices

Optimization is hard: complex system, conflicting goals, dynamic, shifting power (e.g. HTC), changing over time.

Tools

Global optimization: decision support systems, inventory control, network design, design for logistics, cross docking (Walmart: goods move from inbound to outbound trucks without storage), strategic alliances, supply contracts and incentives, VMI (vendor-managed inventory). Managing uncertainty: pull systems, risk pooling, centralization, postponement, alliances, collaborative forecasting. See bullwhip and Fisher.

Worked example

Dell direct model: customer orders online → Dell hubs fed by suppliers and local suppliers → build to order. Challenges: no scale economies vs rivals, costly local production, growing complexity, falling corporate sales, need to strengthen B2C, Dell handles every request and failure (cost centre), less innovation → Dell later added retail channels. Barilla: 2020 goals — double revenue, cut supply-chain CO₂ by 50%; integrated procurement with DSS and supply-chain contracts linking seed suppliers, farmers and producer organizations to Barilla. New trends: global competition, offshoring and re-shoring, shorter life cycles, low-cost channels, stronger customers, e-business, sustainability and reverse chains.

Pitfall

SCM is not only logistics: information flows matter as much as goods. Local optima (cheapest purchase, longest run) can raise total system cost.

Recall

Define SCM and its goal; list SCOR processes, six outcomes and the conflicting objectives table; explain cross docking and VMI.

Picture

Goods flow downstream; information, money and returns flow upstream.
