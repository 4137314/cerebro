Push, pull, JIT and Kanban

Definition

Just-in-time: synchronize the production flow with demand — produce at the pace of customer demand, mixed-model (heijunka), small lots and short set-ups (SMED), pull instead of push, Kanban, make to order. Minimizes inventory and exposes quality and process problems.

Push Pull
based on forecasts, before real demand based on actual demand, after seeing it
work planned and pushed through each work pulled through the system by real

Model

Kanban

Visual signal (card, container, digital) controlling flow with pull logic:

  1. the downstream process (assembly) consumes a part;
  2. this generates a Kanban signal;
  3. the signal goes upstream;
  4. upstream produces only the quantity requested;
  5. repeat.

Aims: avoid over-production, reduce WIP, improve flow, make problems visible. Production is triggered by real consumption, not forecasts. Number of Kanbans (common sizing rule):

D demand rate, L lead time, s safety factor, C container size.

Lower inventory means better quality

Eight units in the queue between two stations: a defect is discovered after 7 minutes (information turnaround time ITAT = 7 × 1 min). With two units: ITAT = 2 minutes → fewer defective units produced before feedback. River-and-rocks: inventory is the water level; lowering it reveals the rocks — machine breakdowns, out-of-spec materials, quality problems, workload imbalances, worker absenteeism, material shortages.

Worked example

D = 400 parts/day, L = 0.5 day, s = 10%, C = 20 parts: cards. Cutting lead time to 0.25 day halves the cards (and WIP) to 5.5 → 6.

Pitfall

Pull does not mean "no forecast at all" for capacity planning; it means execution is triggered by consumption. Removing stock without fixing the rocks just stops the line.

Recall

Contrast push and pull; explain Kanban's five steps; compute ITAT for two queue sizes; draw the river and rocks.
