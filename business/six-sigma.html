Six Sigma and DMAIC

Six Sigma (Motorola, 1980s) reduces variation to near-zero defects (3.4 DPMO); it uses customer-driven goals, the DMAIC cycle, evidence-based tools and a belt organization.

Definition

Born at Motorola in the 1980s against quality problems; reduce variability to "almost zero defects". Four meanings: a vision (highest quality at lowest cost), a metric (99.9997% performance), a benchmark (vs best in class), an application of statistical tools to measure, analyse, improve and control.

Model

Elements: customer-driven objectives, structured improvement cycle, process redesign, evidence-based problem solving, structured training and organization — master black belts, black belts, green belts.

PDCA (Deming) DMAIC Content
Plan Define identify problem, define requirements, set goal
  Measure gather data, refine problem, measure inputs and
    outputs
  Analyse develop hypotheses, find root causes
Do, Check Improve develop and test ideas, implement, validate
Act Control set standards, deal with new problems

Root-cause tools: flow charts, scatter diagrams, 5 whys, cause–effect (Ishikawa, fishbone) diagrams, Pareto diagrams. Ishikawa example — toner cartridges returned (HP): people (dealer managers, training, expectation setting, complacency), systems (price protection, warranty, bulk returns), materials (faulty or empty cartridge, broken tab, non-HP refilled, damaged product), processes (instructions, obsolescence, manufacture, faint print).

Worked example

5 whys: machine stopped → fuse blew → overload → bearing not lubricated → lubrication pump worn → no preventive maintenance schedule (root cause). A process with 500 defects per million is between 4σ and 5σ; reaching 6σ means cutting defects by more than 99%.

Pitfall

3.4 DPMO is not the normal tail beyond 6σ: it includes the 1.5σ shift. DMAIC is a project method; Six Sigma is not only a statistic.
