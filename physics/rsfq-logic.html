RSFQ logic: storing and moving flux quanta

In RSFQ a bit is the presence (1) or absence (0) of an SFQ pulse within a clock period; pulses propagate along Josephson transmission lines, are stored as circulating currents in loops with , and almost every gate is clocked (gate-level pipelining); DC bias current makes static power dominate, which ERSFQ/eSFQ remove with inductive, junction-limited biasing.

Operation of the DFF (derivation sketch)

A loop of inductance between two junctions: an incoming pulse switches the input junction and leaves a circulating current (state "1") provided (), otherwise the flux escapes. The clock pulse adds current through the output junction: if a fluxon is stored the output junction exceeds and emits an SFQ pulse (and the loop resets); if not, the clock pulse escapes through a buffer junction and nothing is output (Likharev and Semenov 1991).

Timing

Every clocked gate needs setup/hold times (a few ps) between data and clock (same concepts as CMOS); clock skew is managed by concurrent or counter-flow clocking along pipelines; JTL delay ≈ 2–5 ps per stage. Clock rates of 20–50 GHz in practical circuits (770 GHz T flip-flop demonstrated).

Power

RSFQ: each junction is biased at ≈ 0.7 through a resistor from a low-voltage bus (a few mV), so static power dominates. In ERSFQ/eSFQ the resistors are replaced by inductors and current-limiting junctions, leaving only the dynamic dissipation per switching junction.

Worked example

Junction µA switching at 20 GHz: dynamic energy J, power nW per junction. A 10-junction circuit: 1 mW dynamic. Classic RSFQ biased from 2.6 mV through resistors with 0.7 I_c per junction: µA 2.6 mV mW static — 45 times more, which is why ERSFQ exists. Cooling cost at 4 K is ~400–1000 W per W, so the energy advantage over CMOS is real only for efficient designs.

Engineering applications

Digital RF receivers (direct digitisation of GHz signals), cryogenic qubit controllers (SFQ pulses can drive qubit rotations), superconducting AI accelerators and network switches, SQUID read-out.

Pitfall

SFQ logic is pulse-based, not level-based: a "1" exists only as a picosecond pulse within a clock period, and fan-out requires explicit splitters — CMOS design habits (wired fan-out, static levels) do not transfer.
