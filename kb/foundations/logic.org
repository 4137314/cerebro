#+title: Logic
#+summary: Logic begins when a formula stops being notation for an argument and becomes an object a theorem can quantify over. From that point the subject has a fixed shape: define what it means for a formula to be /true/ in a structure, define separately what it means to be /derivable/, and then compare the two. Everything below is that comparison, or a consequence of it.
#+date: [2026-08-10 Mon]

Part of [[id:foundations][Foundations]].

* Propositional logic

[[id:propositional-logic-formulas-assignments-and-compactness][Propositional logic: formulas, assignments and compactness]] gives the
semantics -- two inductive definitions, one building formulas and one
evaluating them -- and the theorem that makes the subject infinite
rather than finitary: satisfiability is decided by finite subsets.

[[id:a-proof-system-for-propositional-logic-soundness-and-the-deduction-theorem][A proof system: soundness and the deduction theorem]] gives the syntax,
as a finite combinatorial object, and the easy half of the comparison.
[[id:completeness-of-propositional-logic][Completeness of propositional logic]] gives the hard half, by building a
model out of the syntax itself -- after which compactness reappears as a
corollary, for the reason that a derivation is finite.

* First-order logic

[[id:first-order-languages-structures-and-satisfaction][First-order languages, structures and satisfaction]] makes "\(G\)
satisfies the group axioms" a mathematical statement, and with it
statements about /all/ structures satisfying a theory.
[[id:ultraproducts-and-the-theorem-of-los][Ultraproducts and the theorem of Los]] then prove compactness at this
level without a proof system at all, by assembling a model out of a
family of models along an [[id:filters-ultrafilters-and-the-ultrafilter-lemma][ultrafilter]] -- the only property used being
that "large" is closed under complement, which is exactly what negation
needs.

[[id:elementary-substructures-and-the-lowenheim-skolem-theorems][Elementary substructures and the Lowenheim-Skolem theorems]] say how
little a first-order theory controls: every infinite structure has a
countable elementary substructure and arbitrarily large elementary
extensions, so no such theory pins down the size of its models.

* The limits

[[id:computability-the-halting-problem-and-rices-theorem][Computability, the halting problem and Rice's theorem]] is the other kind
of limitative result: not "the theory has too many models" but "no
procedure decides this". The two undecidability proofs there use only
two properties of any reasonable model of computation -- a program can
simulate a program, and a parameter can be frozen -- which is why the
conclusions do not depend on the machine chosen.

* What is missing

Godel's completeness theorem for first-order logic, whose consequence
was obtained here by [[id:ultraproducts-and-the-theorem-of-los][ultraproducts]] instead; the incompleteness theorems,
and with them arithmetic as an object of study; quantifier elimination
and the model theory of specific theories; intuitionistic and modal
logic; and the connection between [[id:computability-the-halting-problem-and-rices-theorem][computability]] and definability.
