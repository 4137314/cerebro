#+title: Algebra
#+summary: Algebra studies sets with operations, and it studies them through the maps that respect the operations rather than through the elements. That choice is what makes the subject repeat itself: the same three theorems -- a quotient exists, a homomorphism factors through it, the pieces reassemble -- are proved once for groups, once for rings, once for vector spaces, once for modules, with the proofs differing only in what has to be checked to be well defined.
#+date: [2026-08-10 Mon]

Part of [[id:mathematics][Mathematics]].

* The subjects

- [[id:group-theory][Group theory]] -- one operation, invertible. The smallest structure
  in which anything can be undone, and the language of symmetry.
- [[id:rings-and-commutative-algebra][Rings and commutative algebra]] -- two operations, with the
  interaction between them; ideals, factorisation, and the chain
  conditions.
- [[id:field-theory][Field theory]] -- rings in which division is always possible, and the
  extensions that adjoin the roots a polynomial lacks.
- [[id:linear-algebra][Linear algebra]] -- a field acting on an abelian group. The one case
  that is completely understood, and therefore the model for the rest.

* The one theorem, four times

The [[id:maps-invertibility-and-the-canonical-factorisation][canonical factorisation of a map]] says every function is a quotient
followed by an injection. Adding structure to the map turns it into the
first isomorphism theorem: [[id:the-isomorphism-theorems-for-groups][for groups]], [[id:rings-ideals-and-the-quotient-ring][for rings]], [[id:linear-maps-quotient-spaces-and-the-rank-nullity-theorem][for vector spaces]]
(where it acquires a dimension count) and [[id:modules-quotients-and-exact-sequences][for modules]]. In each case
the work is not the theorem but the identification of which
substructures can be divided out -- [[id:normal-subgroups-and-the-quotient-group][normal subgroups]], [[id:rings-ideals-and-the-quotient-ring][two-sided
ideals]], subspaces, submodules -- and that identification is where the
four subjects genuinely differ.

* What the structure buys

- *Counting.* [[id:cosets-the-index-and-lagranges-theorem][Lagrange's theorem]] and the [[id:group-actions-orbits-and-the-orbit-stabiliser-theorem][orbit-stabiliser theorem]] turn
  questions about a finite group into arithmetic, and the [[id:cauchys-theorem-and-the-sylow-theorems][Sylow
  theorems]] into a classification tool.
- *Factorisation.* [[id:euclidean-domains-principal-ideals-and-unique-factorisation][Euclidean domain, principal ideal domain, unique
  factorisation domain]] is a chain of strictly weakening hypotheses, and
  it explains why \(\Z\) and \(K[x]\) behave alike.
- *Finiteness.* The [[id:noetherian-rings-and-the-hilbert-basis-theorem][Hilbert basis theorem]] says that adjoining a variable
  preserves the ascending chain condition, so a system of polynomial
  equations is always a finite one.
- *Non-existence.* [[id:field-extensions-the-tower-law-and-minimal-polynomials][The tower law]] is a multiplicative constraint on
  degrees, and the classical impossibility proofs are that constraint
  and nothing else.

* What is missing

Galois theory, which is the point at which [[id:composition-series-jordan-holder-and-solvable-groups][solvable groups]] and
[[id:splitting-fields-and-their-uniqueness][splitting fields]] -- both present -- become one subject; representation
theory; homological algebra beyond [[id:modules-quotients-and-exact-sequences][exact sequences]]; the structure
theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain,
which would subsume both the classification of finite abelian groups and
the Jordan normal form; tensor products; and category theory, which the
repetitions above are asking for.
