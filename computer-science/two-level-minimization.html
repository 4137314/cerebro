Two-level minimization: prime implicants and covers

A minimum sum of products is a cover of the on-set by prime implicants (cubes that cannot be enlarged without hitting the off-set); Quine–McCluskey generates all primes by merging cubes differing in one variable, essential primes are taken first, and the rest is a set-cover problem — NP-hard in general, solved heuristically by Espresso.

Method

  1. Implicant: a cube (product of literals) with . Prime: no literal can be removed keeping . Every minimum SOP uses only primes.
  2. Generate primes (Quine–McCluskey): list minterms (and don't-cares) by number of ones; merge pairs differing in exactly one bit (); repeat on the merged cubes; cubes never merged are prime.
  3. Prime implicant chart: rows = primes, columns = minterms (not don't-cares). A minterm covered by a single prime makes that prime essential.
  4. Cover the remaining minterms with the fewest (cheapest) primes: row/column dominance, then branching (Petrick's method) — exponential in the worst case.

A Karnaugh map does the same by eye for up to 4–5 variables: largest power-of-two rectangles of ones, wrapping at the edges.

Intuition

Make every product as large as possible (prime), then pick the fewest large products that together touch every one.

Pitfall

Greedy "take the biggest prime first" is not optimal on cyclic charts; and two-level minimality says nothing about multilevel area or delay — it is only the first step of synthesis.
