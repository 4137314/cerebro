Retiming

Moving registers across combinational gates — from all inputs of a gate to its output or back — does not change the input–output behaviour seen at the cycle level but changes the length of the register-to-register paths, so the clock period can be reduced (or the number of registers minimised).

Method

Model the circuit as a graph: nodes are combinational blocks with delay , edges carry a weight = number of registers on them. A retiming assigns an integer lag to each node: registers are moved from its outputs to its inputs. The new weights are

legal if all . Around any cycle the total weight is unchanged (the lags cancel), so loops keep their latency, and the cycle-level behaviour is preserved. The clock period is the longest path with zero registers; Leiserson and Saxe find the minimum period by testing candidate periods, each check being a system of difference constraints solved as shortest paths.

Picture

Two registers at the end moved backwards into the chain.

Intuition

Registers are checkpoints; retiming moves them along the road so that no stretch between two checkpoints is too long.

Pitfall

Moving registers changes their initial (reset) states: an equivalent reset state may not exist after a backward move, and the registers no longer correspond to the names in the RTL, which complicates debugging and verification.
