Register transfers: multiplexers, buses, tri-state

Data move between registers of bits through a dedicated mux per register (any set of transfers in one cycle, -input muxes, wiring explodes), a shared bus driven by one mux (one value per cycle, every register may load it) or a tri-state bus (registers drive the bus through enabled buffers: same transfers as the mux bus, minimal wiring, best scalability, but at most one enable active at a time).

Construction

Dedicated multiplexers: each register has a mux choosing among the others' outputs (select ) and a load . Any permutation in one cycle, even a swap — it works because all registers sample at the same edge. Cost: wires back to every mux, each mux with inputs; adding a register changes every mux. Mux bus: one mux selects one register onto a bus wired to all register inputs; loads choose who stores it. One source per cycle: needs two cycles, in the right order (first save into , then overwrite ). One mux, wiring concentrated in one place, easier to extend. Tri-state bus: a tri-state buffer passes its input when enabled and otherwise disconnects (high impedance, Z in std_logic). Each register drives the bus through its own buffer (enable ) and loads from it (): one line crosses all registers, no central component, lower loads, good speed, adding a register is attaching it. The control must guarantee that at most one is 1 (a decoder on a source address does it); two drivers fighting short-circuit the bus. An ALU or shared adder sits on these structures: sharing saves hardware and serializes operations.

Intuition

Point-to-point roads let everyone travel at once but cover the map with asphalt; a bus is one road with a traffic light.

Pitfall

On FPGAs internal tri-states usually do not exist: synthesis turns them into multiplexers. And during the cycle in which the enables switch, two drivers can overlap briefly — real designs leave a gap or use a mux.
